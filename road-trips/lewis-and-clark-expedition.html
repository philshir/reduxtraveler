Lewis and Clark Expedition Travel Guide

The Lewis and Clark Expedition, also known as the Corps of Discovery Expedition, was a historic journey undertaken by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark from 1804 to 1806. Commissioned by President Thomas Jefferson, the expedition aimed to explore the newly acquired Louisiana Territory, establish trade with Native American tribes, and find a practical route to the Pacific Ocean. The journey covered over 8,000 miles, traversing diverse landscapes, including rivers, mountains, and plains. The expedition provided valuable scientific and geographic knowledge about the western territories of the United States and played a crucial role in westward expansion.

Overview

Lewis and Clark Expedition Timeline (1804-1806)

The Lewis and Clark Expedition, officially known as the Corps of Discovery, was commissioned by President Thomas Jefferson following the Louisiana Purchase. Between 1804 and 1806, Meriwether Lewis, William Clark, and their team traveled from the Mississippi River to the Pacific Ocean, mapping territory, documenting plants and wildlife, and establishing diplomatic relations with Native American nations. The following timeline highlights significant locations along the expedition route using modern historical sites and landmarks.

1804: Departure and Journey Up the Missouri River

Missouri

  • Gateway Arch National Park (St. Louis) - Represents the starting region of the expedition and America's expansion into the West following the Louisiana Purchase.
  • St. Charles Historic District - In May 1804, the expedition departed from St. Charles, the last major American settlement before entering the frontier.
  • Fort Osage National Historic Landmark (Sibley) - Located along the Missouri River route traveled by the Corps of Discovery as it journeyed west.

Kansas

  • Kaw Point Park (Kansas City) - From June 26-28, 1804, the expedition camped at the confluence of the Kansas and Missouri Rivers while making repairs and conducting observations.

1804: Through Nebraska and Iowa

Nebraska

  • Lewis And Clark Landing At Riverfront (Omaha) - Commemorates the expedition's passage through the Omaha area.
  • Lewis And Clark National Historic Trail Headquarters and Visitor Center (Omaha) - Modern interpretive center detailing the expedition's achievements and route.
  • Lewis and Clark Interpretive Trails and Visitor Center Missouri River Basin (Nebraska City) - Marks the expedition's travel through southeastern Nebraska along the Missouri River.
  • Fort Atkinson State Historical Park - Near the site of important meetings between the expedition and Native peoples in 1804.

Iowa

  • Floyd's Bluff (Sioux City) - On August 20, 1804, Sergeant Charles Floyd died here, becoming the expedition's only fatality.

1804: Through South Dakota

South Dakota

  • Spirit Mound State Historic Prairie - Lewis climbed this mound after hearing Native stories about mysterious inhabitants, discovering expansive prairie views instead.
  • Lewis and Clark Recreation Area - Located along a stretch of the Missouri River where the expedition traveled and camped during the fall of 1804.

Winter 1804-1805: Among the Mandan and Hidatsa

North Dakota

  • Knife River Indian Villages National Historic Site - Site where the Corps established relations with the Mandan and Hidatsa peoples and met Sacagawea and Toussaint Charbonneau.
  • Fort Mandan State Historic Site - Winter headquarters of the expedition from November 1804 through April 1805.

1805: Toward the Rocky Mountains

North Dakota

  • Missouri Yellowstone Confluence Interpretive Center (Williston / Fort Buford) - Marks the important junction of the Yellowstone and Missouri Rivers encountered by the expedition.

Montana

  • Upper Missouri River Breaks National Monument (Fort Benton / Lewistown) - The expedition navigated dramatic river canyons and rugged badlands.
  • Lewis And Clark Memorial (Fort Benton) - Commemorates the Corps' passage through Montana's upper Missouri region.
  • Tower Rock State Park - A prominent landmark noted by William Clark in July 1805.
  • Gates Of The Mountains Wilderness (Helena) - Named by Lewis after passing through spectacular limestone canyons.
  • Great Falls - Site of a grueling portage around a series of major waterfalls.
  • Lewis And Clark Interpretive Center (Great Falls) - Interprets one of the most difficult segments of the expedition.
  • Missouri Headwaters State Park - Reached in July 1805 as the Corps approached the source of the Missouri River.
  • Beaverhead Rock State Park - Recognized by Sacagawea as a landmark from her childhood homeland.
  • Lewis And Clark Caverns State Park (Whitehall) - Located near the route used while approaching the Continental Divide.

1805: Crossing the Continental Divide and Idaho

Idaho

  • Lemhi Pass - Lewis crossed the Continental Divide and viewed waters flowing toward the Pacific Ocean.
  • Traveler's Rest State Park (Montana) - Major campsite near present-day Missoula before crossing into Idaho.
  • Lolo Pass - Crossing point through the Bitterroot Mountains and one of the expedition's greatest challenges.
  • Weippe Prairie - The starving Corps emerged from the mountains and received lifesaving assistance from the Nez Perce.
  • Nez Perce National Historical Park (Orofino / Spalding) - Commemorates the friendship and support provided by the Nez Perce people.

1805: Reaching the Pacific Northwest

Washington

  • Lewis and Clark Trail State Park - Located along the route followed while descending the Columbia River.
  • Beacon Rock State Park - One of the most prominent landmarks recorded in the expedition journals.
  • Cape Disappointment - In November 1805, Lewis and Clark reached the Pacific coast, completing their westward journey.

Oregon

  • Lewis and Clark National Historical Park - Includes Fort Clatsop, where the expedition spent the winter of 1805-1806.
  • Astoria - Nearby area explored by expedition members while encamped at Fort Clatsop.
  • Lewis and Clark Salt Works (Seaside) - Site where members produced salt by boiling seawater.
  • Ecola State Park (Cannon Beach) - Associated with Clark's expedition to view a beached whale and explore the coastline.

1806: The Return Journey

On March 23, 1806, the Corps of Discovery departed Fort Clatsop and began its return to St. Louis. Traveling back through many of the same locations, the expedition re-crossed the Bitterroot Mountains, traversed Montana, and descended the Missouri River. After covering nearly 8,000 miles over more than two years, Lewis and Clark successfully returned to St. Louis on September 23, 1806, completing one of the most significant exploration missions in American history.
