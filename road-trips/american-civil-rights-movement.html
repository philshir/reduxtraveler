Overview

American Civil Rights Movement sites offer travelers an opportunity to explore the stories, places, and moments that transformed the United States during the twentieth century. These historic locations preserve the legacy of activists, students, clergy members, community leaders, and ordinary citizens who challenged racial segregation and fought for equal rights under the law. From preserved meeting spaces and protest sites to museums filled with artifacts and firsthand accounts, these destinations help visitors understand the courage and determination that fueled a movement for social change.

Many of these sites are connected to landmark events that reshaped the nation's history, including peaceful demonstrations, voter registration campaigns, legal challenges, and pivotal speeches. Visitors can walk through spaces where important decisions were made, view exhibits documenting grassroots activism, and learn how local communities contributed to a broader national struggle for equality. Together, these places reveal the complex challenges faced by civil rights advocates and the lasting impact of their efforts on American society.

For modern travelers, Civil Rights Movement sites provide more than historical knowledge; they offer powerful opportunities for reflection and inspiration. Through preserved buildings, interactive exhibits, memorials, and educational programs, visitors gain a deeper appreciation for the individuals whose actions advanced justice and expanded civil rights. Whether exploring a museum gallery or standing on a significant historic landmark, travelers encounter stories that continue to resonate across generations and remain relevant in contemporary discussions about freedom, citizenship, and equality.

American Civil Rights Movement Timeline (1954-1968)

The American Civil Rights Movement was a nationwide struggle to end racial segregation, secure voting rights, and achieve equal treatment under the law for African Americans. From the landmark school desegregation battles of the 1950s through the major legislative victories of the 1960s, churches, schools, museums, bridges, memorials, and historic districts across the United States preserve the story of the movement. The following timeline highlights major civil rights events using preserved historic locations associated with the movement.

1954-1957: School Desegregation and the Rise of Mass Resistance

Arkansas

  • Little Rock Central High School National Historic Site (Little Rock) - In 1957, nine African American students known as the Little Rock Nine integrated Central High School, becoming a national symbol of the struggle to enforce school desegregation following Brown v. Board of Education.

1955-1956: The Montgomery Bus Boycott

Alabama

  • Rosa Parks Museum (Montgomery) - Commemorates Rosa Parks and her refusal to surrender her bus seat on December 1, 1955, an event that sparked the Montgomery Bus Boycott.
  • Rosa Parks Bus Stop (Montgomery) - Marks the location associated with Parks' historic arrest and the beginning of a new phase in the civil rights struggle.
  • Dexter Avenue King Memorial Baptist Church (Montgomery) - Served as the church where Martin Luther King Jr. was pastor during the Montgomery Bus Boycott.
  • Dexter Parsonage Museum - Home of Dr. King and his family during the boycott and an important center of movement leadership.
  • Civil Rights Memorial Center (Montgomery) - Honors those who sacrificed their lives in the fight for civil rights.
  • Court Square Fountain (Montgomery) - Located in the historic center of Montgomery, where many significant events connected to the city's civil rights history unfolded.
  • Alabama State Capitol (Montgomery) - The destination of the 1965 Selma-to-Montgomery voting rights march and the site of key civil rights demonstrations.

1960-1961: Sit-Ins and the Freedom Rides

Alabama

  • Freedom Riders National Monument (Anniston) - Preserves locations associated with the 1961 attacks on Freedom Riders challenging segregation in interstate travel.
  • Freedom Rides Museum (Montgomery) - Located in the former Greyhound Bus Station where Freedom Riders continued their campaign against segregated transportation facilities.

1963: Birmingham and the Children's Crusade

Alabama

  • 16th Street Baptist Church (Birmingham) - Headquarters for many civil rights activities during the Birmingham Campaign and the site of the tragic 1963 church bombing.
  • Kelly Ingram Park (Birmingham) - Scene of demonstrations where children and students faced police dogs and fire hoses during the Birmingham Campaign.
  • Birmingham Civil Rights Institute - Interprets the events of Birmingham's struggle against segregation and racial injustice.
  • A.G. Gaston Motel - Served as a meeting place and strategy center for movement leaders, including Martin Luther King Jr.
  • Boutwell Auditorium - Associated with major events and public gatherings during Birmingham's civil rights era.
  • Tuscaloosa - Site of the 1963 University of Alabama desegregation crisis and Governor George Wallace's "Stand in the Schoolhouse Door."

Georgia

  • Martin Luther King, Jr. National Historical Park (Atlanta) - Preserves key sites connected to King's life and leadership during the movement.
  • Birth Home of Martin Luther King Jr. - The childhood home of the movement's most prominent national leader.
  • Ebenezer Baptist Church - Spiritual home of the King family and a center for civil rights activism.
  • The King Center - Dedicated to preserving King's legacy and the principles of nonviolent social change.

1964: Expanding the Struggle Across the South

Mississippi

  • Medgar and Myrlie Evers Home National Monument (Jackson) - Home of civil rights leader Medgar Evers, who was assassinated in 1963 after years of activism.
  • Tougaloo College (Jackson) - Important organizing center for student activism, voter registration efforts, and civil rights demonstrations.
  • Mississippi Civil Rights Museum and the Museum of Mississippi History (Jackson) - Documents Mississippi's central role in the struggle for civil rights.
  • Smith Robertson Museum and Cultural Center - Preserves African American history and the local civil rights experience.

Georgia

  • National Center for Civil and Human Rights (Atlanta) - Explores the Civil Rights Movement and its continuing influence on human rights struggles.
  • Apex Museum - Focuses on African American history and achievements, including the fight for civil rights.
  • Historic Fire Station No. 6 - Preserves important local African American community history.
  • Municipal Market - A historically significant gathering place within Atlanta's African American community.
  • Atlanta History Center - Interprets Atlanta's role in the Civil Rights Movement.
  • Tubman Museum (Macon) - Dedicated to African American history, culture, and civil rights heritage.

1965: Selma and the Voting Rights Movement

Alabama

  • Brown Chapel AME Church (Selma) - Headquarters of the Selma voting rights campaign and starting point for major marches.
  • Edmund Pettus Bridge (Selma) - Site of "Bloody Sunday" on March 7, 1965, when peaceful demonstrators were attacked while marching for voting rights.
  • Selma - Center of the voting rights movement that helped lead to passage of the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
  • Selma to Montgomery National Historic Trail - Preserves the route followed by marchers advocating for voting rights.
  • Lowndes Interpretive Center - Interprets the events and significance of the Selma-to-Montgomery marches.

1968: Memphis and the Final Campaign

Tennessee

  • National Civil Rights Museum (Memphis) - Located at the former Lorraine Motel where Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated on April 4, 1968.
  • Mason Temple Church of God in Christ World Headquarters (Memphis) - Site of King's famous "I've Been to the Mountaintop" speech delivered the night before his assassination.

Remembering the Movement

Mississippi

  • Emmett Till and Mamie Till-Mobley National Monument - Commemorates the murder of Emmett Till and the courage of his mother, whose actions helped galvanize the modern Civil Rights Movement.

Washington, D.C.

  • Lincoln Memorial - Site of the 1963 March on Washington where Martin Luther King Jr. delivered the "I Have a Dream" speech.
  • Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool - Gathering place for one of the largest demonstrations in American history.
  • Martin Luther King Jr. Memorial - National memorial honoring King's leadership and legacy.
  • National Museum of African American History and Culture - Preserves and interprets the broader story of African American history, including the Civil Rights Movement.
  • Frederick Douglass National Historic Site - Honors one of America's most influential civil rights advocates and provides historical context for the movement that followed.

Movement Summary
