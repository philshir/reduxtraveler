How to Get There

From Three Rivers (8 mi): Take CA-198 east into Sequoia National Park, continue toward major trail access areas, and follow signs to designated Eagle Creek access points within the park interior.

Overview

The Eagle Creek corridor in the Mineral King area of Sequoia National Park offers a quieter, more rugged experience than the park's busy giant sequoia groves. The trail follows Eagle Creek up from the floor of the Mineral King valley through forest and granite, with views of the surrounding peaks, and serves as the route to destinations such as the Eagle Sinkholes, Eagle Lake, and Mosquito Lakes. Hikers come for the solitude, the creek, and the alpine scenery. No permit is required for a day hike, but overnight trips require a wilderness permit.

Getting to the Trailhead

Mineral King is a remote, high valley in the southern part of Sequoia National Park, reached by Mineral King Road, which leaves CA-198 just east of Three Rivers and climbs about 25 miles to the valley. The road is narrow, steep, winding, and partly unpaved, takes about 1.5 hours to drive, and is not suitable for trailers or RVs. It is open seasonally, typically from late May through October. Marmots in Mineral King are known to chew on radiator hoses and wiring in early summer, so many visitors wrap the underside of their vehicles with tarps. The hike begins at the Eagle/Mosquito Trailhead near the end of Mineral King Road. Parking is limited.

Trail Description

From the trailhead, the trail crosses the valley floor and climbs along Eagle Creek through forest and meadows, with views of the Mineral King peaks. The trail reaches a junction where hikers can continue toward the Eagle Sinkholes and Eagle Lake or branch toward the Mosquito Lakes. Hikers can turn around at any point; typical outings range from about 4 to 8 miles round trip or more. Follow the signs carefully at the junctions.

Trail Conditions and Difficulty

The hike is moderate to strenuous depending on the destination, with steady climbs and rocky sections. No scrambling is required. At high elevations, the thin air makes climbing harder than many visitors expect, and afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer, so start early. Snow can linger on the upper trail into early summer.
