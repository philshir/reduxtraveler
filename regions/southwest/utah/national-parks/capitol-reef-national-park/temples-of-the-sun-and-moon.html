General Information
Length:
2.8 km (1.7 mi) round‑trip to the main overlook
High Clearance 4WD Vehicle Required:
Yes (roads often impassable when wet)
Estimated time:
45–60 minutes to the overlook; several hours if exploring the scenic drive system
Difficulty:
Easy hiking; challenging vehicle access due to rough dirt roads
Seasons:
Spring and Fall (most favorable); accessible year‑round but roads may become impassable after storms
Pet Policy:
Pets allowed only in developed areas and roads when leashed; not allowed on trails or near formations
Road Starting Location:
Temple of the Sun & Moon Access via Cathedral Valley
Website:
nps.gov/places/temples-of-the-sun-and-moon

How to Get There

From Capitol Reef National Park Visitor Center: Drive east on UT‑24 for 20 miles to Caineville, turn north onto Cathedral Road, and follow the rough dirt route for approximately 16 miles to reach the signed junction leading one mile to both monoliths.

Overview

The Temple of the Sun and Temple of the Moon rise from the barren expanse of Cathedral Valley like colossal stone sanctuaries, their sheer Entrada Sandstone walls glowing warm hues of orange and rose in changing light. In this remote corner of desert backcountry, silence amplifies the immensity of the formations—freestanding monoliths shaped over millions of years as tides, sediment, uplift, and erosion sculpted the landscape into its present form. Visitors often describe the scene as otherworldly: the wide valley floor, the jagged skyline, and the solitary towers standing against an endless horizon evoke a sense of deep time and geologic grandeur reminiscent of ancient natural temples.

Reaching the formations requires navigating long stretches of dirt road, where conditions vary daily depending on weather and maintenance. High‑clearance vehicles are necessary, and travelers should check road conditions before attempting the route. The short walk from the parking area to overlook points is easy, allowing visitors to explore different vantage points or photograph the monoliths in early morning or late afternoon light. Many combine the visit with nearby features such as Glass Mountain or the broader Cathedral Valley Loop. This area is remote, with no water, services, or shade—preparation is essential for safe exploration of one of Capitol Reef’s most striking geologic landmarks.
