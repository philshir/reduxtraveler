How to Get There

From Grand Junction (72mi) Follow I-70BL E and 29 Rd to US-50 E in Orchard Mesa. Use any lane to turn left onto US-50 E. Continue on G50 Rd. Take G Rd to US-50 E/S Main St in Delta. Follow US-50 E to CO-347 N in Montrose County. Turn left onto CO-347 N.

Overview

Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park in western Colorado protects the deepest and most dramatic 12 miles of a 48-mile canyon carved by the Gunnison River. The canyon is among the steepest, narrowest, and darkest in North America: its walls of Precambrian gneiss and schist are so steep and the gorge so narrow that parts of the canyon floor receive only about 33 minutes of direct sunlight a day, which gives the canyon its name. The Painted Wall, a 2,250-foot cliff streaked with pink pegmatite, is the tallest cliff in Colorado.

The area was protected as a national monument in 1933 and became a national park in 1999. Much of the Gunnison River was dammed upstream, which preserved the canyon's scenery and created Curecanti National Recreation Area next door. Visitors come to look into the canyon from the rim overlooks, hike short trails along the edge, fish for trout in the river, and see the stars, as the park is an International Dark Sky Park. It is quieter than many parks and easy to enjoy in a day.

Park Sections

The park has three main areas. The South Rim, about 15 miles east of Montrose, is the most developed and most visited, with the visitor center, a campground, and the 7-mile South Rim Road that has 12 overlooks. The North Rim, reached from Crawford by about 11 miles of gravel road, is quieter, more primitive, and closed in winter. East Portal, on the Gunnison River at the canyon's eastern end, is reached by a steep, winding road that is closed in winter, and gives access to the river for fishing. The North and South Rims are not directly connected, and driving between them takes about 2 hours.

Sightseeing and Exploring the Park

The South Rim Road runs about 7 miles along the canyon's edge, with a series of overlooks that include Gunnison Point, Pulpit Rock, Cross Fissures, Painted Wall View (the best view of the cliff), Rock Point, Cedar Point, Dragon Point, and Sunset View. Most overlooks are a short walk from the parking area. The less-visited North Rim Road leads to Chasm View, Balanced Rock, Exclamation Point, and Cedar Point, with views of the narrowest part of the canyon. The steep East Portal Road drops to the river. In winter, the South Rim Road beyond the visitor center is closed to cars and opens for skiing and snowshoeing.

Hiking

Rim trails are short and moderate. The Rim Rock Trail is a flat, paved walk of about 2 miles round trip along the rim near the visitor center, and the Oak Flat Loop (about 2 miles) drops a little below the rim into the woods. The Warner Point Nature Trail (1.5 miles) leads to views of the canyon and surrounding mountains, and the Cedar Point Nature Trail is a short walk. On the North Rim, the North Vista Trail leads to Exclamation Point and beyond. Routes to the river, called "gullies" or "routes," such as the Gunnison Route, are steep, unmaintained, and strenuous, and require a free permit; carry plenty of water, and plan for a long climb out.

Other Activities

The Gunnison River in the canyon is a Gold Medal trout fishery, and fishing is popular at East Portal and in the inner canyon for those willing to hike. Expert rock climbers climb the big walls with permits, and kayaking in the canyon is for experts only. In the Dark Sky Park, ranger programs and stargazing events are offered at the South Rim. In winter, the closed South Rim Road is open for cross-country skiing and snowshoeing. Nearby Curecanti National Recreation Area and Blue Mesa Reservoir provide boating, fishing, and camping, and there are no commercial outfitters or cave tours inside the park.

Flora and Wildlife

The rims are covered in pinyon pine, Utah juniper, Gambel oak, serviceberry, sagebrush, and mountain mahogany, with aspen and Douglas fir on the North Rim, and wildflowers in spring and early summer. Wildlife includes mule deer, elk, black bears, coyotes, bobcats, mountain lions, and many birds, including peregrine falcons, golden eagles, white-throated swifts, and Steller's jays. Peregrine falcons nest on the cliffs and are sometimes seen from the overlooks. Viewing is on your own, and you are most likely to see deer on the rim at dawn and dusk.

Access

The South Rim entrance is on Colorado Highway 347, about 15 miles east of Montrose via US-50. Montrose, with a regional airport, is about 20 minutes away. Grand Junction is about 1.5 hours northwest, Gunnison about 1 hour east, and Denver about 5 hours. The North Rim is reached from Crawford on Highway 92 and then on a gravel road, about 11 miles. Visitors arrive by car, and there is no public transportation to the park. Services are limited inside the park, so stock up in Montrose.

Seasons

Summer is the main season, with warm days and thunderstorms in the afternoon. Spring and fall are milder; wildflowers appear in spring and the Gambel oak leaves turn red and gold in fall. The South Rim Road is usually open to vehicles from early April to mid-November, and in winter it is plowed only as far as Gunnison Point. The North Rim Road, the East Portal Road, and the North Rim campground close from about November to April due to snow. Winter brings very cold weather but few visitors, and the snow-dusted canyon is striking.

Camping and Lodging

The South Rim Campground has about 88 sites in three loops, with some open year-round, and takes reservations in summer. The North Rim Campground has a small number of primitive sites, and East Portal Campground, in Curecanti National Recreation Area, is at the river. Backcountry camping in the inner canyon requires a permit. There is no lodging in the park. Montrose, 15 miles from the South Rim, has the most hotels, including chain hotels, motels, and restaurants. Delta, Crawford, and Gunnison offer more options, and Ouray and Telluride, about an hour or two south, are popular mountain towns.

Visitor Centers

The South Rim Visitor Center is the main visitor center, with exhibits on the geology, wildlife, and history of the canyon, a short film, a bookstore, and a patio overlooking the canyon; it is open every day except for a few winter holidays. The North Rim Ranger Station is a small seasonal station with information and permits, and the East Portal contact station in Curecanti is open in summer. Hours vary by season, so check the National Park Service website before you go.

South Rim

Carved by the relentless force of the Gunnison River, the South Rim of Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park offers one of the most dramatic canyon landscapes in North America. From overlooks perched thousands of feet above the river, visitors gaze into a chasm so steep and narrow that sunlight barely reaches its depths, revealing sheer walls of dark Precambrian rock streaked with lichen and shadow. Along Rim Rock Drive, short trails and scenic pullouts invite travelers to pause, photograph soaring peregrine falcons, and absorb the vast silence that has shaped this remote corner of western Colorado for millennia.

The South Rim is the park’s most accessible area, blending rugged wilderness with thoughtful infrastructure that enhances exploration without diminishing its wild character. A visitor center provides insight into the canyon’s geology, ecology, and human history, while nearby trails such as Oak Flat Loop and Warner Point lead hikers through pinyon-juniper woodlands to unforgettable viewpoints. At sunrise and sunset, the canyon walls ignite in bands of gold and crimson, rewarding patient observers with a spectacle that feels both intimate and immense—an experience emblematic of the park’s enduring power and allure

South Rim Drive

Winding along the canyon’s brink, South Rim Drive delivers a cinematic journey through Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park, where a series of scenic overlooks reveal vertiginous drops, shadowed rock walls, and fleeting glimpses of the Gunnison River far below. Each pullout invites travelers to linger as light and weather transform the canyon’s dark stone, turning a simple drive into an expedition through one of the most dramatic landscapes in the American West.

Tomichi Point

From this lofty South Rim overlook, the canyon drops away in staggering depth, revealing layered walls of ancient rock that emphasize just how abruptly the Gunnison River carved its path.

Gunnison Point

One of the park’s most accessible viewpoints, Gunnison Point delivers a classic vista into the inner canyon, where darkness, depth, and silence define the Black Canyon’s character.

Rim Rock Trail

Tracing the canyon’s edge near the South Rim Visitor Center, the Rim Rock Trail offers a front-row seat to the raw immensity of Black Canyon of the Gunnison, where a mostly level, paved path slips through pinyon-juniper woodland before opening to sheer overlooks plunging nearly 2,000 feet to the river below. Interpretive signs along the way decode ancient metamorphic rock and the relentless power of water, inviting travelers to slow down and savor a moment where geology, light, and silence converge.

Oak Flat Loop Trail

Clinging to the inner edge of the South Rim, the Oak Flat Loop Trail invites adventurous hikers off the overlook circuit and into the canyon’s raw, intimate terrain, where narrow paths thread across steep slopes of ancient schist and gneiss. From this exposed route, shifting views open across the Black Canyon’s vertiginous walls as ravens ride rising thermals and the immensity of the landscape presses close, offering an encounter with wilderness that feels both perilous and profoundly alive.

Pulpit Rock Overlook

Perched dramatically above the void, Pulpit Rock frames a sweeping view of sheer, plunging walls that convey the canyon’s scale with both beauty and menace.

Cross Fissures View

Named for the intersecting cracks etched into the canyon walls, this overlook highlights the immense geological stresses that fractured ancient stone over millions of years.

Rock Point

Offering a broad, open perspective, Rock Point reveals the canyon as a vast, sculpted amphitheater where vertical cliffs dominate the horizon.

Devils Lookout

At Devils Lookout, twisted rock formations and dizzying depths combine to create one of the South Rim’s most dramatic and unsettling views.

Painted Wall

Dominating the South Rim with sheer authority, the Painted Wall rises more than 2,250 feet above the Gunnison River, making it the tallest cliff face in Colorado and one of the most arresting sights in Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park. Streaked with light-colored pegmatite that resembles brushstrokes on dark stone, this immense rock wall captures shifting light and shadow throughout the day, offering travelers a portrait of geologic forces frozen in vertical time.

Cedar Point

One of the park’s closest overlooks to the canyon edge, Cedar Point confronts visitors with a sudden, heart‑pounding drop into the Black Canyon’s shadowed depths.

Sunset Point

As daylight fades, Sunset Point transforms the canyon walls into a canvas of golds, reds, and deep purples, making it a favorite for photographers and evening visitors.

High Point Overlook

True to its name, High Point offers one of the most expansive vistas in the park, emphasizing the canyon’s immense vertical relief and broad sweep.

Warner Point Nature Trail

Following the narrow ridge to the southernmost tip of the South Rim, the Warner Point Nature Trail leads visitors on a short but evocative walk through sagebrush and twisted pinyon pines to sweeping views of Black Canyon’s vast, silent interior. From its final overlook, the land falls away toward the distant Gunnison River as storms, light, and ancient stone converge—offering an moment that captures both the fragility and grandeur of this high desert wilderness.

North Rim

Chasm View Nature Trail

Leading from the South Rim Visitor Center to one of the park’s most dramatic overlooks, the Chasm View Nature Trail offers a gentle walk through pinyon-juniper forest before the land suddenly drops away into the depths of Black Canyon of the Gunnison. From the viewpoint, visitors peer into a narrow, shadow-filled gorge where the Gunnison River threads far below, a reveal that captures the canyon’s sheer scale and haunting beauty in a single, unforgettable pause.

Narrows View

This overlook peers into the canyon’s tightest constriction, where the walls squeeze close together and sunlight struggles to reach the river far below.

Balanced Rock View

Here, erosion takes center stage as visitors admire a precariously balanced formation perched above the abyss, a quiet testament to time and gravity.

Big Island View

Looking down on a massive rock “island” surrounded by steep cliffs, Big Island View showcases the complex sculpting of the canyon’s interior.

Island Peaks View

A cluster of jagged spires rises from the canyon floor, creating a striking, almost alpine skyline isolated within the depths of the Black Canyon.

Deadhorse Trail

Dropping steeply from the South Rim into the heart of the canyon, the Deadhorse Trail offers a demanding but deeply rewarding descent through sheer cliffs of ancient rock toward stark viewpoints of the Gunnison River far below. This rugged route strips away crowds and comfort, immersing hikers in the canyon’s raw scale and silence—an authentic, encounter with the uncompromising wilderness that defines Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park.

North Vista Trail

Climbing away from the North Rim along a quieter edge of the park, the North Vista Trail leads hikers through open sagebrush country toward ever-expanding views of Black Canyon of the Gunnison, where the land suddenly fractures into immense depth. As the trail rises toward Exclamation Point and Green Mountain, the canyon unfolds in dramatic layers of shadow and stone, offering an experience that rewards patient travelers with solitude, sweeping perspective, and a powerful sense of the landscape’s scale and remoteness.

Exclamation Point

Marking the dramatic end of the North Vista Trail, Exclamation Point delivers a jaw‑dropping panorama where the canyon’s sheer depth feels both overwhelming and unforgettable.

Other Attractions

Gunnison Point Trail, South Rim

The river can also be accessed by steep, unmaintained trails called "routes" or "draws" on the north and south rim. These routes require about two hours to hike down and two to four hours to hike back up, depending on which route is taken. All inner canyon descents are strenuous and require Class 3 climbing and basic route finding skills. Steep talus, impassable ledges, and lack of cover are some of the challenges hikers are faced with. Poison ivy also grows abundantly in the draws and on the canyon floor. Long sleeves and hiking boots are strongly recommended. The flow rate of the Gunnison River should also be considered for those planning on camping in the canyon, as high river levels can wash out the camp sites. The National Park Service warns the following: "Routes are difficult to follow, and only individuals in excellent physical condition should attempt these hikes ... Hikers are expected to find their own way and to be prepared for self-rescue." A free back country permit is required for all inner canyon use except at the west end.

East Portal Drive

Descending in a series of tight switchbacks from the rim to the canyon floor, East Portal Drive reveals Black Canyon of the Gunnison from a rare riverside perspective, where the walls loom steeply overhead and the Gunnison River rushes through a narrow, shadowed corridor. This dramatic route transforms the park’s vast vertical wilderness into an immersive journey, offering a glimpse of the forces that carved one of the deepest and most uncompromising landscapes in the American West.
