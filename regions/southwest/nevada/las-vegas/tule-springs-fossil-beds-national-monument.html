Tule Springs Fossil Beds National Monument

★5.0
  1. Southwest

Expansive desert monument preserving Ice Age fossil beds with self-guided trails, paleontological sites, and sweeping views of the Mojave landscape.

How to Get There

From Las Vegas, take US-95 north to Durango Drive, head north past Floyd Lamb Park, and continue to the Moccasin Road parking area at the monument’s edge.

Overview

Tule Springs Fossil Beds National Monument protects more than 22,000 acres of desert badlands on the northern edge of the Las Vegas Valley, where layers of ancient wetland sediment hold some of the richest Ice Age fossil beds in the Southwest. The monument preserves the remains of Columbian mammoths, giant ground sloths, camels, American lions, bison and horses that lived here between roughly 250,000 and 10,000 years ago. It was designated a national monument in December 2014 after decades of effort by scientists and local advocates. Visitors come to hike or bike among the rolling washes and wind-carved ridges, learn about the Pleistocene world and enjoy a quiet natural escape within sight of the city.

Access

The monument is on the far north side of Las Vegas, about 20 to 30 minutes from downtown, and is reached by taking US-95 north to Durango Drive and heading north to the monument edge. The main access point is the trailhead near Durango Drive and Moccasin Road, and there is also access from the area near Floyd Lamb Park. Nearly all visitors arrive by car, although parts of the monument can be reached by bike, and there is no public transportation to the trailheads. The monument is undeveloped, so plan to bring water, sun protection and a map.

Seasons

The monument is open year-round during daylight hours, and the seasons shape the experience. Spring and fall are the most pleasant times to hike, with cool mornings, comfortable afternoons and occasional wildflowers after wet winters. Summers are extremely hot with little shade, so visits should be limited to early morning or avoided. Winters are mild but can be windy and chilly. Heavy rain can make the washes and dirt roads muddy and unsafe, so check the weather before you go, and note that the monument is closed at night and on national holidays.

Things to Do

Explore an Ice Age Landscape at the Edge of Las Vegas

Tule Springs Fossil Beds National Monument is a rare place where the Ice Age lies just below the surface, in a setting that is only minutes from the city. The badlands, called the Las Vegas Formation, preserve the layers of an ancient wetland that attracted huge animals.

Learn About Ice Age Animals

Fossils discovered here include mammoths, sloths, camels, horses, bison and lions. Interpretive signs and National Park Service programs explain how scientists read the layers and what the environment was like when giant mammals roamed the valley.

Hike or Bike the Desert Washes

The monument's trails and dirt roads cross open desert, dry washes and low ridges, offering plenty of room to explore on foot or by bicycle. The terrain is gentle, and the views include the Spring Mountains and the Las Vegas skyline.

Watch for Desert Wildlife and Wildflowers

The monument is home to desert tortoises, lizards, coyotes, birds and many desert plants. After winter rains, spring wildflowers add color to the landscape.

Visit Nearby Floyd Lamb Park at Tule Springs

Adjacent to the monument, this city park has shady lawns, ponds, picnic areas and a historic ranch site. It makes a good addition to a monument visit, especially for families.

Top Trails

Moccasin Road Trailhead Routes

Length: Varies; routes of roughly 1 to 5 miles
Elevation Gain: Minimal
Difficulty: Easy

Trails from the Moccasin Road trailhead wander across open desert and shallow washes in the heart of the fossil beds. The paths are flat and uncomplicated, with views across the monument and signs explaining the geology and Ice Age history.

Durango Drive Trailhead Routes

Length: Varies; routes of roughly 2 to 6 miles
Elevation Gain: Minimal to Moderate
Difficulty: Easy to Moderate

Routes beginning near Durango Drive follow wide desert tracks through the badlands, with chances to explore ridges and side washes. They are best in cool weather and give a feeling of space despite being close to town.

Visitor Centers

Tule Springs Fossil Beds National Monument does not have a staffed visitor center at its trailheads, and there are no restrooms or other facilities on site. Maps, current conditions, ranger-led program schedules and details about the future visitor facilities are available on the National Park Service website, and rangers also provide information through events and outreach in the Las Vegas area. Because services are limited, arrive prepared with water and supplies.

Camping and Lodging

There is no camping or lodging at the monument, and overnight stays are not allowed in the fossil beds. The closest hotels are in North Las Vegas and northwest Las Vegas, within about 15 to 25 minutes, including chain hotels and casino properties such as Aliante Casino and Hotel. Downtown Las Vegas and the Las Vegas Strip are about 30 minutes away and offer the full range of resorts, from the Golden Nugget and Plaza to the large Strip properties. For a cooler getaway, Mount Charleston, about 45 minutes away, has lodges and cabins, and campers can find campgrounds in the Spring Mountains and at Lake Mead. Check each property's website for current availability.
