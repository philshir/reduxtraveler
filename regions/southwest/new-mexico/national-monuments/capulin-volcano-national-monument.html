How to Get There

From Raton (26 mi): Take I-25 S to NM-72 E toward Capulin, turn left onto US-64 E, then follow NM-325 north to the entrance of Capulin Volcano National Monument.

Overview

Capulin Volcano National Monument protects an extinct cinder cone volcano that rises about 1,300 feet above the high grasslands of northeastern New Mexico, topping out at 8,182 feet. Capulin is one of the best-preserved volcanic cones of its kind in the country, formed in an eruption roughly 55,000 to 62,000 years ago within the Raton-Clayton Volcanic Field, which holds at least 100 volcanoes. President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed it a national monument in 1916 to protect the symmetrical cone and its geology, and astronauts even used the area for geology training before the Apollo 16 mission. Capulin is popular because visitors can drive a paved road up the side of a volcano, walk the crater rim and see far across the plains and into neighboring states from the top on clear days.

Access

The monument is in Union County in the far northeast corner of the state, about 30 miles east of Raton and about 4 hours from Albuquerque. It sits on NM-325 just north of the small community of Capulin, off US-64/87, and almost all visitors arrive by car on paved roads. A two-mile paved road winds from the visitor center to a parking lot at the rim, and the climb can be steep; vehicles pulling trailers or longer than about 35 feet may not be allowed on the road, so check ahead. Services in the area are limited, with food and fuel mostly in Raton, Clayton and Des Moines.

Seasons

The monument is open year-round and is busiest from late May through early September. Late June to early August brings the "hilltopping" phenomenon, when large numbers of butterflies and other insects gather at the summit. Spring and fall bring mild weather, good wildlife viewing and fewer crowds, while winter can bring snow, ice and strong winds that may close the summit road. Afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer, so plan to be off the rim if lightning approaches, and the rim can be much colder and windier than the plains below at any time of year.

Things to Do

Climb an Extinct Volcano by Car and on Foot

Capulin Volcano National Monument offers one of the easiest ways to stand on the rim of a volcanic crater. After driving the road to the summit, visitors can walk the rim trail and look down into the vegetated crater and out across the surrounding plains.

Drive the Volcano Road

The two-mile paved road spirals up the cone to a parking area just below the rim. Along the way there are pullouts with views, and the drive itself is a major highlight for many families.

Walk the Crater Rim and Look Into the Vent

The Crater Rim Trail circles the top of the volcano with 360-degree views of grasslands, other volcanic cones and the distant Sangre de Cristo Mountains. A steeper side trail descends into the crater toward the old volcanic vent.

Explore the Lava Flows and Base of the Volcano

Trails around the base pass old lava flows and collapsed lava tubes, with signs describing how the volcano formed and how plants and animals returned to the area. The lower trails are quieter than the rim and are good for wildflowers and wildlife.

Watch for Wildlife and Butterflies

The monument sits where the Great Plains meet the Rocky Mountain foothills and supports mule deer, pronghorn, elk and many species of birds. In summer, hilltopping butterflies gather on the summit.

Top Trails

Crater Rim Trail

Length: Approximately 1 mile loop
Elevation Gain: Moderate
Difficulty: Moderate

This paved loop circles the top of the cone and provides 360-degree views of the volcanic field, the plains and distant mountains. Strong winds and steep drop-offs mean it is not ideal for young children without supervision.

Crater Vent Trail

Length: Approximately 0.2 miles one way
Elevation Gain: Moderate over a short distance
Difficulty: Moderate to Strenuous

This steep trail descends into the crater to the vent where lava once erupted. It offers an up-close look at the inside of the volcano and the vegetation growing within it.

Lava Flow Trail

Length: Approximately 1 mile loop
Elevation Gain: Minimal
Difficulty: Easy

This loop runs through hardened lava flows near the base of the volcano. Interpretive signs describe the volcanic features and how the surrounding landscape was shaped.

Visitor Centers

The Capulin Volcano Visitor Center is at the base of the volcano road and is the main stop before heading to the summit. Inside are exhibits on the volcano's formation, geology, wildlife and human history, a short film, a bookstore, restrooms and rangers who can provide trail and road information. A short nature trail begins nearby. The center keeps seasonal hours and closes on major winter holidays, so check the National Park Service website for current times.

Camping and Lodging

There is no camping or lodging within the monument, but there is a picnic area. The nearest hotels, motels and restaurants are in Raton, about 30 miles west, and in Clayton, about 30 miles east, with a few small motels in the village of Capulin. Camping is available at Sugarite Canyon State Park near Raton and at Clayton Lake State Park, which is also known for its dinosaur tracks. Trinidad, Colorado, about an hour northwest, offers additional lodging options. Check each property's website for current availability.

The visitor center features exhibits about the volcano and the area's geology, natural and cultural history, and offers educational programs about volcanoes. There is also a video presentation about the volcano. The name capulin comes from a type of choke cherry, Prunus virginiana, that is native to southern North America.

Apollo 16's John Young and Charlie Duke did some of their geologic training here in May 1971. William R. Muehlberger was one of the geology instructors.

Description

From the National Park Service:

Capulin Volcano National Monument is a well-preserved, relatively young (55,000 to 62,000 years old), symmetrical cinder cone. It rises steeply from the surrounding grassland plains to an elevation of 8,182 feet above sea level. The irregular rim of the crater is about a mile in circumference and the crater about 400 feet deep.

Capulin Volcano is one of the outstanding landmarks located in the northeast corner of New Mexico, where the rolling grasslands meet the foothills of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains. Capulin Volcano's highest point provides unobstructed, panoramic views of the volcanic field, distant snow-capped mountains, and portions of five states (New Mexico, Oklahoma, Kansas, Texas and Colorado).

Capulin Volcano also offers visitors excellent opportunities for observing and understanding volcanic formation. The large volcanic field surrounding the monument contains at least 100 recognizable volcanoes, and aids visitors in gaining insights into 10 million years of the geological history of northern New Mexico.

Visitor activities

The two mile road that winds from the bottom of the volcano all the way to the top is popular with visitors. Once the top is reached, views of the surrounding volcano field, Sangre De Cristo mountains, the flora and fauna are visible. From late June to early August a phenomenon called hilltopping can be seen on the Crater Rim trail, thousands upon thousands of ladybugs cover trees and bushes at the top of the volcano. Capulin is also International Dark-Sky Association Gold Tier Certified, which means it has one of the darkest night skies in the entire country. Due to low levels of light pollution incredible views of the Milky Way and constellations are evident.

Visitors can hike a variety of different trails located throughout the park. The Lava Flow trail is a mile long loop that runs along the southern portion of the park. The Boca Trail is a 1.7 mile long loop that runs along the western base of the volcano, hikers can view the collapsed lava tubes that were created thousands of years ago at the foundation of the volcano. There are two different trails accessible from the top of the volcano: the Crater Rim trail which is a one-mile loop that goes around the entirety of the rim of the volcano and the Crater Vent trail which goes 200 yards into the crater itself.

Animal life

Although Capulin is primarily known for its volcanic geology, the park boasts a rich diversity of plant and animal life. The grasslands of the Great Plains and the forests of the Rocky Mountains combine at Capulin to form a unique ecotone which provides habitat for 73 species of birds in addition to numerous other animals. Songbirds such as the spotted towhee, Bullock's oriole, mountain bluebird, and Steller's jay all call Capulin Volcano home. Larger birds such as the wild turkey, osprey, red-tailed hawk, as well as New Mexico's state bird, the roadrunner. There are five different species of hummingbird that migrate through Capulin during the summer months.

Some of the larger mammals that frequent the park include the American black bear, cougar, pronghorn, elk, and the most numerous of larger mammals, the mule deer. Smaller mammals such as the American badger, Mexican free-tailed bat, ring-tailed cat, desert cottontail, and North American porcupine also like to call Capulin Volcano National Monument home. The unique landscape and flora create a habitat that allows a large and diverse group of animals to co-exist in a relatively small area. The abundance of diversity is not contained to just warm blooded animals however, Capulin also has a large group of cold blooded reptiles as well. The prairie rattlesnake and bullsnake are seen throughout the park. western fence lizards and horned lizards can be seen collecting the sun's rays on the multiple hiking trails. The tiger salamander is spotted in pools of water that sometimes collect around the park.
