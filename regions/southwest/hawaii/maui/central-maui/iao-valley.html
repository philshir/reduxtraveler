Iao Valley

★5.0

Lush rainforest park known for its towering needle-shaped peak, botanical gardens, and historical significance as a sacred Hawaiian site.

How to Get There

From Kahului, HI (5 mi): Drive west on Kaʻahumanu Avenue, continue onto Main Street into Wailuku, then follow ʻĪao Valley Road to the park entrance at the end of the road.

Overview

ʻĪao Valley State Monument is a lush, steep-walled valley in the West Maui Mountains, best known for the ʻĪao Needle, a green volcanic spire that rises about 1,200 feet from the valley floor. The valley is a sacred place in Hawaiian history and was the site of the Battle of Kepaniwai in 1790, when Kamehameha's forces defeated the army of Maui in a fight that helped lead to the unification of the islands. Rain-fed streams, misty slopes and native and introduced plants create a dramatic rainforest setting. Visitors come to walk the short paths to the lookout, see the ʻĪao Needle, enjoy the scenery and learn about the valley's cultural and historical importance.

Access

The monument is at the end of ʻĪao Valley Road in Wailuku, about 3 miles west of the town center and about 5 miles from Kahului. Visitors drive Kaʻahumanu Avenue and Main Street into Wailuku, then follow ʻĪao Valley Road up the valley, a paved and narrow route. A parking lot at the end of the road serves the park. Non-resident visitors need to reserve a time slot in advance, and the lot can fill quickly, so book ahead.

Seasons

The monument is open year-round, but the valley is one of Maui's rainiest places, and clouds often gather by midday. The best views of the ʻĪao Needle are usually in the morning, before clouds roll in. Winter is the wettest time, with heavy rain that can swell the stream and cause flash floods, and in the past the valley has been closed for dangerous conditions. Summer is usually drier, though showers are possible any time. Trails are slippery when wet, so wear shoes with good grip.

Things to Do

See the ʻĪao Needle

The ʻĪao Needle is the valley's iconic landmark, a tall green pinnacle that is best seen from the lookout. Its shape and the surrounding cliffs make for striking photos.

Learn About the Battle of Kepaniwai

Interpretive signs describe the 1790 battle in which Kamehameha defeated the Maui army, when the ʻĪao Stream was said to run red. The story gives the valley a deep historical significance.

Walk the Ethnobotanical Loop

A short loop passes plants that were important to Hawaiians and to later settlers, such as kalo (taro), banana, sugarcane, ti and kukui. Signs describe how they were used.

Visit Kepaniwai Heritage Gardens

Just down the road, the Kepaniwai Park and Heritage Gardens celebrate the many cultures that came to Maui, with pavilions in Hawaiian, Japanese, Chinese, Filipino, Portuguese and other styles. It makes a good picnic stop.

Enjoy the Streams and Rainforest

The sound of the stream, the ferns and the mist-covered ridges give the valley a peaceful feel. Visitors should stay out of the water when the stream runs high.

Top Trails

ʻĪao Needle Lookout Trail

Length: Approximately 0.6 miles loop
Elevation Gain: Approximately 100 feet
Difficulty: Easy

This paved path climbs a set of steps to the lookout with views of the ʻĪao Needle and the valley. It is short but includes stairs, and it takes about 30 to 45 minutes.

ʻĪao Valley Ethnobotanical Loop

Length: Approximately 0.5 miles loop
Elevation Gain: Minimal
Difficulty: Easy

A gentle path beside the stream passes demonstration gardens and signs about traditional plants. It is good for families and a nice complement to the lookout trail.

Visitor Centers

ʻĪao Valley State Monument does not have a full visitor center, but the entrance area has an information booth or staff presence, restrooms, a small interpretive display and signage about the valley's history. Details about reservations, fees and current conditions are available from the Hawaiʻi State Parks website. The Maui Historical Society in Wailuku and local visitor resources can add further background.

Camping and Lodging

There is no camping or lodging at the monument. Wailuku, about 10 minutes away, has several small hotels and inns, and the town is also home to local restaurants and shops. Kahului, about 15 minutes away, has chain hotels such as the Courtyard by Marriott Maui Kahului Airport and the Maui Seaside Hotel. Kīhei and Wailea, about 30 minutes south, offer condos and large resorts like the Grand Wailea and Fairmont Kea Lani, and Kāʻanapali resorts are about 45 minutes west. Check each property's website for current availability.
