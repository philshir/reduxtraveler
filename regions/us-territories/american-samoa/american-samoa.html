Overview

Set deep in the South Pacific, American Samoa is the southernmost U.S. territory, a chain of volcanic islands of about 45,000 people where rainforest-covered peaks drop to coral-ringed seas. The seat of government is on Tutuila, with Pago Pago as the territory's capital and main harbor. It is an unincorporated territory, and its people are U.S. nationals.

Visitors come for the uncrowded beaches, snorkeling, and hiking, and to experience fa‘a Samoa, the Samoan way of life, centered on family, village councils, and church. Villages are organized around chiefs, or matai, and visitors are asked to respect customs such as Sunday rest and evening prayers. Samoan language and traditions remain strong, and the experience feels distinctly Polynesian.

Geography

American Samoa has five volcanic islands and two coral atolls: Tutuila and tiny Aunu‘u, the three Manu‘a islands of Ofu, Olosega, and Ta‘u, uninhabited Rose Atoll, and Swains Island. Tutuila has steep ridges and the deep harbor of Pago Pago, formed in a volcanic crater. Lata Mountain on Ta‘u, at about 3,160 feet (964 m), is the highest point. The climate is tropical year-round, and the islands lie about 2,600 miles southwest of Hawaii.

History

Polynesian voyagers settled the Samoan islands about 3,000 years ago, and Manu‘a is considered the cradle of Samoan culture. European contact began in the 1700s, and the London Missionary Society brought Christianity in the 1830s. The Treaty of Berlin in 1899 divided the Samoan islands between Germany and the United States, and the U.S. Navy took over Tutuila for a coaling station at Pago Pago in 1900. The U.S. Navy administered the islands until 1951, and the territory now has its own constitution and elected governor, while its residents are U.S. nationals, not citizens by birth.

National Protected Areas

The National Park of American Samoa is the only U.S. national park south of the equator, and it protects rainforest, coral reefs, and fruit bat habitat on Tutuila, Ofu, and Ta‘u. The Fagatele Bay area is part of the National Marine Sanctuary of American Samoa, the largest marine sanctuary in the U.S. system, and Aunu‘u Island National Natural Landmark protects a volcanic crater with a quicksand-like swamp. Rose Atoll Marine National Monument is a remote seabird and turtle sanctuary.

Territorial Parks and Historic Sites

Territorial sites and beaches add to the experience. Two Dollar Beach and Palagi Beach are popular for swimming, Blunts Point has World War II gun batteries, and Turtle and Shark in Vaitogi is a village site tied to a Samoan legend. The Jean P. Haydon Museum in Pago Pago shows Samoan history and art, Mount ‘Alava has a cable car and hiking trail, and Vatia is a remote village reached by a trail.

Seasons and When to Visit

American Samoa has a tropical climate year-round, with temperatures near 80 degrees. The drier season runs from May to October, which is the most comfortable time to visit, and the wetter season runs from November to April, with heavy rain and occasional cyclones. Flag Day in April is a cultural celebration, with longboat races and traditional dance, and Sunday is a day of rest in villages. Flights arrive from Honolulu, and travelers should plan carefully because services are limited.
