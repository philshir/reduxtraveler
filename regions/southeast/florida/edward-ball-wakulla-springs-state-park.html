How to Get There

From Tallahassee (16 mi): Take FL-61 S (Crawfordville Highway) south to Wakulla Springs Road, then follow signs east to Wakulla Park Drive and the park entrance.

Overview

Edward Ball Wakulla Springs State Park is a Florida state park that protects one of the largest and deepest freshwater springs in the world, about 14 miles south of Tallahassee in Wakulla County. Financier Edward Ball bought the land in 1934 and built the Mediterranean-style Wakulla Springs Lodge in 1937, and the state acquired the property from his trust in 1986. The roughly 6,000-acre park is a National Natural Landmark, designated in 1966, and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Its crystal-clear first-magnitude spring feeds the Wakulla River, and boat tours through cypress-lined waters are the classic way to see manatees (in winter), alligators, turtles and abundant birds. The spring bottom holds the fossilized bones of mastodons and other Ice Age animals, Paleo-Indians camped here about 12,000 years ago, and the setting stood in for the jungle in early Tarzan films and for Creature from the Black Lagoon.

Access

The park is about 14 to 16 miles south of Tallahassee, depending on the route. From Tallahassee, take FL-61 (Crawfordville Highway) south to Wakulla Springs Road, then follow signs east to Wakulla Park Drive and the entrance. Nearly all visitors arrive by car, and there is no public transportation. Parking and the swimming area can be busy on summer weekends, so arrive early. Pets are allowed in designated outdoor areas on a short leash but not on swimming beaches, in concession areas or in park buildings.

Seasons

Winter brings manatees to the spring and river on the coldest days, and it is also a quiet time for birding and for hiking the park's trails. Spring and summer are the busy season, with swimming and snorkeling at the spring peaking between April and August. Boat tours run year-round and are the best way to see wildlife in any season, although water clarity varies and glass-bottom tours have become rare. Summers are hot and humid with afternoon thunderstorms, and fall is mild and pleasant.

Things to Do

River Boat Tour

A 45- to 60-minute guided cruise on the Wakulla River, the best way to see wildlife.

Swim in the Spring

A designated swimming area with clear, cool water.

Stay or Dine at the Lodge

27 antique-furnished rooms, a marble-topped bar, an Art Deco elevator, and a painted ceiling.

Hike, Bike, or Ride Horses

Hike, bike, or ride horses on three trail systems through pine forest, cypress wetlands, and hardwood hammock.

Top Trails

Wakulla Springs Trail System

Length: More than 9 miles
Difficulty: Easy to Moderate

Highlights include pine flatwoods, hardwood forest, and wetlands.

Cherokee Sink Trail

Length: About 2 miles round trip
Difficulty: Easy

Highlights include a dramatic sinkhole tied to the region's karst system.

Nature Trail Loop

Length: About 1 mile
Difficulty: Easy

Highlights include interpretive signs on native plants and animals.

Visitor Centers

The park does not have a stand-alone visitor center. The historic Wakulla Springs Lodge is the focal point, with a dining room and concessions, and an interpretive exhibit explains the spring's natural and human history. Boat tours depart from near the spring, and park staff can provide trail maps and information at the entrance station.

Camping and Lodging

There is no campground in the park, but the Lodge at Wakulla Springs, built in 1937 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places, offers 27 rooms on site. Other hotels and restaurants are in Tallahassee, about 16 miles north, and in Crawfordville to the south. For camping, Ochlockonee River State Park, southwest of the park, and campgrounds in the Apalachicola National Forest west of Tallahassee are within about an hour.
