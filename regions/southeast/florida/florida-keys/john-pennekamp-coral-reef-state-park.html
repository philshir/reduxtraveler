How to Get There

Overview

John Pennekamp Coral Reef State Park in Key Largo, in the Florida Keys, was the first underwater park in the United States, protecting about 70 nautical square miles of coral reef, seagrass and mangrove habitat within the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. Conservationists, led by Miami Herald editor John D. Pennekamp and marine scientist Gilbert Voss, pushed for protection after souvenir hunters damaged the reefs, the adjacent federal reef area was proclaimed a preserve in 1960, and the park opened in 1963 and was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1972. The reefs, including Molasses Reef and Grecian Rocks, are part of the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States. People come to snorkel and dive, take glass-bottom boat tours, paddle mangrove trails, see the Christ of the Abyss statue, and visit the aquarium.

Access

The park is at 102601 Overseas Highway (U.S. 1) in Key Largo, reached by paved road only. Most reef visits happen by boat: park concessions run glass-bottom boat, snorkeling and dive trips, and kayaks, canoes and paddleboards can be rented for the mangrove trails and shoreline. Pets are not allowed, and reef tours should be booked ahead in busy winter periods.

Seasons

The park is open daily from 8am to sunset year-round. Winter and spring are the dry season, with the most visitors, and summer brings heat, humidity and frequent afternoon storms, with the wettest months from roughly June through October overlapping with hurricane season. Wind and weather can cancel boat tours and reduce visibility at any time of year, so check with the concession before traveling. Manatees, dolphins and sea turtles can be seen in the park's waters at various times of year.

Things to Do

Snorkel and Dive the Coral Reefs
The park is world-famous for snorkeling and scuba diving among vibrant reefs teeming with tropical fish, sea fans, sponges, rays, and sea turtles.

Visit the Christ of the Abyss Statue
A submerged bronze statue of Christ near Key Largo Dry Rocks — a third casting of a sister statue near Genoa, Italy, placed here in 1965 — has become one of the most photographed underwater landmarks in North America. It's reachable by snorkeling and diving tours.

Kayak Through Mangrove Tunnels
Paddle marked trails through mangrove forests and calm coastal waters, spotting fish, crabs, and wildlife along the way.

Wildlife Viewing and the Visitor Center
Manatees, dolphins, sea turtles, and shorebirds are common sights in the park's waters. The visitor center's 30,000-gallon saltwater aquarium and exhibits are a good rainy-day stop for learning about the reef ecosystem.

Top Trails

Wild Tamarind Trail
Length: ~0.5-mile loop | Difficulty: Easy
A short walk through tropical hardwood hammock, with interpretive signs on the park's terrestrial side.

Length: ~0.5-mile loop | Difficulty: Easy

Mangrove Boardwalk
Length: Variable | Difficulty: Easy

Paddling Trails
Length: Variable | Difficulty: Easy to Moderate
Marked water trails through mangrove creeks and seagrass beds for kayaks and paddleboards.

Visitor Centers

The park visitor center features a 30,000-gallon saltwater aquarium with six smaller tanks, natural history exhibits and a theater showing nature films, and it is a good rainy-day stop and a place to learn about the reef before heading out. Tour tickets, rentals and information are available from the park and its concessions; check with the park (305-676-3777) for current visitor center hours.

Camping and Lodging

The park has full-facility and youth/group campgrounds; check the Florida State Parks website for current availability and reservation details. Lodging is plentiful in Key Largo, including resorts and motels along U.S. 1, and in Islamorada to the south, with more choices in Homestead and Florida City, and Miami about an hour and a half north. Winter is the busiest season, so reserve ahead.

History

By the 1950s, souvenir hunters were chiseling and dynamiting coral from the reefs off the Keys. Miami Herald editor John D. Pennekamp and marine scientist Gilbert L. Voss led the push to protect them, and in 1960 the area was proclaimed the Key Largo Coral Reef Preserve. It was renamed for Pennekamp and opened to the public in 1963.
