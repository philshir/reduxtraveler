Pisgah National Forest

★5.0
  1. Southeast
  2. North Carolina
  3. Pisgah National Forest

Mark Turner, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Vast mountain forest encompassing scenic waterfalls, rugged peaks, wild rivers, and hundreds of miles of recreational trails across some of the most diverse landscapes in the Southern Appalachians.

How to Get There

From Asheville (35 mi): Take I-26 East to Exit 40, follow NC-280 toward Brevard, continue to US-64 West and then US-276 North into Pisgah Forest, where numerous trailheads, recreation areas, and the Pisgah Ranger Station provide access to the national forest.

Overview

Pisgah National Forest is a landscape of cascading waterfalls, ancient mountains and densely wooded valleys that captures the wild essence of the Southern Appalachians. It was established in 1916 as one of the first national forests in the eastern United States, assembled largely from lands bought for public ownership, and is the birthplace of professional forestry in America. The forest encompasses more than half a million acres of mountains, wilderness areas and recreation areas, and visitors come for iconic destinations such as Looking Glass Rock and Sliding Rock, mountain streams that wind through forests of oak, hickory, rhododendron and hemlock, and some of the most popular hiking, mountain biking and fishing in the region. Scenic drives along the Blue Ridge Parkway reveal sweeping vistas of layered ridges, while quieter backcountry areas offer solitude.

Access

The forest lies in western North Carolina, with its main recreation areas south and west of Asheville around Brevard and along US 276, and other sections extending north toward Burnsville and Linville Gorge. From Asheville, I-26 and US 64 or NC 280 lead to the Pisgah Ranger District, and the Blue Ridge Parkway provides access to high-elevation areas such as Graveyard Fields and Black Balsam Knob. A car is needed to explore the forest, and many trailheads have limited parking that fills on summer and fall weekends. Cell service is spotty, and some forest roads are gravel.

Seasons

Spring brings wildflowers and flowing waterfalls, summer offers cool forests, swimming holes and waterfalls, though weekend crowds can be heavy, and fall delivers spectacular foliage, especially on the high ridges. Winter provides long-range mountain views once the leaves are down, but snow and ice often close the Blue Ridge Parkway and some forest roads, and some campgrounds and recreation areas close for the season. Afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer, and conditions on the high balds can change quickly.

Visitor Centers

The Pisgah Ranger Station and visitor center near Brevard, along US 276 in the heart of the forest, provides maps, permits and information on trails and conditions. The Cradle of Forestry in America Historic Site, also along US 276, has exhibits and a forest discovery center, and generally operates seasonally. The Blue Ridge Parkway has seasonal visitor centers nearby, including the one near Asheville, which can help plan visits to the higher elevations.

Camping and Lodging

The forest has several developed campgrounds, including Davidson River Campground near Brevard and Lake Powhatan Campground near Asheville, along with many dispersed camping areas and backcountry sites. Check seasons and reservation requirements before arriving. Hotels, inns and cabins are plentiful in Brevard, Asheville, Hendersonville, Waynesville and Black Mountain, all within about an hour of most areas of the forest, with more rural options in Burnsville and Marion near the northern sections.

Featured Locations

Ken Thomas, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Black Balsam Knob Trail
★ 5.0

High-elevation mountain bald offering sweeping 360-degree views, open grassy summits, and one of the most iconic hiking experiences in the Blue Ridge Mountains.

Dallas Epperson, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Hawksbill Mountain
★ 5.0

Craggy mountain peak in Linville Gorge Wilderness offering a short, steep trail to sweeping 360-degree views of the Blue Ridge Mountains and dramatic cliffs.

Ken Thomas, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Linville Falls
★ 5.0

Waterfall located in the Blue Ridge Mountains of North Carolina, the falls move in several distinct steps, beginning in a twin set of upper falls, moving down a small gorge, and culminating in a high-volume 45-foot (14 m) drop

Omarcheeseboro, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Linville Gorge Wilderness
★ 5.0

One of only two wilderness gorges in the Southern United States

Ken Thomas, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Looking Glass Falls
★ 5.0

Roadside waterfall plunging more than sixty feet into a scenic mountain pool, offering one of the most accessible and photographed natural attractions in the Blue Ridge Mountains.

Ken Thomas, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Looking Glass Rock
★ 5.0

Granite-domed mountain summit reached by a scenic hike, renowned for panoramic views of Pisgah National Forest, Blue Ridge peaks, and the surrounding wilderness landscape.

Ken Thomas, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Moore Cove Falls
★ 5.0

Picturesque forest waterfall reached by a short hike, featuring a broad cascade that spills over a rock ledge and allows visitors to walk behind the curtain of falling water.

Ken Thomas, Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Sliding Rock
★ 5.0

Natural mountain water slide carved into smooth granite, allowing visitors to glide through rushing water into a refreshing pool within the scenic forests of the Blue Ridge Mountains.

Dallas Epperson, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons; Image Size Adjusted

Table Rock Mountain
★ 5.0

Scenic mountain peak in the Linville Gorge Wilderness offering a short but steep trail to panoramic views, popular for hiking, rock climbing, and photography.
