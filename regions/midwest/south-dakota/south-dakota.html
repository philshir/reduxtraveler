Overview

South Dakota is a Great Plains state of about 920,000 people, famous for Mount Rushmore, the Badlands, the Black Hills, and a strong Wild West and Native American heritage. The capital is Pierre, and the largest city is Sioux Falls. The state is named for the Dakota Sioux, and a large share of its residents are Native American.

Millions of visitors each year are drawn to Mount Rushmore National Memorial, the Badlands, Custer State Park and its bison herd, and the Crazy Horse Memorial. Other draws include the old mining town of Deadwood, the roadside attraction Wall Drug, the World's Only Corn Palace, and the annual Sturgis Motorcycle Rally in August.

Geography

The Missouri River divides the state into "East River" and "West River." East River is mostly rolling glacial prairie and farmland, with lakes and the Big Sioux River, while West River has ranchland, buttes, the Badlands, and the Black Hills. The Black Hills are a range of pine-covered mountains sacred to the Sioux, and Black Elk Peak, at 7,242 feet (2,207 m), is the highest point in the state and the highest point in the United States east of the Rocky Mountains. Western South Dakota has more in common with the West than the Midwest, and much of it observes Mountain Time.

History

The Sioux, along with the Arikara and other nations, lived here for centuries, and the Lakota became dominant on the plains by the early 19th century. Lewis and Clark passed through in 1804, and Dakota Territory was organized in 1861. The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty guaranteed the Black Hills to the Lakota, but Custer's 1874 expedition confirmed gold, and the rush to Deadwood in 1876 led to the seizure of the hills and years of conflict, including the tragedy at Wounded Knee in 1890. Railroads brought homesteaders, and South Dakota became the 40th state on November 2, 1889, the same day as North Dakota.

The Black Hills

The Black Hills rise like an island of dark pine forest from the plains of western South Dakota and northeastern Wyoming, about 125 miles long and 65 miles wide. The Lakota call them Paha Sapa, "the heart of everything that is," and consider them sacred, and the name "black" comes from the dark ponderosa pines that cover the slopes. The granite peaks, limestone canyons, caves, and meadows hold the state's best-known attractions: Mount Rushmore National Memorial, where the 60-foot faces of four presidents were carved between 1927 and 1941, and the nearby Crazy Horse Memorial, a mountain carving still in progress. Custer State Park has about 1,300 bison, the Wildlife Loop Road, Sylvan Lake, and the Needles Highway, and Black Elk Peak is the highest point east of the Rockies. Underground are Wind Cave and Jewel Cave, two of the longest caves in the world, and Spearfish Canyon has waterfalls and cliffs. The Gold Rush town of Deadwood is where Wild Bill Hickok was shot in 1876, and Rapid City is the largest city in the hills. The Mammoth Site in Hot Springs, the Mickelson Trail, and the Sturgis Motorcycle Rally every August round out the region.

National Protected Areas

South Dakota has an impressive collection of National Park Service sites. Badlands National Park protects eroded buttes, fossils, and mixed-grass prairie with bison and bighorn sheep, and Wind Cave National Park preserves a boxwork cave and prairie wildlife. Jewel Cave National Monument contains one of the longest caves in the world, Mount Rushmore National Memorial features the 60-foot portraits carved between 1927 and 1941, and Minuteman Missile National Historic Site preserves a Cold War missile silo and control center. The Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail and the Missouri National Recreational River also cross the state.

State Parks and Historic Sites

Custer State Park is the crown jewel of the state park system, with about 1,300 bison, scenic drives such as the Needles Highway, and Sylvan Lake. Spearfish Canyon in the northern Black Hills has waterfalls and cliffs, and Spirit Mound near Vermillion was visited by Lewis and Clark. Other significant sites include the Mammoth Site in Hot Springs and 1880 Town.

Seasons and When to Visit

Summer is the peak season for the Black Hills and Badlands, with warm days, long daylight, and the Sturgis Rally in early August, but crowds are large and lodging is expensive. Late spring and early fall are ideal for hiking, wildlife viewing, and fall color, and the Buffalo Roundup in Custer State Park draws crowds in late September. Winters are cold and windy, though the Black Hills offer skiing, snowmobiling, and quiet scenic drives, and some seasonal roads and attractions close.
