Overview

New York is a Mid-Atlantic and Northeastern state of about 19.6 million people, with the largest city in the country, New York City, and landscapes that range from the Adirondack and Catskill Mountains to Niagara Falls and the Atlantic beaches. Albany is the capital, and the state is named for the Duke of York.

Visitors come for New York City's skyline, museums, theaters, and food, and for Niagara Falls, the wineries of the Finger Lakes, and the beaches of The Hamptons and Long Island. Others come to hike and ski in the Adirondacks and Lake Placid, visit Hudson Valley mansions, and explore Revolutionary War sites such as Fort Ticonderoga.

Geography

New York has several distinct regions. Long Island and the New York Harbor islands lie on the Atlantic coast, the Hudson River flows north to south from the Adirondacks past the Catskills, and the Appalachian Plateau covers the Southern Tier. Mount Marcy, in the Adirondacks, is the highest point at 5,344 feet (1,629 m). The state borders Lake Erie and Lake Ontario, the St. Lawrence River, and Niagara Falls, and has thousands of lakes, including the Finger Lakes.

History

The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy and Algonquian peoples lived in the region when Henry Hudson explored in 1609 and the Dutch founded New Amsterdam on Manhattan in 1624. The English took over in 1664, and New York became the 11th state in 1788. The Erie Canal, opened in 1825, made New York City the nation's leading port. Ellis Island welcomed millions of immigrants from 1892 to 1954, and the city became the center of American finance, publishing, art, and theater in the 20th century.

New York City

New York City is the largest city in the United States, with more than 8 million people in five boroughs: Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx, and Staten Island. It is the nation's center of finance, media, fashion, and the arts, and one of the most visited cities in the world. The Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island stand in the harbor, while the skyline is anchored by the Empire State Building, One World Trade Center, and the Chrysler Building. Central Park is an 843-acre urban oasis, Times Square and the Broadway Theatre District are the heart of the entertainment world, and the museums include the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Museum of Modern Art, the American Museum of Natural History, and the Guggenheim. The 9/11 Memorial & Museum honors the victims of the 2001 attacks, and the Brooklyn Bridge, the High Line, and the Staten Island Ferry are classic experiences. The city is also known for its diverse food, from pizza and bagels to every cuisine in the world.

Long Island and the Hamptons

Long Island is a 118-mile island that stretches east from New York City into the Atlantic, with more than 7 million people in Brooklyn, Queens, Nassau County, and Suffolk County. The North Shore has Gold Coast mansions from the Gilded Age, such as Oheka Castle, which inspired The Great Gatsby, while the South Shore has long barrier beaches such as Jones Beach and Fire Island National Seashore, which is a car-free barrier island with the Fire Island Lighthouse. The eastern end splits into two forks: the North Fork has wineries and farm stands, and the South Fork is home to The Hamptons, a string of seaside villages that have long been the summer playground of New York's wealthy. Southampton, East Hampton, Sag Harbor, and Amagansett have wide beaches, celebrity estates, art galleries, and farm-to-table restaurants, and Montauk, at the very tip of the island, has the Montauk Point Lighthouse, built in 1796 and the oldest in the state, and a laid-back surf and fishing culture.

Finger Lakes

The Finger Lakes are a group of 11 long, narrow lakes in upstate New York, carved by glaciers, in a region of rolling hills, gorges, and farmland. It is the state's wine country, with more than 100 wineries along Seneca, Cayuga, Keuka, and Canandaigua Lakes, and is known especially for Riesling. The lakes are also known for their waterfalls: Watkins Glen State Park has a gorge trail with 19 waterfalls, Taughannock Falls drops 215 feet, higher than Niagara Falls, and Robert H. Treman and Buttermilk Falls are near Ithaca, a college town that is home to Cornell University and Ithaca College. Seneca Falls is the birthplace of the women's rights movement, with the 1848 convention that launched it commemorated at Women's Rights National Historical Park, and Auburn has the home of Harriet Tubman. Visitors can kayak, hike, and taste wines, cheeses, and craft beers, and fall color and the summer boating season are the busiest times.

Adirondack Mountains

The Adirondack Mountains in northeastern New York form the heart of Adirondack Park, a six-million-acre mix of public and private land that is the largest protected area in the contiguous United States, bigger than Yellowstone, Yosemite, Glacier, and Grand Canyon combined. The region has 46 high peaks above 4,000 feet, including Mount Marcy at 5,344 feet, the highest in the state, more than 3,000 lakes, and 30,000 miles of rivers and streams. Lake Placid hosted the Winter Olympics in 1932 and 1980, and the Lake Placid Olympic Museum and Whiteface Mountain keep the winter sports tradition alive. Lake George is a popular vacation spot with Fort William Henry, and Fort Ticonderoga was a key Revolutionary War fortress on Lake Champlain. Saranac Lake and The Wild Center offer nature and history. Visitors come to hike, paddle, ski, and see fall color, which peaks in late September and early October.

Niagara Falls

Niagara Falls, on the Niagara River between New York and Ontario, Canada, is one of the most famous natural wonders in North America. Three waterfalls make up the falls: Horseshoe Falls, the largest, which is mostly in Canada, American Falls, and the small Bridal Veil Falls. More than 3,000 tons of water pass over the crest every second, and the falls drop about 170 feet. Niagara Falls State Park, the oldest state park in the United States, opened in 1885 and was designed by Frederick Law Olmsted. Visitors can ride the Maid of the Mist boat to the base of the falls, walk the Cave of the Winds boardwalk beneath Bridal Veil Falls, and see the gorge from Prospect Point and Goat Island. Whirlpool State Park and Devil's Hole offer more of the gorge, and the Rainbow Bridge crosses into Canada. The falls are illuminated at night, and summer is the peak season.

National Protected Areas

New York's national park sites include the Statue of Liberty National Monument and Ellis Island, Gateway National Recreation Area, and Stonewall National Monument in New York City. Saratoga National Historical Park marks a turning point of the Revolution, Fort Stanwix is a reconstructed fort, and Vanderbilt Mansion shows Gilded Age life in the Hudson Valley. Fire Island National Seashore protects a barrier island, and Hyde Park has the Roosevelt sites.

State Parks and Historic Sites

New York's state parks include Letchworth State Park, the "Grand Canyon of the East," with three waterfalls on the Genesee River. Niagara Falls State Park is the oldest state park in the country, and Adirondack Park is the largest park in the contiguous U.S., with six million acres of public and private land. Watkins Glen and Taughannock Falls in the Finger Lakes, Bear Mountain on the Hudson, and Jones Beach on Long Island are popular, and Mount Defiance overlooks Fort Ticonderoga.

Seasons and When to Visit

Summer is the peak season for beaches, lakes, and outdoor festivals, though New York City is hot and humid. Fall is a favorite for foliage in the Adirondacks, Catskills, and Hudson Valley, with peak color typically from late September to mid-October. Winter brings skiing at Lake Placid and Whiteface, snow in the Adirondacks, and holiday lights in New York City. Spring has waterfalls at full flow and cherry blossoms in the city.
