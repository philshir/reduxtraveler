How to Get There

From Downtown Nashville (10 mi): Take I-65 South toward Brentwood, exit near Harding Place, continue toward Granny White Pike and Otter Creek Road, then follow signs to Radnor Lake State Natural Area at 1160 Otter Creek Road.

Overview

Radnor Lake State Park, officially Radnor Lake State Natural Area, is a Tennessee state park and natural area of about 1,368 acres in the Oak Hill neighborhood of Nashville. The lake was created by the Louisville and Nashville Railroad in 1914 and later became a wildlife sanctuary. After a public campaign in the early 1970s to save it from development, the land was protected, and it is managed as a Class II state natural area for day use only. The park is known for wildlife, including deer, otters, owls and waterfowl, and for its trails through hardwood forest. Visitors come for easy hiking, birdwatching and quiet time in nature just minutes from downtown Nashville.

Access

The park is on Otter Creek Road in Oak Hill, about 7 to 10 miles south of downtown Nashville, and is reached from Franklin Pike, Granny White Pike or Harding Pike. Most visitors drive, and the parking lots are small and fill quickly on weekends and in nice weather, so arrive early or visit on a weekday. Otter Creek Road through the park is closed to cars and open to walkers. The park is open for day use only, with gates opening in the early morning and closing shortly after sunset, and no camping or picnicking is permitted. Rules on pets and bicycles are strict, so check the park's website before you go.

Seasons

Spring brings wildflowers, nesting birds and fresh green leaves, and early morning visits are a favorite for birders. Summer is hot and humid, with the dense tree canopy offering shade on the trails and wildlife most active early and late in the day. Fall color is excellent around the lake, and the park is at its busiest on pleasant weekends. Winter is cool and quiet, and migrating waterfowl such as ducks gather on the lake, while leafless woods open up views across the water.

History

Radnor Lake was created by the Louisville and Nashville Railroad Company in 1914, and was initially used for watering steam locomotives and supplying the watering pens for shipped livestock. Afterwards, it became a local sportsman's club for L & N executives and guests. Efforts to preserve the Radnor Lake area began in 1923 when the executive vice president of L&N Railroad declared the site a "Wildlife Sanctuary" at the request of the Tennessee Ornithological Society. Executives with the railroad and their friends (The Sportsman's Club) used the sanctuary for fishing, but a reverence for the beauty of the area was present even then among L&N families and neighbors who lived in the surrounding hills. In 1962, the property was sold to be developed but initial work proved to be problematic and public pressure influenced the state to purchase the property in 1973 and create the State's first Natural Area.

Radnor Lake State Natural Area is a Class II State Natural Area, meaning it is a day use area only, and there is no camping or picnicking allowed.

Activities

Hiking

Radnor Lake has several miles of hiking trails, featuring varying difficulty levels.

Visitor's Center

In the entrance to Radner Lake, there is a visitor's center that contains artwork and other exhibits on display. It also has a map of the entire lake.

Birding

Many waterfowl and transients can be found in the park, including several duck species, such as ring-necked duck, canvasback, gadwall and American wigeon. In addition, the Barbara J. Mapp Aviary Education Center is home to injured-non releasable birds, including a great horned owl, red-tailed hawk, black vulture, golden eagle, and bald eagles.

Visitor Centers

The visitor center near the main entrance has exhibits and artwork, a map of the natural area and information from park staff, and is typically open on a limited weekly schedule. Nearby, the Barbara J. Mapp Aviary Education Center is home to non-releasable birds, including owls, hawks and eagles, and gives visitors a close look at native birds of prey. Check the Tennessee State Parks website for current hours and programs.

Camping and Lodging

Radnor Lake is a day-use natural area, and there is no camping or lodging inside the park. Nashville has hotels in every price range, with options in the Green Hills, Brentwood and downtown areas close to the park, and Franklin and Murfreesboro are about 25 to 40 minutes away. For camping, Montgomery Bell State Park, Cedars of Lebanon State Park and Henry Horton State Park are within about an hour and offer campgrounds, and the first two also have cabins.
