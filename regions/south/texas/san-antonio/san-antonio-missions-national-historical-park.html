Overview

San Antonio Missions National Historical Park preserves four of the five Spanish colonial missions built along the San Antonio River in the 18th century, and Congress established it in 1978. Founded by Franciscan friars to spread Christianity among local Native peoples, the missions together with The Alamo form a UNESCO World Heritage Site designated in 2015, the only one in Texas. From north to south along the river, the park includes Mission Concepción, the oldest unrestored stone church in America; Mission San José, popularly known as the "Queen of the Missions"; Mission San Juan Capistrano; and Mission Espada, home to a still-functioning 18th-century aqueduct and acequia. All four remain active Catholic parishes, and the Alamo, the fifth and best-known mission, sits upstream in downtown San Antonio and is managed separately by the State of Texas.

Access

The four missions lie south of downtown San Antonio along the river, spread over roughly 10 miles, and each has its own parking area. Most visitors drive between them, following the signed Mission Road route, and public transit options are limited, so a car or bicycle is the easiest way to see them all. The Mission Reach section of the San Antonio River Walk connects all four missions with 15 miles of hiking, biking, and paddling trails linking them to downtown. Admission to the park is free.

Seasons

Spring and fall are the best seasons, with comfortable temperatures for walking between missions and biking the river trails, and spring brings wildflowers along the river. Summers are very hot and humid, so plan visits for the morning and carry water. Winters are mild, with occasional cold snaps. The park grounds and visitor center are closed on Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Day, and New Year's Day, and since the missions are active parishes, some areas may be closed during services and special events.

Things to Do

Visit the Four Missions

Tour Mission Concepción, Mission San José, Mission San Juan Capistrano, and Mission Espada to see the stone churches, granaries, and walls that make up one of the best-preserved Spanish colonial mission systems in the country.

Stop at the Visitor Center at Mission San José

Mission San José is the largest of the missions and has the park's main visitor center and store, which makes it a good place to begin and learn about the mission story.

Bike or Walk the Mission Reach

The river trails link the missions with downtown and make a good way to see the area on foot or by bicycle, with views of the restored river habitat.

See the Espada Aqueduct and Acequia

Near Mission Espada, a historic stone aqueduct still carries water across a creek, part of one of the oldest surviving Spanish colonial irrigation systems in the United States.

Attend a Service or Ranger Program

Because the missions are active Catholic parishes, regular services are open to visitors, and rangers lead programs on the history, architecture, and culture of the missions.

Visitor Centers

The Visitor Center at Mission San José is the park's main visitor facility, with an information desk and the park store, and it is open daily from 9am to 5pm. Rangers there can help plan a visit to all four missions and explain what is happening at each on the day. Smaller information points and ranger contacts at the other missions vary, so check with the park for current staffing and exhibits.

Camping and Lodging

There is no camping or lodging within the park. Visitors stay in San Antonio, which offers hotels of every kind, from downtown and River Walk properties to South Side lodging closer to the missions. RV parks can be found around the city. For camping within about an hour, Guadalupe River State Park north of the city has campsites.
