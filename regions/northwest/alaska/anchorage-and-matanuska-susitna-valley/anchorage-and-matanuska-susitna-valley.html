Anchorage and Matanuska Susitna Valley Travel Guide

Region that offers a unique blend of urban amenities and stunning natural landscapes. Anchorage, the largest city in Alaska, serves as a gateway to the wilderness, while the Matanuska Susitna Valley is known for its breathtaking scenery, outdoor recreational opportunities, and rich cultural heritage. Visitors can explore the vibrant city life of Anchorage, with its museums, restaurants, and shops, before venturing into the valley to experience activities such as hiking, fishing, and wildlife viewing amidst the backdrop of majestic mountains and glaciers.

Overview

Anchorage and the Matanuska-Susitna Valley make up Southcentral Alaska’s population center, home to roughly 40 percent of the state’s residents. Anchorage, the state’s largest city and main gateway, combines urban museums and restaurants with wilderness at its doorstep, and the Mat-Su Valley to the north offers farms, glaciers, and mountain views.

Visitors come for the Anchorage Coastal Trail, wildlife viewing, glacier cruises and hikes, flightseeing, and the Alaska Railroad. The area is also the starting point for trips to Denali, the Kenai Peninsula, and Prince William Sound.

Geography

Anchorage sits at the head of Cook Inlet between Knik Arm and Turnagain Arm, with the Chugach Mountains rising directly east of the city. Turnagain Arm has one of the largest tidal ranges in the United States and a famous tidal bore. The Matanuska and Susitna river valleys stretch north from the city between the Chugach, Talkeetna, and Alaska ranges, and Denali can be seen on clear days. The climate is milder than the Interior, with cool summers and cold winters.

History

The Dena’ina Athabascans have lived around Cook Inlet for thousands of years. Captain James Cook explored the inlet in 1778, and Anchorage was founded in 1915 as a tent city for the construction of the Alaska Railroad. Military bases brought rapid growth in World War II, and in 1935 the federal government moved Midwestern farm families to the Matanuska Valley as part of a New Deal colony. The 1964 Good Friday earthquake, the strongest in North American history, heavily damaged Anchorage, and the discovery of oil in the 1960s and the pipeline in the 1970s fueled the city’s growth.

Cities and Towns

Anchorage has the Alaska Native Heritage Center, the Anchorage Museum, and an airport that is a major cargo hub. Girdwood, home to Alyeska Resort, lies along Turnagain Arm on the Seward Highway. In the Mat-Su Valley, Palmer and Wasilla are the main towns, and Talkeetna is a popular base for flightseeing and Denali climbers.

National Protected Areas

The Anchorage and Mat-Su area has no national park unit within it, and nearby Chugach National Forest is not covered here. National parks within reach include Denali National Park and Preserve to the north, Kenai Fjords National Park to the south, and Lake Clark National Park and Preserve across Cook Inlet, accessible by small plane. The Iditarod National Historic Trail passes through the region.

State Parks and Historic Sites

Chugach State Park is one of the largest state parks in the country, with nearly half a million acres of trails, Flattop Mountain, and wildlife just outside Anchorage. Independence Mine State Historical Park at Hatcher Pass preserves a 1930s gold-mining camp, and Matanuska Glacier State Recreation Area lets visitors walk on a glacier along the Glenn Highway. Denali State Park and Eklutna Lake offer more hiking and camping, and Eklutna’s historic village is known for its Russian Orthodox spirit houses.

Seasons and Best Time to Visit

Summer is the main season, with up to 19 hours of daylight, wildflowers, salmon runs, and the Alaska State Fair in Palmer in late August. Fall brings colors in September, the first northern lights, and fewer crowds. Winter is the season for skiing at Alyeska, the Fur Rendezvous festival, and the ceremonial start of the Iditarod in Anchorage in early March. Spring breakup brings mud, and heavy snow and short days make winter driving slower.
