Denali National Park: Tundra Wildlife, Sled Dogs, and the High One

1 Day

Six million acres of untamed Interior Alaska surround North America's tallest peak, a mountain that rises to 20,310 feet and about 18,000 feet from base to summit, one of the largest vertical rises of any mountain on land. Denali National Park and Preserve is a vast landscape of spruce taiga, open tundra, braided glacial rivers, and the snowy peaks of the Alaska Range, crossed by a single road and home to grizzly bears, moose, caribou, Dall sheep, and wolves. This one-day itinerary pairs a narrated bus tour deep along the Denali Park Road with the park's beloved sled dogs and a peaceful evening walk near the entrance. Stops beyond East Fork, including Polychrome Overlook, the Toklat River, Eielson, Wonder Lake, and Kantishna, are left out because buses traveled no farther than East Fork at mile 43 in 2026 after the Pretty Rocks landslide. Bus service to the end of the road is planned to resume in phases starting in 2027, so check current conditions before you go.

Day 1

The day begins at the park entrance area, where the visitor center, bus depot, sled dog kennels, and most marked trails are located. Only the first 15 miles of the park road are open to private vehicles, so a park bus is the best way to see the wilderness interior. Reserve your bus tour well in advance, since reservations open on December 1 of the prior year and summer departures fill quickly. Buses run from mid-May through mid-September, and no food is sold beyond the bus depot, so pack a lunch, layers, rain gear, and binoculars.

1

Start your morning at the Denali Visitor Center near the park entrance, open from mid-May through mid-September. Exhibits introduce the park's wildlife, glaciers, and Athabascan heritage, along with the story of naturalist Charles Sheldon, whose efforts led to the park's creation as Mount McKinley National Park in 1917. Rangers can share the latest wildlife sightings and mountain conditions, and the center is a stop for the free entrance-area shuttles. From here, it is a short trip to the Denali Bus Depot for your tour departure.

2

Board the narrated Tundra Wilderness Tour, a trip of about 5 to 5.5 hours that travels to the Murie Cabin near the East Fork River at mile 43. A driver-naturalist shares the park's natural history and culture as the road leaves the spruce forest behind and climbs into open tundra with sweeping views of the Alaska Range. Buses pause at the Teklanika River, a broad braided glacial river crossed at mile 30, and continue over Sable Pass, one of the best areas in the park for spotting grizzly bears, caribou, moose, and Dall sheep. On clear days, Denali itself appears on the horizon, though the mountain is often hidden by clouds. For a less expensive and more flexible option, green transit buses also travel to East Fork and let riders get off to hike across the tundra.

3

After returning to the entrance area, take the free Sled Dog Demonstration Shuttle from the visitor center, which departs 40 minutes before each demonstration, since the kennels have no visitor parking. Denali is the only national park in the United States with a working team of sled dogs, which have patrolled the park's wilderness in winter since the 1920s. Meet the Alaskan huskies, learn how rangers rely on them for winter travel and patrols, and watch a ranger-led demonstration as an excited team pulls a sled around the kennel track.

4

End the day with an easy walk on the Horseshoe Lake Trail near the park entrance, which can be reached on the free Riley Creek Loop Shuttle. The trail descends through quiet spruce forest to a lake shaped like a horseshoe, where beaver dams and lodges line the shore and the Nenana River rushes nearby. With long summer days in Interior Alaska, the soft evening light is perfect for spotting beavers, moose, and waterfowl before heading back to your lodging in Denali Park or Healy.
