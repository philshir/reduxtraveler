Fairbanks: Gold Rush History, Northern Lights, and the Heart of Interior Alaska

2 Days

On the Chena River in the Tanana Valley, about 120 miles south of the Arctic Circle, Fairbanks is the largest city in Interior Alaska and the gateway to the state's Arctic. Founded in 1901 when trader E. T. Barnette set up a trading post on the Chena River, the town boomed after the discovery of gold nearby in 1902, and today it is home to the University of Alaska Fairbanks and known for the midnight sun in summer and some of the world's best northern lights viewing from late August to April. This two-day itinerary spends the first day exploring the university and Pioneer Park, with a walk among reindeer in between, and spends the second day on the Chena River, along the gold country of the Steese Highway, and soaking at Chena Hot Springs. The World Ice Art Championships, held only in late winter, Denali National Park, about two hours south, and flights and tours to the Arctic Circle are left out, as they are better suited to a longer stay.

Day 1

The first day centers on the University of Alaska Fairbanks campus on the west side of town and Pioneer Park near downtown, a short drive apart. A car is the easiest way to get around Fairbanks. In summer, nearly 24 hours of daylight make for long days of sightseeing, while winter visitors should dress for temperatures that can drop below -40°F. Reserve your reindeer walk in advance.

1

Begin your morning at the University of Alaska Museum of the North, the state's premier museum of natural and cultural history, housed in a striking contemporary building on the University of Alaska Fairbanks campus that was designed to reflect the northern landscape. Its collections of millions of artifacts and specimens document Alaska's geology, wildlife, archaeology, fine art, and Indigenous cultures, with galleries ranging from Ice Age fossils and historic artifacts to contemporary Alaska Native art. Step outside for views over Fairbanks and the Tanana Valley toward the Alaska Range.

2

From the museum, it is a short drive across campus to the Georgeson Botanical Garden, a public garden and living research facility that showcases what can flourish in Alaska's challenging northern climate. In summer, the long hours of daylight produce vibrant flower beds, hardy perennials, herbs, and remarkably large vegetables, while demonstration gardens and interpretive displays explain the science of growing plants in the subarctic. It is a peaceful place for a stroll and photography.

3

In the afternoon, join a guided walk at the Running Reindeer Ranch, founded by local author and naturalist Jane Atkinson. Rather than viewing animals behind a fence, guests stroll through a boreal spruce forest alongside free-roaming reindeer, which often wander right beside you. Guides share stories about the personalities and behaviors of the reindeer, the history of domesticated reindeer in Alaska, and the ecology of the northern forest. Walks must be reserved in advance.

4

End the day at Pioneer Park, a historic park developed to commemorate the centennial of the United States purchase of Alaska. Stroll the boardwalks past historic cabins and buildings relocated from around the region, visit the Pioneer Museum, which tells the story of the miners, traders, homesteaders, riverboat operators, and Indigenous peoples of the Interior, and see the sternwheeler SS Nenana, a National Historic Landmark that once operated on Alaska's rivers. In summer, the park's shops, galleries, and cultural events make for a lively evening.

Day 2

The second day begins on the Chena River, then heads northeast of town along the Steese Highway into the gold country that gave birth to Fairbanks, and ends with an evening drive about 60 miles east to Chena Hot Springs. Riverboat cruises operate in summer, so reserve your tickets in advance. From late August to April, stay out late and look north on clear nights for the aurora borealis.

1

Start the morning with a narrated sternwheeler cruise on the Chena and Tanana rivers, a Fairbanks tradition that recalls the era when riverboats carried supplies and passengers throughout the Interior. Along the way, watch a bush plane take off and land beside the boat, see a sled dog kennel on the riverbank where mushers demonstrate how their teams train, and stop at a recreated Athabascan village, where Alaska Native guides share traditions of fishing, hunting, and making clothing to survive the northern winters.

2

Drive north on the Steese Highway to the Trans-Alaska Pipeline viewing point, where you can walk beside the pipeline that has carried oil about 800 miles from Prudhoe Bay to Valdez since 1977. Continue a short distance to Gold Daughters, founded by sisters Jordan and Ilaura Reeves, whose family has deep roots in the Fairbanks mining industry. After a panning lesson, try your luck with authentic Alaskan paydirt containing real gold, and explore the outdoor mining museum filled with historic equipment. Many visitors find not only gold flakes but also garnets and occasional Ice Age fossils.

3

Back in town, visit the Fountainhead Antique Auto Museum, widely regarded as one of the finest automobile museums in the United States. Its collection of rare, meticulously restored pre-World War II vehicles, from horseless carriages and early electric cars to racing machines and luxury touring cars, is paired with period fashions from the same eras. Exhibits also highlight the challenges of early motoring in Alaska, including the territory's first automobiles.

4

End the day with a scenic drive east along Chena Hot Springs Road to Chena Hot Springs, a resort in the forested hills about 60 miles from Fairbanks. Relax in the natural outdoor hot spring pool, surrounded by boulders and spruce forest, and enjoy dinner at the resort. In winter, the long, dark nights and clear skies make this one of the best places near Fairbanks to watch the northern lights dance overhead while soaking in the warm water.
