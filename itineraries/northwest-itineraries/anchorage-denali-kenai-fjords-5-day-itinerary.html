Anchorage, Denali & Kenai Fjords: The Best of South-Central Alaska

5 Days

This 5-day itinerary traces a classic Alaska loop north from Anchorage into the wild interior beneath North America's tallest peak, then south along one of the most scenic highways in the country to a fjord-carved coastline still being shaped by an ancient icefield. Along the way, the Matanuska-Susitna Valley's farmland gives way to the boreal forest of the Parks Highway, the vast wilderness of Denali National Park opens onto tundra and grizzly country, and the drive down Turnagain Arm delivers you to Kenai Fjords, where tidewater glaciers calve directly into the sea. Together these three destinations capture Alaska at its most iconic — urban gateway, mountain interior, and glacier coast.

Day 1 — Anchorage & Mat-Su Valley

Begin in Anchorage, Alaska's largest city and the natural hub for exploring the surrounding wilderness. The day moves from a deep immersion in Native Alaskan culture to the mountain wilderness at the city's back door, then out along a fjord-like inlet and into the farming valley that supplies much of the state's produce, famous for its improbably oversized vegetables grown under the near-endless summer sun.

1

This outdoor and indoor cultural center represents all eleven major Native cultural groups of Alaska through life-size traditional dwellings set around a small lake, alongside dance performances, storytelling, and demonstrations of traditional games and crafts led by Native Alaskan guides.

2

One of the largest state parks in the United States, Chugach State Park rises directly from Anchorage's eastern edge, its glaciated peaks, alpine lakes, and hiking trails offering true wilderness within a short drive of downtown — a rare arrangement for a major American city.

3

This narrow, glacially carved fjord south of Anchorage is famous for its dramatic bore tide, one of the largest tidal ranges in North America, along with regular beluga whale sightings and Dall sheep visible on the cliffs directly above the highway.

4

North of Anchorage, the fertile Matanuska-Susitna Valley produces the state's famous giant vegetables, grown under nearly twenty hours of summer daylight. Above the valley, the gravel Hatcher Pass road climbs into alpine tundra past the historic Independence Mine, offering sweeping views back down over the farmland and out toward the mountains that ring Anchorage.

Day 2 — North to Denali via Talkeetna

Head north on the Parks Highway, Alaska's main artery between Anchorage and the Interior, breaking the drive at a small mountaineering town that has served as the launch point for Denali summit expeditions for a century. On clear days, this stretch of highway offers some of the best roadside views of Denali itself, North America's tallest peak, without ever leaving the pavement.

1

This small, historic town at the confluence of three rivers has served as the base camp for Denali mountaineering expeditions since the 1950s, its quirky, artist-filled Main Street retaining an authentic frontier-town charm. The Talkeetna Roadhouse, a working bed-and-breakfast since the 1940s, remains a beloved stop for its enormous cinnamon rolls and hearty, family-style meals.

2

The Alaska Railroad connects Anchorage to Denali and Fairbanks along a scenic route paralleling much of the highway, and its Hurricane Turn train remains one of the last true flag-stop railroads in the United States, still picking up rural homesteaders anywhere along the tracks who flag it down — a living relic of how much of Interior Alaska was settled.

3

The Parks Highway follows the churning, glacier-fed Nenana River for much of the approach to Denali, a popular whitewater rafting run through a narrow canyon just outside the park entrance. Further north, the small river town of Nenana is famous for the Nenana Ice Classic, a century-old betting pool on the exact minute the river ice breaks up each spring.

Day 3 — Denali National Park & the Interior

Spend the day exploring Denali National Park and Preserve, six million acres of tundra, taiga forest, and glaciated peaks surrounding the 20,310-foot summit of Denali itself, the tallest mountain in North America. Private vehicles are largely restricted beyond the first fifteen miles, so the park's single ninety-two-mile road is traveled by shuttle and tour buses deep into grizzly, caribou, and Dall sheep country. For those with an extra day, the boreal forests and hot springs of Alaska's Interior extend the wilderness experience further north toward Fairbanks.

1

Denali, meaning "the tall one" in the Koyukon Athabaskan language, rises higher above its surrounding terrain than any other mountain on Earth, and the park that bears its name protects an intact subarctic ecosystem where grizzly bears, wolves, moose, and caribou roam largely undisturbed. The mountain generates much of its own weather and is visible only about a third of the days each summer, making a clear-day sighting a genuine highlight for visitors.

2

Located near the park entrance, this research and education facility, named for pioneering conservationists Adolph and Olaus Murie, serves as Denali's winter visitor center and offers exhibits on the park's wildlife research and citizen science programs.

3

The park road crosses these two wide, braided glacial rivers en route to its western reaches, their pale, silty water carrying rock flour ground by glaciers upstream. Both areas are prime wildlife viewing territory, with grizzly bears frequently spotted foraging along the gravel bars.

4

At the very end of the park road, ninety-two miles from the entrance, this former gold-mining settlement now hosts a handful of remote wilderness lodges, offering the deepest access into Denali's backcountry available without a multi-day trek and some of the most reliable mountain views in the park.

5

With extra time, the Interior stretches north to Fairbanks, Alaska's second-largest city, gateway to the Arctic and a premier destination for viewing the aurora borealis. Nearby, Chena Hot Springs offers natural geothermal pools amid the boreal forest, while the gravel Steese and Elliott Highways continue on toward the far northern reaches of the state, and the remote Denali Highway offers an alternative, largely unpaved route back toward the Parks Highway for those seeking true backcountry driving.

Day 4 — South to Kenai Fjords

Return to Anchorage and continue south along the Seward Highway, consistently ranked among the most scenic drives in the United States as it hugs the cliffs above Turnagain Arm before crossing the Kenai Peninsula to the coast. The day's destination is Kenai Fjords National Park, where an enormous icefield feeds glaciers that carve directly down to tidewater, calving into a bay rich with marine wildlife.

1

Designated an All-American Road, the Seward Highway winds 127 miles from Anchorage to Seward, passing beneath hanging glaciers, along the tidal flats of Turnagain Arm, and through the mountain pass at the Kenai Peninsula's spine, with pullouts offering some of the most photographed roadside scenery in Alaska.

2

Exit Glacier is the only part of Kenai Fjords National Park reachable by road, and an easy trail leads directly to its receding face, with markers along the way noting how far the ice has retreated year by year. A far more strenuous trail continues up to the Harding Icefield itself, a vast, 700-square-mile expanse of ice from which nearly forty glaciers flow outward in every direction, the largest icefield entirely within the United States.

3

Most of Kenai Fjords National Park is accessible only by boat, and full-day tours departing Seward's harbor into Resurrection Bay bring visitors face to face with calving tidewater glaciers, along with regular sightings of orcas, humpback whales, sea otters, puffins, and Steller sea lions along the fjord-carved coastline.

Day 5 — Seward & the Kenai Peninsula

Your final day explores the historic port town of Seward itself and the broader Kenai Peninsula beyond it, a region defined by its relationship to the sea — from a Cold War-era coastal defense fort hidden in the forest to the artist community and halibut fishing grounds of Homer at the peninsula's southern tip.

1

This small harbor town at the head of Resurrection Bay hosts one of America's oldest footraces every Fourth of July, a grueling, nearly vertical sprint up and down Mount Marathon, the peak that rises directly behind Main Street. Downtown, the Van Gilder Hotel, built in 1916, remains one of the oldest operating hotels in Alaska, its lobby lined with historic photographs of the town's founding.

2

Accessible by trail at low tide or by water taxi, this coastal recreation area preserves Fort McGilvray, a concrete Army fortification built in 1941 to guard Resurrection Bay against a feared Japanese invasion, its abandoned gun emplacements and tunnels still perched dramatically above the water.

3

West across the peninsula, the town of Kenai holds one of the oldest Russian Orthodox parishes in Alaska and sits at the mouth of the Kenai River, famous among anglers for producing some of the largest king salmon ever recorded, a reminder of how central fishing remains to life across the peninsula.

4

At the southern tip of the peninsula, the artsy fishing town of Homer stretches out along the Homer Spit into Kachemak Bay, a marine wilderness of tidewater glaciers, sea otters, and abundant halibut fishing framed by the snow-capped peaks of Kenai Fjords National Park across the water — a fittingly scenic bookend to a trip spent tracing Alaska's mountains, tundra, and glacier coast.
