Lake Clark National Park: Volcanoes, Turquoise Lakes, and Coastal Brown Bears

2 Days

Just 100 miles southwest of Anchorage across Cook Inlet, Lake Clark National Park and Preserve protects about four million acres where the Alaska Range and the Aleutian Range meet. It is a dramatic landscape of jagged peaks, glaciers, turquoise lakes, wild rivers, and two steaming active volcanoes, Mount Redoubt and Mount Iliamna. Along the coast, brown bears graze in sedge meadows and dig for clams on the beaches, while inland the park's namesake lake stretches more than 40 miles. Despite being so close to Anchorage, Lake Clark is one of the least visited national parks in the country. This two-day itinerary flies through spectacular Lake Clark Pass to Port Alsworth, the park's hub, then visits Dick Proenneke's famous cabin at Twin Lakes and the Cook Inlet coast for bear viewing. Multi-day backpacking routes and river trips on the park's Wild and Scenic Rivers are left out, as they require backcountry experience.

Day 1

The adventure begins with a small-plane flight from Anchorage to Port Alsworth on the shore of Lake Clark, a trip of about 45 minutes. There are no roads into the park, so every part of it is reached by plane or boat. Port Alsworth has private lodges that offer packages including meals, flights, and guided activities, and booking a lodge is the easiest way to plan your stay. June through September is the best time to visit, with bears most active from July through September.

1

Depart from Anchorage and fly west across Cook Inlet into the mountains through Lake Clark Pass, one of the most scenic flights in Alaska. The plane threads between glaciers, waterfalls, and turquoise lakes as the peaks of the Alaska and Aleutian ranges rise on either side. On clear days, look for the snow-covered summits of Mount Redoubt and Mount Iliamna, which often steam from their tops.

2

Land at Port Alsworth, the small community that serves as the park's hub, with its airstrip, field headquarters, and lodges along the lakeshore. Stop at the visitor center, open in summer, to learn about the park's volcanoes, the salmon runs that make Lake Clark a key nursery for the Bristol Bay sockeye fishery, and the Dena'ina Athabascan people whose historic village at Kijik is now a National Historic Landmark. Rangers can share trail conditions and wildlife sightings.

3

Spend the afternoon on the park's most popular hike, the Tanalian Falls Trail, about 4.5 miles round trip through spruce and birch forest to a roaring waterfall on the Tanalian River. Continue a short distance beyond the falls to the shore of Kontrashibuna Lake, a long, narrow lake surrounded by mountains, for one of the most peaceful views in the park. Carry bear spray and make noise on the trail, as bears are common in the area.

4

Return to your lodge for an evening on the shores of Lake Clark. The long summer daylight lingers over the water and the surrounding mountains, and the quiet of the wilderness is broken only by the call of loons. Many lodges offer fishing, canoeing, or simply a lakeside campfire to end the day.

Day 2

Today combines two of the park's most memorable experiences: a visit to Dick Proenneke's cabin at Twin Lakes and a flight to the Cook Inlet coast for bear viewing. Both are reached by floatplane or small plane, and itineraries depend on weather and the operator's schedule, so arrange your flights in advance through your lodge or an air service. Bring rain gear, layers, and waterproof boots for the coast.

1

Fly north to Upper Twin Lakes to visit the log cabin that Richard "Dick" Proenneke built by hand in 1968 using simple hand tools. He lived here alone for about 30 years, and his story, told in the film "Alone in the Wilderness," has inspired generations of visitors. In summer, rangers are often stationed nearby to share stories of his life and show visitors around the cabin. Take time to walk along the turquoise lakeshore and imagine life in this remote and beautiful setting.

2

In the afternoon, fly to the park's Cook Inlet coast, where places such as Chinitna Bay and Silver Salmon Creek are among the best places in Alaska to watch brown bears. Guided groups land on the beach or in coastal meadows and watch from a respectful distance as bears graze on sedges, dig for clams on the tidal flats, and fish for salmon in the creeks later in the summer. With the volcanoes rising in the background, it is a fitting finale before your return flight to Anchorage.
