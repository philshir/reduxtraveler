Three Islands, One Journey: Oahu, Maui & Hawaii Island

7 Days

Hawaii is not one island but an archipelago of distinct personalities, and this 7-day itinerary island-hops across the three most visited: Oahu, the urban and historic heart of the state; Maui, a landscape of volcanic summits and waterfall-lined coastline; and Hawaii Island, still actively being built by fire. Because time is limited, each stop favors the destinations most emblematic of that island — royal palaces and Pearl Harbor on Oahu, the Road to Hana and Haleakala on Maui, and an active volcano and historic Kona Coast on Hawaii Island — condensed from fuller single-island itineraries into an efficient, high-impact week. Interisland flights are short, generally under an hour, making this ambitious pace realistic with early starts and tight but manageable connections.

Day 1 — Oahu

Your journey begins in Honolulu, where the only royal palace on American soil stands blocks from a harbor that changed the course of a world war. The morning moves through the compact, historic streets of Downtown Honolulu and Chinatown before the afternoon turns solemn at Pearl Harbor, one of the most visited and moving sites in all of Hawaii.

1

The only official royal residence in the United States, Iolani Palace was built by King Kalakaua in 1882 and later served as the site of Queen Liliuokalani's imprisonment following the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom. Directly behind it, the open-air Hawaii State Capitol uses volcano-shaped chambers and royal palm-like columns to echo the islands' natural landscape.

2

Known as Hawaii's "Westminster Abbey," this coral-block church built in 1842 served as the site of royal coronations, weddings, and funerals for generations of Hawaiian monarchs, and its quiet, tree-shaded grounds remain a resting place for early missionary families.

3

One of the oldest Chinatowns in the United States offers open-air markets and a working-class energy distinct from Waikiki's resort corridors. Nearby, the Bishop Museum, founded in honor of Princess Bernice Pauahi Bishop, is the largest museum in Hawaii and the premier repository of Hawaiian and Pacific cultural artifacts, from feathered royal capes to voyaging canoe history.

4

The USS Arizona Memorial straddles the sunken hull of the battleship without touching it, marking the resting place of more than nine hundred sailors and Marines still entombed below from the December 7, 1941 attack. Moored nearby, the USS Missouri is where Japan formally surrendered in 1945, closing the circle on the war that began in this same harbor. Reserve timed tickets in advance, as they sell out daily.

Day 2 — Oahu

Your second day on Oahu climbs to a volcanic overlook above the city before settling into Waikiki, the famous crescent beach where Hawaiian royalty once surfed on wooden boards. A short detour to the windward side's most photogenic bay rounds out a day that captures both Oahu's natural drama and its beach-resort identity before you fly on to Maui.

1

This dramatic overlook delivers sweeping views where sheer cliffs drop toward the windward coast beneath near-constant trade winds. It was the site of a decisive 1795 battle in which King Kamehameha I unified Oahu under his rule, a pivotal chapter in the story of the Hawaiian Kingdom.

2

Formed inside a collapsed volcanic crater, Hanauma Bay is Oahu's most celebrated snorkeling destination, its shallow coral reef sheltering parrotfish, butterflyfish, and green sea turtles within a protected marine conservation area. Advance reservations are required.

3

This unmistakable volcanic tuff cone offers one of the best short hikes on Oahu, climbing through a former military bunker system to a summit lookout with sweeping views of Waikiki's high-rises against the turquoise Pacific.

4

Close out your time on Oahu at Kuhio Beach Park, the calm, breakwater-protected heart of Waikiki's shoreline, just steps from the Kapaemahu healing stones and the historic Moana Surfrider and Royal Hawaiian hotels — the classic, palm-lined Waikiki experience before catching your evening or next-morning flight to Maui.

Day 3 — Maui

Arrive on Maui and set aside the full day for the Road to Hana, one of the most celebrated drives in the world — 64 miles and roughly 620 curves threading past waterfalls, rainforest, and dramatic coastline on the island's wild windward side. Start early, take your time at each stop, and continue past Hana town to the pools and trails of Kipahulu.

1

The volcanic Keanae Peninsula remains home to a traditional taro-farming community and a small white church standing against the crashing surf. Further along, Kahanu Garden preserves Polynesian canoe plants alongside Piilanihale Heiau, the largest ancient Hawaiian temple platform in the state.

2

Home to one of Hawaii's most photographed black-sand beaches, this coastal park also hides freshwater lava tube caves, a natural sea arch, and a dramatic blowhole along its rugged shoreline.

3

The small, remote town of Hana feels frozen in an earlier, slower era of Hawaii, with red-sand and black-sand beaches and a genuine sense of isolation from the resorts of West and South Maui — a natural lunch stop before continuing toward Kipahulu.

4

In the Kipahulu section of Haleakala National Park, the Pipiwai Trail climbs through a giant bamboo forest to the 400-foot Waimoku Falls. Below, the terraced Pools of Oheo — long nicknamed the "Seven Sacred Pools" — tumble down toward the ocean at Ohe'o Gulch.

Day 4 — Maui

Rise before dawn for the drive up Haleakala, the massive shield volcano whose name means "House of the Sun," for a summit landscape and sunrise that Mark Twain once called the most sublime spectacle he had ever witnessed. In the afternoon, descend all the way to sea level and cross to West Maui, where a 19th-century whaling port has become one of the island's most popular resort towns.

1

Haleakala Volcano last erupted centuries ago and now anchors a national park spanning coastal rainforest to alpine desert. At the summit, Pu'u Ula Ula marks the highest point on Maui at 10,023 feet, with the Sliding Sands Trail descending into a stark, Mars-like crater landscape and the Silversword Loop showcasing the rare, spiky Haleakala silversword found nowhere else on Earth.

2

Once the capital of the Hawaiian Kingdom and later a rowdy whaling port, Lahaina's historic district preserves this layered past through sites including the Lahaina Banyan Court, planted in 1873; the Pioneer Inn, Maui's oldest hotel; and Waiola Church, resting place of Hawaiian royalty.

3

Hawaii's first master-planned resort destination, Kaanapali is anchored by a three-mile stretch of golden sand and Black Rock, a lava outcropping prized for snorkeling and, according to Hawaiian tradition, the point from which souls leaped into the afterlife — a relaxed final evening on Maui before continuing to Hawaii Island.

Day 5 — Hawaii Island

Fly to Hawaii Island, the Big Island, and head directly to Hawaii Volcanoes National Park, one of the most geologically active places on Earth. From a steaming summit caldera to a lava tube tunneling through solid rock, the park compresses the entire creation story of the Hawaiian Islands into a single, walkable landscape.

1

The park's centerpiece, Kilauea Caldera, is a vast volcanic depression at the summit of one of the world's most active volcanoes. Within it, Halemaumau Crater — traditionally home to the fire goddess Pele — has repeatedly filled with a glowing lava lake in recent eruptions.

2

The Kilauea Iki Trail descends into a smaller adjacent crater that was a churning lava lake in a 1959 eruption, now solidified and still steaming. Nearby, the Thurston Lava Tube offers an easy, lit walk through a tunnel carved by flowing lava roughly 500 years ago.

3

This 19-mile road descends nearly 3,700 feet from the summit to the coast, passing pit craters and vast fields of hardened lava. At road's end, the Holei Sea Arch marks a dramatic stop where a 2018 eruption once buried the highway ahead.

4

An easy boardwalk trail near the coast leads to Pu'u Loa, the largest concentration of Hawaiian petroglyphs in the state. End the day back at the caldera rim at Volcano House, welcoming visitors since 1846 and offering unmatched views of Halemaumau over dinner.

Day 6 — Hawaii Island

Drive to the sun-drenched Kona Coast, the historic center of the Hawaiian Kingdom's final years and the heart of the island's coffee country. This is a day of royal history and turquoise water in equal measure, moving between palaces, sacred bays, and a place of ancient refuge along the lava-rock shoreline.

1

Hulihee Palace, a two-story New England-style mansion built in 1838, once served as a vacation home for Hawaiian royalty and is now open as a museum. Directly across the street, Mokuaikaua Church, built in 1837, stands as the oldest Christian church in Hawaii.

2

This restored early-1900s coffee farm in the uplands above Kona offers a hands-on look at the region's famous coffee industry, with costumed interpreters demonstrating traditional roasting and drying methods on the same volcanic slopes still used by growers today.

3

This deep, protected bay is both a marine life conservation district renowned for exceptional snorkeling and the site of Captain James Cook's fatal encounter with Hawaiian islanders in 1779, marked by a white obelisk visible across the water.

4

Known historically as the "Place of Refuge," this sacred site once offered sanctuary to defeated warriors and those who broke the ancient kapu system. Restored temple platforms, carved wooden ki'i statues, and royal fishponds set against a black-lava shoreline make it one of the most evocative historical sites on the island.

Day 7 — Hawaii Island

Your final day heads north into Kohala and Waimea, the island's ranching and royal heartland, where a massive lava-rock temple marks the founding of the unified Hawaiian Kingdom. Close the trip on one of the island's most celebrated white-sand beaches before your departing flight.

1

Built by Kamehameha I in 1791 on the advice of a prophet who foretold it would help him conquer and unify the Hawaiian Islands, this massive lava-rock temple overlooking Kawaihae Bay remains one of the most historically significant structures in the state.

2

The cool upland town of Waimea sits at the heart of Parker Ranch, one of the oldest and largest cattle ranches in the United States, founded in 1847 and still shaping the island's paniolo (Hawaiian cowboy) culture.

3

One of the largest and most consistently ranked white-sand beaches in Hawaii, Hapuna's wide, gently sloping sand and clear water make it the ideal final stop for a swim before heading to the Kona airport — a fittingly relaxed close to a week spent island-hopping across the state's three most iconic destinations.
