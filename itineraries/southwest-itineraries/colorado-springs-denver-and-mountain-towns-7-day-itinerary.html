Peaks & Prairie: Colorado Springs, Denver & the High Country

7 Days

Colorado's front range rises so abruptly out of the Great Plains that you can watch the elevation change in real time — flat prairie one moment, a wall of 14,000‑foot peaks the next. This 7‑day itinerary starts at the base of that wall in Colorado Springs, home to "America's Mountain" itself, before shifting north to Denver's gold‑domed downtown and the college‑town energy of Boulder, then climbing west into the true high country: a National Park carved by ice, a paved road to 14,000 feet, and a string of gold‑rush towns turned ski resorts ending in the aspen groves around Aspen itself. Grouped by geography to minimize backtracking across a genuinely enormous stretch of terrain, this trip trades a few of the region's smaller museums and city parks for the sites that define Colorado at its most iconic.

Day 1 — Colorado Springs

Your trip begins in Colorado Springs, a city built quite literally in the shadow of Pikes Peak, whose jagged silhouette dominates the western skyline from nearly every downtown street. Today stays close to the city center and its dramatic red-rock backdrop, easing into the altitude with a mix of striking natural scenery, Olympic-caliber sports history, and frontier-era museums before tomorrow's climb to the summit itself.

1

Founded in 1871 as a genteel resort town for wealthy Eastern tourists chasing mountain air, Colorado Springs has grown into the state's second-largest city while retaining an outsized concentration of Olympic training facilities, military installations, and sweeping mountain views. Its compact downtown grid, laid out with Pikes Peak framed at the end of nearly every westbound street, makes it an easy, walkable base for the two days ahead.

2

Towering red sandstone fins, tilted nearly vertical by the same geologic forces that pushed up the Rockies, make Garden of the Gods one of the most photographed free attractions in Colorado. The visitor center provides essential orientation — exhibits on the park's 300-million-year geologic history and clear maps of the walking paths that wind directly beneath formations like Balanced Rock and the Kissing Camels — before you set out on foot among the towering red rock spires.

3

A relaxed riverside park just west of downtown, America the Beautiful Park takes its name from the patriotic anthem inspired by a view from Pikes Peak's summit, and its wide lawns and the towering Julie Penrose Fountain make it a pleasant, low-key stop between more intensive sightseeing. It also sits directly beside the U.S. Olympic and Paralympic Museum, making the two a natural pair.

4

Colorado Springs has served as the headquarters of the U.S. Olympic and Paralympic Committee since the 1970s, and this striking, angular museum — opened in 2020 — celebrates that legacy through interactive exhibits that let visitors race a virtual bobsled, test their reaction time against an Olympic sprinter, and explore the history of the Paralympic movement in genuinely thoughtful depth. It's one of the most modern and accessible museums in the city, purpose-built with adaptive design for visitors of all abilities.

5

Housed in the elegant, free-standing former El Paso County Courthouse, this free museum traces the region's story from Ute tribal history through the 1859 gold rush and the health-seeker boom that first put Colorado Springs on the map. The building itself — with its clock tower and grand central staircase — is as much a draw as the exhibits inside, and it makes for an easy, unhurried close to your first day.

Day 2 — Colorado Springs

Today is entirely devoted to Pikes Peak and the canyon country of neighboring Manitou Springs — the single most concentrated day of dramatic scenery in the entire trip. You'll gain more than 8,000 feet of elevation without breaking a sweat, then have the option to earn a much smaller climb the hard way on one of the steepest trails in the country. Bring layers: temperatures at the 14,115-foot summit can be forty degrees colder than at the base, even in summer.

1

Operated by the historic Broadmoor resort, this cog railway has carried passengers from Manitou Springs to the summit of Pikes Peak since 1891, climbing through pine forest, past crashing streams, and finally above treeline into stark alpine tundra over the course of about an hour. A full mechanical rebuild completed in 2021 modernized the fleet while preserving the original 8.9-mile route — reserve tickets well in advance, as trains sell out regularly in summer and fall.

2

At 14,115 feet, Pikes Peak is one of Colorado's most famous "fourteeners" and the peak that inspired Katharine Lee Bates to write "America the Beautiful" after reaching its summit in 1893 — a plaque at the top commemorates the moment. The summit visitor center, rebuilt in 2021 with sweeping glass walls, offers 360-degree views across the plains to the east and an endless sea of Rocky Mountain peaks to the west, along with the famous high-altitude doughnuts cooked at a specially calibrated recipe for 14,000 feet.

3

For those driving the Pikes Peak Highway instead of — or in addition to — riding the cog railway, this alpine reservoir roughly halfway up the mountain makes an essential rest stop, its still water throwing back a mirror reflection of the peak above on calm mornings. A small visitor center, restrooms, and short lakeside trails make it a natural place to stretch your legs before continuing the climb.

4

Originally built as a funicular railway to service a pipeline, the Manitou Incline is now one of the most notoriously punishing hikes in the country — roughly 2,700 wooden railroad-tie steps climbing nearly 2,000 vertical feet in under a mile. It's a favorite training ground for professional athletes and military units, but the descent via the adjoining Barr Trail is manageable for anyone with reasonably steady knees, and the view back down over Manitou Springs is worth the burn.

5

Tucked into the limestone walls of Williams Canyon, this cave system has drawn tourists since the 1880s, with guided tours winding past stalactites, flowstone, and cavern chambers first explored by two boys in 1880. Above ground, the same site now also offers zip lines and via ferrata routes strung across the canyon, making it as much an adrenaline stop as a geological one.

6

Tucked into the granite walls of South Cheyenne Cañon and historically nicknamed the "Grandest Mile of Scenery in Colorado," Seven Falls drops water down a series of seven distinct cascades in a single narrow gorge. A staircase of more than 200 steps climbs directly alongside the falls to an overlook at the top, and an additional trail continues on to Midnight Falls further up the canyon for those wanting more.

Day 3 — Denver

An hour north of Colorado Springs, Denver trades red-rock drama for a genuinely walkable, gold-domed downtown. Today moves through the Golden Triangle museum district, past the state capitol, and into the historic warehouse blocks of LoDo (Lower Downtown), where 19th-century brick buildings now hold some of the city's best shopping and dining.

1

The "Mile High City" earned its nickname from a simple fact of elevation — exactly 5,280 feet above sea level, marked by a step on the State Capitol's front stairs — and its compact, grid-planned downtown backs directly up against the Rocky Mountain foothills on clear days. Founded during Colorado's 1858 gold rush, Denver has grown into a genuinely cosmopolitan city while keeping mountain access close enough that locals routinely ski before lunch and hike after work.

2

Capped with a dome plated in genuine Colorado gold leaf, the State Capitol has anchored downtown Denver since 1894. Free guided tours climb up into the dome itself for a close-up look at the gilding and a panoramic view over Civic Center Park, and the exact 5,280-foot mile-high marker on the west steps remains one of the city's most reliably photographed spots.

3

Laid out in the early 1900s City Beautiful movement style, this formal park connects the Capitol to the Denver City and County Building and the surrounding Golden Triangle museum district, its Greek amphitheater and colonnades giving downtown Denver an unexpectedly classical centerpiece. It's the natural pivot point of the day, with nearly every stop that follows within a short walk.

4

The museum's Frederic C. Hamilton Building, a jagged, titanium-clad structure designed by architect Daniel Libeskind and completed in 2006, is a landmark in its own right — an angular counterpoint to the Capitol's classical dome just down the street. Inside, the museum holds one of the most comprehensive collections of American Indian art in the world, alongside strong holdings in Western American and contemporary art.

5

Directly adjacent to the Denver Art Museum, this small, purpose-built museum holds the entire estate of Abstract Expressionist painter Clyfford Still — nearly 2,400 works that, per the terms of his will, could only be given in their entirety to a single city willing to build a dedicated museum. Denver took up the offer in 2011, and the result is a rare, complete window into one influential artist's full career.

6

The state's official history museum uses hands-on exhibits — including a giant interactive relief map of Colorado you can walk across — to trace everything from Ancestral Puebloan history at Mesa Verde to the mining booms, ski industry, and water conflicts that shaped the modern state. It's an efficient way to gain context for the mining towns and mountain landscapes you'll be visiting later in the week.

7

Denver's grand 1914 Beaux-Arts train station was beautifully restored in 2014 into a bustling hub of restaurants, a boutique hotel, and a soaring Great Hall lined with communal tables and string lights — it's as much a social gathering spot for locals today as a functioning transit station. It also anchors LoDo, the historic warehouse district that makes up the rest of the afternoon.

8

This mile-long pedestrian promenade runs the length of downtown Denver, lined with shops, restaurants, and a free shuttle bus that makes it easy to hop between the Capitol area and LoDo without a car. It's the connective spine of downtown sightseeing, less a single destination than the street you'll find yourself walking or riding along all day.

9

Denver's oldest and most historic block, Larimer Square preserves a row of ornate Victorian-era commercial buildings from the 1870s and 80s, saved from demolition by a 1960s preservation campaign that essentially launched Denver's historic preservation movement. Today its restored facades house upscale restaurants and boutiques strung with year-round twinkle lights, making it one of the most atmospheric spots in the city for a final dinner before heading further afield tomorrow.

Day 4 — Boulder & Rocky Mountain National Park

Today heads north out of Denver to Boulder, the laid-back college town tucked directly beneath the Flatirons, before continuing on to Rocky Mountain National Park — one of the crown jewels of the entire national park system and a full day's destination in its own right. Get an early start; Trail Ridge Road alone can take hours to drive properly, and afternoon thunderstorms are common at high elevation in summer.

1

Boulder's pedestrian-only downtown core has been a car-free shopping and dining strip since 1977, its brick pavers lined with street performers, buskers, and a dense cluster of independent shops backed by direct views of the Flatirons rising just west of downtown. It's the natural place to grab breakfast and coffee before heading up into the foothills.

2

The five slanted sandstone slabs known as the Flatirons form Boulder's single most recognizable backdrop, tilted at a dramatic angle by the same tectonic uplift that formed the Rockies some 300 million years ago. The Flatirons Vista Trail, an easy loop just south of town, offers some of the best unobstructed views of the formation without the steep, technical climbing routes serious rock climbers use to summit the Flatirons themselves.

3

Perched on a mesa above Boulder, this dramatic, reddish-toned building was designed by architect I.M. Pei to echo the sandstone cliffs of nearby Native American ancestral sites and now houses one of the world's leading atmospheric and climate research institutions. Free public tours and a small visitor center explain the science of weather and climate modeling, while trails leading out from the building climb directly into the surrounding foothills.

4

A short, easy walk from a pullout on winding Flagstaff Mountain Road leads to one of the best panoramic viewpoints over the Boulder Valley, taking in the Flatirons, the city grid below, and on clear days the snow-capped peaks of the Continental Divide far to the west. It's a quick but worthwhile detour on the drive out of town toward Estes Park and Rocky Mountain National Park.

5

Established in 1915, Rocky Mountain National Park packs more than 100 named peaks over 11,000 feet into a compact stretch of the Front Range accessible via the town of Estes Park. Trail Ridge Road, the highest continuously paved through-road in the country, climbs above 12,000 feet through open alpine tundra where elk graze right alongside the pavement — a required timed-entry reservation is needed for park access during peak summer season, so plan and book ahead. Even a short stop at one of the high-elevation overlooks delivers a view of the Continental Divide that ranks among the best in the entire Rocky Mountain range.

Day 5 — Golden, Morrison & Mount Blue Sky

Back through Denver and out the other side today, heading into the foothills southwest of the city before climbing to one of the highest paved roads on the continent. This route strings together Old West history, a natural rock amphitheater turned world-famous concert venue, and a genuine 14,000-foot summit, all within a couple hours' drive of downtown Denver.

1

A natural rock formation of towering red sandstone monoliths — Creation Rock and Ship Rock — flanks a stage that has hosted everyone from The Beatles to modern headline acts, its acoustics considered among the best of any outdoor venue in the world. Even without a concert scheduled, the amphitheater and its surrounding trails are open to the public and free to explore, and climbing to the top row of seats for the view back down the canyon is worth the visit on its own.

2

The small town of Morrison gave its name to the Morrison Formation, a rock layer famous worldwide for producing some of the most significant dinosaur fossil discoveries of the 19th century, and this compact museum displays finds excavated right in the surrounding hills. Just up the road, Mount Falcon Park's hiking trails lead past the stone ruins of a hilltop mansion built by newspaper publisher John Brisben Walker in the early 1900s, destroyed by fire decades ago and left as an evocative, half-standing ruin with sweeping views over the Denver plains.

3

Set on a working rail yard in Golden with the foothills as a backdrop, this museum preserves more than 100 pieces of historic narrow-gauge rolling stock from Colorado's mining-era railroads, housed in a replica 19th-century depot. It's a hands-on stop where many of the locomotives and cabooses can be boarded and explored, a favorite with train enthusiasts and kids alike.

4

Atop Lookout Mountain above Golden, this museum and gravesite honor William "Buffalo Bill" Cody, the frontier scout and showman whose traveling Wild West show made him one of the most famous Americans of the late 19th century. Cody specifically requested burial here for the panoramic views stretching from the Denver skyline out across the plains — the same view visitors get today from the overlook beside his grave.

5

A short drive further west via Idaho Springs, Mount Blue Sky's summit road climbs to 14,130 feet, making it the highest paved road in North America — a genuine feat of engineering that switchbacks above treeline into a stark, wind-scoured alpine world where mountain goats and bighorn sheep are a near-guaranteed sight. Renamed from Mount Evans in 2023 in recognition of the Cheyenne and Arapaho peoples, the drive to the top delivers a 14,000-foot summit experience without requiring a single step of strenuous hiking — though the altitude alone can leave visitors winded just getting out of the car.

Day 6 — Breckenridge & Leadville

Continuing west and south along the spine of the Rockies, today leaves the Denver metro area behind entirely for two of Colorado's most storied mining-boom towns, both now beautifully preserved and easily explored on foot. The drive between them crosses Fremont Pass at over 11,000 feet, with views of some of the highest peaks in the entire Rocky Mountain range.

1

Founded during an 1859 gold rush, Breckenridge has preserved one of the largest historic districts in Colorado — more than 250 Victorian-era buildings still line its Main Street, now filled with galleries, breweries, and outdoor outfitters rather than saloons and assay offices. Today it's better known as a major ski resort town, but the gold-rush bones are everywhere, from restored miners' cabins to a Main Street grid still laid out exactly as it was in the 1880s.

2

At 10,152 feet, Leadville holds the title of highest incorporated city in the United States, and in the 1880s its silver mines briefly made it one of the richest and rowdiest boomtowns in the country, home for a time to a young Molly Brown before her fame on the Titanic. The National Mining Hall of Fame and Museum and a downtown of intact Victorian storefronts capture that era vividly, while the thin, high-altitude air today draws elite endurance athletes training for events like the notoriously grueling Leadville 100 trail races.

Day 7 — Glenwood Springs, Aspen & Snowmass

Your final day follows the Colorado River and then the Roaring Fork Valley into some of the state's most famous resort towns, ending at one of the most photographed mountain landscapes in North America. It's a fitting close to the trip — from Colorado Springs' single fourteener to a whole cluster of jagged, snow-streaked peaks reflected in a still alpine lake.

1

Tucked at the mouth of dramatic Glenwood Canyon, this town has been a hot springs resort destination since the 1880s, when the enormous mineral pool downtown — still among the largest hot springs pools in the world — drew visitors including Theodore Roosevelt and gunfighter Doc Holliday, who is buried on a hillside overlooking town. A soak in the mineral-rich water, framed by canyon walls on all sides, makes an ideal midday break on the drive toward Aspen.

2

Once a 19th-century silver-mining boomtown, Aspen reinvented itself after World War II into one of the world's premier ski and mountain resort destinations, its downtown a mix of preserved Victorian brick buildings and high-end galleries, restaurants, and boutiques. Even outside ski season, the town's compact, walkable core and the gondola ride up Aspen Mountain for panoramic valley views make it worth a slow afternoon of wandering.

3

A short drive from Aspen, Snowmass Village offers a quieter, more laid-back alternative to its famous neighbor, built around one of the largest ski mountains in Colorado and, in summer, a base camp for hiking and mountain biking trails fanning out into the surrounding Elk Mountains. Its pedestrian-friendly base village makes it an easy, relaxed stop on the way to the day's final destination.

4

Widely regarded as the most photographed mountain scene in North America, the twin peaks of North and South Maroon Peak rise directly above still Maroon Lake, their deep red, mineral-streaked rock reflected almost perfectly on calm mornings. A mandatory seasonal shuttle from Aspen manages traffic into the narrow valley during peak summer and fall color months — arrive early, or take the shuttle up for sunrise, for both the best light and the smallest crowds at one of the most stunning natural landscapes anywhere in the Rockies, and a fitting final image to close out a week spent chasing Colorado's highest peaks.
