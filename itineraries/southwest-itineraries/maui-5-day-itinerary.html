Maui: Valleys, Volcano, and the Winding Road to Hana

5 Days

Maui packs an outsized range of landscapes into a single island — a lush, knife-ridged valley at its historic center; a 10,000-foot volcanic summit cold enough for frost; a legendary coastal road strung with waterfalls and black-sand coves; a whaling-era port town turned resort strip; and a leeward coastline of golden beaches facing a sunken volcanic crater offshore. This 5-day itinerary moves outward from Central Maui in a loop that respects the island's geography, climbing Haleakala before the crowds arrive, tracing the full Road to Hana in a single unhurried day, and closing with the contrasting coastlines of West and South Maui.

Day 1

Begin in Central Maui, in and around the twin towns of Wailuku and Kahului, where the island's plantation-era history and its deepest Hawaiian roots sit within a few minutes of each other. A jagged green valley sacred to Hawaiian royalty, a missionary-era church, and a wetland sanctuary busy with native waterbirds trace the island's story from ancient chiefdom through sugar plantation to modern gateway — all before you've driven more than a few miles from the airport.

1

This lush, cathedral-like valley in the West Maui Mountains is dominated by the Iao Needle, a 1,200-foot volcanic spire wrapped in cloud and rainforest. Sacred to Native Hawaiians and the site of a decisive 1790 battle in which King Kamehameha I conquered Maui, the valley today offers an easy paved trail leading to sweeping viewpoints and cool, misty streams.

2

Set in a former missionary home built partly atop the walls of an old Hawaiian compound, this small but rich museum houses Maui's most significant collection of Hawaiian artifacts, missionary-era furniture, and historic paintings, offering essential context before venturing further into the valley beyond.

3

Named for Queen Kaahumanu, the powerful favorite wife of Kamehameha I who requested a church be built on this site, this New England-style stone church anchors Wailuku's historic district. Its clean white steeple and Hawaiian-language services reflect the enduring blend of missionary architecture and native tradition found throughout the town.

4

These two adjoining Hawaiian temple platforms overlook the Wailuku plain and the ocean beyond, once central to the religious and political power of Maui's ruling chiefs. Halekii ("house of images") and Pihana ("gathering place") together offer one of the best-preserved windows into pre-contact Hawaiian religious life on the island.

5

Housed in a former plantation manager's residence in Kahului, this museum traces the sugarcane industry that shaped Maui's economy, immigrant workforce, and landscape for well over a century. Historic tools, irrigation models, and photographs explain how sugar transformed the island long before tourism did.

6

Tucked beside Kahului's airport runways, this quiet wetland refuge shelters the endangered Hawaiian stilt and Hawaiian coot amid mudflats and mangroves, an unexpectedly peaceful pocket of nature to close out a day spent among Central Maui's historic sites.

Day 2

Rise well before dawn for the drive up Haleakala, the massive shield volcano whose name means "House of the Sun." At over 10,000 feet, the summit offers a lunar, otherworldly landscape and — with the right timing — a legendary sunrise above the clouds that Mark Twain once called the most sublime spectacle he had ever witnessed. The day continues downhill through Upcountry Maui's cooler farm country, a landscape of flower farms, ranches, and small mission churches that feels worlds away from the beach.

1

Haleakala Volcano last erupted centuries ago and now stands as the centerpiece of a national park spanning coastal rainforest to alpine desert. At the summit, Pu'u Ula Ula (Red Hill) marks the highest point on Maui at 10,023 feet, with the overlooks at Kalahaku and Leleiwi offering equally dramatic views into the crater below, especially at sunrise when reservations are required in advance.

2

The Sliding Sands Trail descends steeply from the summit into the crater's cinder-cone landscape, a stark, Mars-like terrain in shades of red, orange, and black. Along the way, the short Silversword Loop showcases the rare Haleakala silversword, a spiky, silver-leafed plant found nowhere else on Earth that blooms once in its lifetime before dying.

3

Planted in 1910 as an early experiment in commercial forestry, this grove of towering non-native conifers and eucalyptus stands in striking contrast to the surrounding native shrubland. A short nature loop here is one of the best places in the park to spot native Hawaiian honeycreepers.

4

Descending into Upcountry, the cool climate around Kula supports two flower-filled attractions — the Kula Botanical Garden and the Enchanted Floral Gardens — both showcasing proteas, orchids, and native plants rarely seen at sea level. For a more rugged detour, Polipoli Spring State Recreation Area offers misty redwood groves and hiking trails at nearly 6,000 feet, a surprising echo of the Pacific Northwest.

5

This octagonal Catholic church, built in 1897 by Portuguese immigrant sugar workers, remains one of Upcountry's most distinctive landmarks, its hand-carved altar shipped from Austria giving an unexpected touch of European craftsmanship to the ranchlands of Maui.

Day 3

Set aside the full day for the Road to Hana, one of the most celebrated drives in the world — 64 miles and roughly 620 curves of two-lane road threading past waterfalls, rainforest, and dramatic coastline on Maui's wild windward side. The destination matters less than the journey itself; plan to start early, take your time at each stop, and expect the drive alone to fill most of the day before continuing on to the pools and trails of Kipahulu beyond Hana town.

1

Just before the road narrows, Hookipa Beach is world-renowned among windsurfers and kiteboarders for its powerful, consistent swells and serves as a scenic overlook even for those not in the water. Further along, the Waikamoi Preserve offers a short nature trail through native rainforest, an early taste of the dense greenery to come.

2

These two roadside parks offer some of the drive's most reliable rest stops, with Kaumahina delivering a sweeping coastal viewpoint back toward Keanae, and Puaa Kaa providing small waterfalls and swimming pools just steps from the car — easy, low-effort highlights along an otherwise winding route.

3

The Keanae Arboretum showcases native and introduced Hawaiian plants including taro, breadfruit, and rainbow eucalyptus with its strikingly multicolored bark. Just beyond, the volcanic Keanae Peninsula remains home to a traditional taro-farming community and a small white church standing defiantly against the crashing surf.

4

This expansive ethnobotanical garden preserves canoe plants brought by Polynesian voyagers alongside Piilanihale Heiau, the largest ancient Hawaiian temple platform in the state — a massive lava-rock structure believed to have been built for the powerful Maui chief Pi'ilani.

5

Home to one of Hawaii's most photographed black-sand beaches, this coastal park also hides freshwater lava tube caves, a natural sea arch, and a dramatic blowhole along its rugged shoreline, all framed by jet-black volcanic rock against turquoise water.

6

The small, remote town of Hana feels frozen in an earlier, slower era of Hawaii, with red-sand and black-sand beaches, historic churches, and a genuine sense of isolation from the resorts of West and South Maui. It makes a natural lunch stop before continuing on toward Kipahulu.

7

Just past Hana, in the Kipahulu section of Haleakala National Park, the Pipiwai Trail climbs through a giant bamboo forest and past the cascading Falls at Makahiku to the 400-foot Waimoku Falls. Below, the terraced Pools of Oheo — long nicknamed the "Seven Sacred Pools" at Ohe'o Gulch — tumble down toward the ocean, with the Kipahulu Valley Biological Reserve protecting the rare rainforest watershed above, off-limits to hikers but visible from a distance.

Day 4

Today heads to West Maui, where a 19th-century whaling port has become one of the island's most popular resort corridors, and the coastline beyond grows wilder and more dramatic the further north you drive. Wander the wooden storefronts of a once-rowdy sailors' town, admire the pristine beaches that made Kaanapali and Kapalua famous, and — for those craving a true detour — follow the narrow, cliff-hugging road around the island's remote northwest tip.

1

Once the capital of the Hawaiian Kingdom and later a rowdy 19th-century whaling port, Lahaina's historic district preserves this layered past through sites including the Lahaina Banyan Court, planted in 1873 and among the largest banyan trees in the country; the Pioneer Inn, Maui's oldest hotel; Waiola Church and cemetery, resting place of Hawaiian royalty; Maria Lanakila Catholic Church; and the Wo Hing Temple Museum, a former Chinese fraternal society hall documenting the island's immigrant plantation labor history.

2

Nicknamed the "Sugar Cane Train," this restored narrow-gauge steam railroad once hauled cane between plantation fields and mill, and now carries visitors on a scenic, old-fashioned ride connecting the historic town to the resort area of Kaanapali.

3

Hawaii's first master-planned resort destination, Kaanapali is anchored by a three-mile stretch of golden sand and Black Rock, a lava outcropping prized for snorkeling and, according to Hawaiian tradition, the point from which souls leaped into the afterlife.

4

Further up the coast, the quieter, more upscale Kapalua Resort fronts pristine crescent bays framed by former pineapple fields. Rising above it, the Puu Kukui Watershed Preserve protects one of the wettest spots on Earth, a rare, boggy summit ecosystem that feeds the streams of the entire West Maui Mountains.

5

For a wilder detour, the road continues past Honolua Bay, a protected marine reserve prized for snorkeling and surfing, into the remote village of Kahakuloa, one of the last traditional Hawaiian settlements accessible by car. Beyond it, Nakalele Point features a natural blowhole that erupts dramatically along a stark, wind-scoured lava coastline — a rugged bookend to the manicured resorts left behind.

Day 5

Your final day turns to South Maui, a sun-drenched coastline of resort towns, wetland preserves, and one of the island's most extraordinary offshore features. Begin at the aquarium gateway between the island's two coasts, look for native waterbirds in a protected wetland, and consider capping the trip with a boat excursion out to a crescent-shaped volcanic crater rising from the sea, before an easy final evening among the beach resorts of Wailea.

1

Set at Maalaea Harbor between West and South Maui, this large aquarium focuses exclusively on Hawaiian marine life, from a walk-through acrylic tunnel surrounded by reef sharks to exhibits on humpback whales and coral reef ecology, making it an excellent introduction to the waters you may snorkel later in the trip.

2

This coastal wetland refuge along the isthmus between Maui's two mountain masses provides critical habitat for the endangered Hawaiian stilt and coot, alongside migratory shorebirds. A boardwalk trail and visitor center make it an easy, informative stop on the drive south.

3

Strung along the coastal highway between Maalaea and West Maui, this ironwood-shaded roadside park offers a long, uncrowded beach with views out toward the neighboring islands of Lanai and Kahoolawe, a quiet contrast to South Maui's busier resort beaches.

4

Visible offshore from South Maui, this partially submerged volcanic crater forms a crescent-shaped islet and marine preserve renowned for some of the clearest water and best snorkeling in Hawaii. Half-day boat excursions departing from Maalaea or Kihei harbors are the only way to reach it, and its protected waters teem with reef fish rarely seen so close to shore.

5

Close the trip in Wailea, South Maui's polished resort district, where a paved coastal path links a string of golden-sand beaches backed by upscale hotels, restaurants, and gardens — a relaxed, sun-soaked finale after a week spent tracing the island's volcanic peaks, waterfall-lined coastline, and historic port towns.
