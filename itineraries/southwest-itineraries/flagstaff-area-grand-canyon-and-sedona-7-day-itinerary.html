Red Rocks & Deep Time: Flagstaff, Grand Canyon & Sedona

7 Days

Northern Arizona packs an almost absurd range of geologic time into a single tank of gas: a volcano that erupted less than a thousand years ago, a canyon two billion years in the making, a meteorite strike from 50,000 years back, and red rock formations still being sculpted by wind and water today. This 7‑day itinerary starts among Flagstaff's volcanic fields and ancient pueblos, spends two full days on the Grand Canyon's South Rim, and closes with four days threading the slot canyons, vortex sites, and sandstone spires of Sedona. Grouped by drive time rather than list order, the trip trades a handful of Sedona's more remote backcountry trails for the routes and viewpoints that define the region at its most iconic — red rock underfoot for most of the week, and one of the seven natural wonders of the world in the middle of it.

Day 1 — Flagstaff's Volcanoes & Ancient Pueblos

Your trip opens on a 34-mile scenic loop north of Flagstaff that manages to connect a live volcanic landscape to nine-hundred-year-old cliff dwellings, followed by a detour east to one of the best-preserved meteorite impact craters on Earth. It's a day that moves through deep time almost too quickly to process — cinder fields barely a thousand years old giving way to a crater older than modern humans.

1

This volcanic cinder cone erupted around the year 1085, making it one of the youngest volcanoes in the continental United States, and its slopes still show the black, jagged lava flows and orange-red rim that gave it its name — the color reminded early explorers of a permanent sunset. Climbing the cone itself is prohibited to protect the fragile cinder, but the mile-long Lava Flow Trail loops directly across the solidified lava field at its base, offering an up-close look at a landscape barely a thousand years old.

2

Continuing along the same scenic loop road, Wupatki preserves the red sandstone pueblos built by ancestral Puebloan farmers who moved into the area after Sunset Crater's eruption — the volcanic ash, counterintuitively, improved soil moisture retention and drew a wave of new settlement. The largest structure, Wupatki Pueblo, once stood multiple stories tall and included a rare masonry ballcourt, evidence of trade and cultural exchange with communities as far away as Mesoamerica.

3

A short drive east of Flagstaff, Walnut Canyon holds dozens of cliff dwellings built into natural limestone alcoves by the Sinagua people around the 1100s, tucked into the walls of a steep, narrow canyon. The Island Trail's roughly 240 steps descend to a rock "island" formed by a bend in the canyon, looping directly past more than 20 preserved dwelling rooms — a rare chance to walk through the doorways of homes nearly a thousand years old.

4

About 35 miles east of Flagstaff near Winslow, this nearly mile-wide crater was gouged out some 50,000 years ago by a nickel-iron meteorite traveling at roughly 26,000 miles per hour — it remains the best-preserved impact crater on the planet, so pristine that NASA once used it to train Apollo astronauts for lunar terrain. Observation decks along the rim, along with a small museum housing a 1,406-pound meteorite fragment, put the crater's genuinely alien scale into perspective before you head back into Flagstaff for the night.

Day 2 — Grand Canyon South Rim: The Village & Hermit Road

Nothing quite prepares you for the first look over the edge, no matter how many photos you've seen. Today centers on Grand Canyon Village and Hermit Road to its west, a corridor of some of the park's most photographed viewpoints connected by a mostly flat, paved rim trail — an easy day logistically, but one that deserves unhurried time at nearly every stop.

1

Open year-round and sitting at roughly 7,000 feet elevation, the South Rim is where the vast majority of the six million annual visitors experience the Grand Canyon, and for good reason — it offers the easiest access, the most developed infrastructure, and views that need absolutely no embellishment. Nearly two billion years of exposed rock layers, a mile deep and up to 18 miles across, make this one of the very few landscapes on Earth that genuinely looks bigger than any photograph can capture.

2

Named for Stephen Mather, the first director of the National Park Service, this is the closest major overlook to the main visitor center and the spot where most first-time visitors get their initial view into the canyon. It's understandably crowded, but the sheer width of the vista — stretching across multiple side canyons and buttes — means there's rarely a bad angle, even shoulder to shoulder with other visitors.

3

Running roughly 13 miles along the canyon edge and mostly paved, the Rim Trail is the connective spine of a South Rim visit, linking Mather Point to Hermits Rest with a free shuttle running parallel for anyone who doesn't want to walk the whole way. Because it requires no significant elevation change, it's the rare Grand Canyon trail accessible to nearly everyone, letting visitors pick off multiple viewpoints at a relaxed, self-directed pace.

4

The most famous of the Grand Canyon's corridor trails, Bright Angel follows an old Havasupai route down through several distinct rock layers toward the Colorado River, with rest houses and seasonal water at 1.5 and 3 miles down for day hikers who don't need to go all the way to the bottom. Even a short walk past the first switchbacks offers a dramatically different perspective than any rim overlook — remember that the return climb takes roughly twice as long as the descent, so turn back well before you feel tired.

5

This 7-mile scenic route west of the village strings together some of the South Rim's most celebrated overlooks, including Powell Point, Hopi Point, and Mohave Point — closed to private vehicles for most of the year and served instead by a free shuttle, which keeps the road pleasantly uncrowded compared to the more heavily trafficked eastern viewpoints. Hopi Point in particular is one of the best spots on the entire rim to watch the sun set over the canyon's shifting colors.

6

Marking the western end of Hermit Road, this rustic stone building was designed in 1914 by architect Mary Colter, who deliberately built it to look like the ramshackle dwelling of an eccentric hermit, complete with a massive stone fireplace and deliberately uneven, hand-hewn construction. It's one of several Colter-designed structures on the South Rim, and remains a working rest stop and gift shop with one of the finest sunset views along Hermit Road.

Day 3 — Grand Canyon South Rim: Desert View Drive

Today follows Desert View Drive east from the village, a 25-mile route that climbs to some of the highest and most dramatic viewpoints on the South Rim before ending at a stone watchtower with a view stretching into the Painted Desert. A short, steep hike partway down the South Kaibab Trail delivers one of the biggest payoffs on the entire trip for one of the smallest time investments.

1

Reached via a steep but short 0.9-mile hike down the South Kaibab Trail, Ooh Aah Point earns its slightly on-the-nose name honestly — it's the first spot on the trail where the canyon suddenly opens into a full, unobstructed panorama rather than the more limited views from the rim above. Unlike Bright Angel, the South Kaibab Trail follows an exposed ridgeline rather than a side canyon, so there's no shade and no water, but the views in exchange are considered by many hikers to be the best short hike in the entire park.

2

Built in the 1890s to service the Last Chance copper mine on Horseshoe Mesa, this steep, unmaintained trail is far less crowded than the corridor trails and drops through some of the canyon's most striking rock formations almost immediately from the trailhead. Even a short descent of the first switchbacks — among the most exposed and rugged of any maintained South Rim trail — rewards experienced hikers with genuine solitude and a different geological perspective than the busier trails nearby.

3

Beyond the trailheads, Desert View Drive is dotted with pullouts at Moran Point, Lipan Point, and Navajo Point, each offering a subtly different angle on the canyon's layered geology and, at Lipan Point in particular, one of the best unobstructed views of the Colorado River itself. These quieter overlooks, generally less crowded than the village-area viewpoints, are worth stopping at even briefly as you make your way east.

4

The 25-mile drive itself is part of the appeal here — a winding two-lane road through ponderosa pine forest that periodically opens onto sweeping canyon views, quieter and less trafficked than the shuttle-only Hermit Road. Give yourself most of the day for the full round trip with stops; it's a slow, scenic drive by design, not a route meant to be rushed.

5

Standing at the eastern end of the drive, this 70-foot stone tower was designed in 1932 by Mary Colter, who modeled it closely on ancestral Puebloan watchtowers found throughout the Southwest — inside, murals painted by celebrated Hopi artist Fred Kabotie depict Hopi legend and ceremony across several floors. Climbing to the top delivers a rare vantage point looking directly down onto the Colorado River and out toward the Painted Desert, a fitting final image before the drive back to Flagstaff or on toward Sedona.

Day 4 — Sedona: Town, Landmarks & Vortex Views

A scenic 2-hour drive south from the Grand Canyon brings you into Sedona, where towering red sandstone buttes surround a small town built around art galleries, New Age shops, and some of the most photographed rock formations in the American Southwest. Today is an orientation day among the town's most iconic landmarks, easing in before the heavier trail days ahead.

1

Named in 1902 for Sedona Schnebly, a local postmaster's wife, this small city of around 10,000 residents sits ringed by red sandstone buttes and spires that glow a deeper orange-red at sunrise and sunset than at any other time of day. Since the 1980s, Sedona has also become closely associated with New Age spirituality and its purported energy "vortex" sites, but even visitors with no interest in that side of things will find the sheer geologic drama of the surrounding formations reason enough to visit.

2

Sedona's small municipal airport sits improbably atop a flat-topped mesa overlooking town, and the scenic lookout just below the runway is widely considered the single best sunset spot in Sedona, with a 360-degree view over the surrounding red rock formations. It's also cited as one of the area's strongest vortex sites, drawing a steady stream of visitors at both sunrise and sunset regardless of their views on the vortex phenomenon itself.

3

Built in the 1970s and modeled after the arts town of Tlaquepaque in Guadalajara, Mexico, this shopping village recreates a Mexican colonial atmosphere with cobblestone walkways, wrought-iron balconies, and towering sycamore trees shading dozens of independent galleries, boutiques, and restaurants. It's a deliberately unhurried counterpoint to the trail-heavy days ahead, and one of the best places in town for a relaxed lunch.

4

Designed in 1956 by Marguerite Brunswig Staude, a student of Frank Lloyd Wright, this striking modernist chapel appears to grow directly out of a red rock cliff face, its 90-foot cross-shaped window framing the surrounding buttes from the altar inside. Still an active Catholic chapel today, it welcomes visitors of all faiths, and the short paved switchback path up to its entrance offers one of Sedona's most dramatic combinations of architecture and landscape.

Day 5 — Sedona: Oak Creek Canyon

Highway 89A north out of town climbs into Oak Creek Canyon, a lush, forested gorge that feels almost alpine compared to Sedona's desert-red rock — towering canyon walls, a cold, clear creek, and hiking trails that stay shaded and cool even in the height of summer. It's the natural place to swap dry heat for a swim.

1

Oak Creek has carved a natural, algae-slicked waterslide directly into the sandstone here, and generations of visitors have used it exactly as the name suggests — sliding down a smooth eighty-foot chute into a cold pool below. The park also preserves the historic Pendley Homestead, a working apple orchard settled in the 1910s, and remains one of the most popular swimming spots in all of Arizona, so arrive early in summer to beat both the crowds and the parking lot closures.

2

Consistently ranked among the best hikes in Arizona, this trail follows a tributary of Oak Creek through a narrowing canyon of towering, water-stained walls, crossing the creek more than a dozen times along its roughly 6-mile round trip. The payoff is a hike that feels almost otherworldly compared to Sedona's arid red rock trails — deep shade, hanging gardens of ferns, and a genuine sense of stepping into a hidden world.

3

Originally cut in the early 1900s by rancher Abraham Young to move cattle in and out of Oak Creek Canyon and later improved by the Civilian Conservation Corps, this steep switchback trail climbs directly out of the canyon floor to the East Pocket fire lookout on the rim above. It's far less crowded than Slide Rock or West Fork just down the road, and the aggressive elevation gain delivers a genuinely different perspective — looking down into the canyon rather than up at its walls.

Day 6 — Sedona: Dry Creek & Boynton Canyon

West of town, the Dry Creek Road corridor holds some of Sedona's single most iconic hikes — a natural sandstone arch, a canyon sacred to the Yavapai-Apache people, and a hollowed cave alcove that ranks among the most photographed spots in the entire region. This is the highlight reel of Sedona hiking, best tackled with an early start to beat both the heat and the crowds.

1

Sedona's largest natural sandstone arch and arguably its single most photographed hike, Devils Bridge draws crowds for good reason — the moderate 4-mile round trip trail ends with a short, exposed scramble onto a narrow stone span with a sheer drop and a jaw-dropping view down the canyon behind it. Parking at the unpaved trailhead fills fast, so most visitors either arrive at sunrise or add roughly 2 extra miles by parking along Dry Creek Road and walking in.

2

Considered sacred ground by the Yavapai-Apache people, Boynton Canyon is flanked by towering red rock walls on both sides and is also cited as one of Sedona's most powerful vortex locations. The roughly 5-mile round-trip trail winds through fragrant juniper and pine forest at the canyon floor before climbing to a dead-end alcove, passing directly below the luxury Enchantment Resort tucked discreetly into the canyon's mouth.

3

A shorter, easier neighbor to Boynton Canyon just across Dry Creek Road, this shaded, mostly flat trail makes an excellent lower-effort addition to the day, with a short, steep spur partway along leading up to a natural sandstone arch tucked into the canyon wall. It's an easy trail to underestimate given the bigger names nearby, but the arch detour alone makes it worth the stop.

4

A short, steep series of switchbacks climbs about 400 feet to the flat mesa top of Doe Mountain, delivering an outsized panoramic reward for a relatively modest time investment — roughly a mile round trip to the top, plus as much wandering across the open summit as you'd like. It's one of the best easily accessible sunset spots in West Sedona, without the crowds that gather at Airport Mesa.

5

A short, easy walk near the mouth of Long Canyon leads to this shallow, wide alcove, hollowed by erosion into a shape some visitors interpret as resembling a womb — hence the name, and its associated folklore as a site historically used by Native American women during childbirth. Whatever you make of the legend, the smooth, curving rock overhead makes for one of the most striking and easily photographed formations in the entire Dry Creek area, and a relaxed close to a demanding trail day.

Day 7 — Sedona: The Village of Oak Creek & Soldier Pass

Your final day heads south along Highway 179 to Bell Rock and Cathedral Rock — two of the most recognizable formations in all of Sedona — before finishing at a state park and a trail riddled with sinkholes and outlaw legend on the north side of town. It's a fitting close: a full week of geology, from an active-in-geologic-time volcano near Flagstaff to the eroding red sandstone of Sedona's final trails.

1

This easy, mostly flat loop trail circles two of Sedona's most recognizable formations — the bell-shaped Bell Rock, said by many to be one of the strongest vortex sites in the area, and the massive, flat-topped Courthouse Butte beside it. Well-marked and gently graded, it's one of the most approachable trails on this list, making it a good choice for a lighter final morning after a week of steeper hikes.

2

Cathedral Rock's spires are arguably the single most photographed formation in Sedona, especially reflected in Oak Creek from nearby Red Rock Crossing at sunset. The trail to its saddle is short but genuinely challenging, involving hand-over-hand slickrock scrambling up smooth, steep sandstone with real exposure in places — it's a rewarding scramble for those comfortable with it, but not a casual stroll like the Bell Rock loop.

3

Less crowded than Sedona's trail system, this state park along Oak Creek functions as an environmental education center, with gentle nature trails winding through riparian woodland and past the historic "House of Apache Fire," a stone home built by a Hollywood-connected couple in the 1940s. It's a calmer, greener counterpoint to the red rock scrambles of the past few days, with ranger-led nature walks available for those interested in the area's plant and animal life.

4

On the north side of town, this trail passes three distinct natural features in quick succession: the Seven Sacred Pools, a series of stepped potholes carved into slickrock; Devil's Kitchen, one of the largest sinkholes in Arizona, formed by an underground cavern collapse; and Robbers Roost, a cluster of shallow caves in a rock outcropping long rumored — with little solid evidence — to have sheltered outlaws hiding from the law in the late 1800s. Limited daily parking permits are required at the trailhead and should be reserved in advance online.

5

Tucked into the Broken Arrow area southeast of town, this trail climbs to a dramatic overlook where the slickrock drops away sharply beneath your feet — the source of its name — with sweeping views back over Sedona's red rock skyline. Popular with both hikers and Jeep tour operators navigating the area's famously rugged slickrock roads, it's a fittingly dramatic final overlook to close out a week spent chasing viewpoints across northern Arizona.
