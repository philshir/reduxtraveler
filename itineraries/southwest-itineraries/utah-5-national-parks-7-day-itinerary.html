Utah's Mighty 5: A Seven-Day Journey Through Arches, Canyonlands, Capitol Reef, Bryce Canyon & Zion

7 Days

Few places on Earth compress so much geologic drama into so small a corner of a map. In seven days, this itinerary carries you across red rock deserts carved by ten million years of wind and water, through a labyrinth of sandstone fins and freestanding arches, over mesas that drop a thousand feet to the Colorado River, along a buckled reef of Navajo sandstone once impassable to wagons, into a amphitheater of glowing hoodoos, and finally down a slot canyon carved by the Virgin River. Utah's five national parks — Arches, Canyonlands, Capitol Reef, Bryce Canyon, and Zion — are strung along a single great loop through the state's high desert, and this route follows them roughly west to east and north to south, minimizing driving time while maximizing golden-hour light on some of the most photographed rock formations in the American West. Not every trail on the map is included here; with sixteen days' worth of hiking packed into a week, this itinerary favors the routes and viewpoints that consistently rank as the most rewarding, leaving the more remote backcountry — Horseshoe Canyon, the Maze, Cathedral Valley — for a future trip.


Day 1 — Arches National Park: The Windows & Park Avenue

Your week begins just outside Moab, where a single park road climbs onto a plateau holding the highest concentration of natural stone arches on the planet. Today is an introduction to the park's grand scale — towering sandstone fins, balanced boulders the size of houses, and the first of more than two thousand catalogued arches. The pace is easy and the rewards are immediate: every pullout along this stretch of road delivers a formation that would anchor a lesser park all on its own. Save some energy for late afternoon, when the low sun sets the rock glowing deep orange-red.


1


Just past the entrance station, the Park Avenue Trail drops you into a canyon lined with soaring sandstone fins whose silhouettes recall a skyline of skyscrapers — hence the name. The easy 2-mile round-trip walk along the canyon floor is one of the best short introductions to Arches' scale, and it's especially striking in early morning when the eastern fins catch the first light.



2


A boulder the size of three school buses balances impossibly atop a slender sandstone pedestal, the product of the same erosional forces that carved every arch nearby but stopped just short of toppling it. A short 0.3-mile loop circles the formation, offering changing perspectives as you walk, and the pullout doubles as one of the park's most reliable sunset photo spots.



3


The Windows section clusters several massive arches within a single short walk, making it the most efficient place in the park to see monumental sandstone openings up close. North Window and South Window frame the desert like a pair of enormous eyes, while nearby Turret Arch adds a third distinct silhouette. The full loop covers about an hour of easy walking.



4


A short walk from the Windows parking area leads to two massive arches that share a single stone base, springing from the same point of rock like a pair of open jaws. Double Arch's south opening is tall enough to walk directly beneath, and the formation's scale — the tallest arch opening in the park — makes it a favorite spot to simply sit and take in the geology.





Day 2 — Arches National Park: Delicate Arch & Devils Garden

Today tackles the two centerpiece hikes of Arches, saving the most iconic formation in Utah for golden hour. Start early to beat both the heat and the crowds in Devils Garden, then spend the afternoon working toward Delicate Arch — the free-standing sandstone arch that appears on Utah license plates and is, for many visitors, the singular image of the American Southwest.


1


The trail through Devils Garden is Arches' premier backcountry hike, threading between towering fins to reach a string of arches over roughly 7 miles round-trip if done in full. Most visitors walk the easy first 1.6 miles to Landscape Arch, a ribbon of stone so thin and so long — over 290 feet — that it seems to defy physics. It is the longest natural arch in North America.



2


Tucked between narrow sandstone fins, Sand Dune Arch sits in a cool, shaded pocket with soft sand underfoot, making it a favorite short detour for families and a nice midday break from the sun. A short drive away, Skyline Arch frames a wide desert vista and is easily viewed from its roadside pullout, with a brief walk available for a closer look.



3


A maze of narrow, sunlit corridors between towering sandstone fins, the Fiery Furnace is unlike anywhere else in the park — a place where the trail all but disappears and route-finding becomes part of the adventure. Access requires a permit or a ranger-led tour, but the reward is a sense of scale and solitude found nowhere else in Arches.



4


The 3-mile round-trip hike to Delicate Arch climbs slickrock with almost no shade, but the payoff is unmatched: a 60-foot freestanding arch perched on the rim of a natural sandstone bowl, with the La Sal Mountains rising in the distance behind it. Time the hike for late afternoon, when the arch and canyon walls turn a deep amber and the crowds gathered at the bowl's edge fall quiet as the sun drops.





Day 3 — Canyonlands National Park: Island in the Sky

A short drive from Moab, the Island in the Sky mesa rises more than a thousand feet above the surrounding terrain, offering some of the most dramatic overlooks in the entire national park system. Where Arches is intimate and close-up, Canyonlands is vast — layered canyons stretch to the horizon in every direction, carved by the Green and Colorado Rivers where they meet far below. Today moves at a slower, more contemplative pace, built around a handful of short trails that each open onto staggering views.


1


Perched directly on the rim of a sheer cliff, Mesa Arch frames an enormous canyon view through its stone window and is one of the most photographed spots in Utah at sunrise, when light bounces off the underside of the arch and glows a brilliant orange. The half-mile round-trip trail is short and easy, though arriving early is essential to beat both the light and the crowds.



2


At the southern tip of the mesa, Grand View Point delivers exactly what its name promises: an unbroken panorama over the White Rim sandstone bench and the canyons beyond, with the confluence of the Green and Colorado Rivers hidden somewhere in the maze below. A 2-mile round-trip trail follows the rim for those who want more than the overlook alone.



3


A short walk leads to the rim of one of Utah's strangest formations — a mile-wide crater of jumbled, colorful rock whose origin is still debated by geologists, with theories ranging from a collapsed salt dome to the scar of an ancient meteorite impact. The overlook trail is brief, but the odd, chaotic landscape below is unlike anything else in the park.



4


A quieter, less-crowded alternative to the park's marquee viewpoints, this 1.8-mile round-trip trail traces the mesa's edge to a series of overlooks above the White Rim, the pale sandstone bench that famously carries the 100-mile White Rim Road far below. It's an excellent spot for a quiet lunch with a view that rivals any in the park.





Day 4 — Capitol Reef National Park: The Waterpocket Fold

Leaving Moab behind, the drive west onto Highway 24 reveals Capitol Reef's great secret: a 100-mile wrinkle in the Earth's crust called the Waterpocket Fold, where colorful sandstone cliffs rise abruptly from the desert floor. Less visited than its neighbors, Capitol Reef rewards travelers with a quieter, more personal encounter with red rock — a historic orchard town tucked improbably into the canyon, petroglyphs left by the ancient Fremont people, and slot-like gorges that once served as the only wagon route through the reef.


1


The paved Scenic Drive winds beneath towering cliffs of the Waterpocket Fold, passing through Fruita, a small historic settlement where Mormon pioneers planted orchards of apple, peach, and cherry trees that still bear fruit today, tended by the park service. The green valley floor, framed by red rock walls, is one of the more surreal contrasts in the entire park system.



2


This 1.7-mile round-trip trail climbs through slickrock and past ancient Fremont granaries to reach a massive natural bridge spanning 133 feet — large enough to walk beneath. It's widely considered the best short hike in the park, offering a genuine sense of accomplishment without requiring a full day.



3


Once the only passable wagon route through the Waterpocket Fold, Capitol Gorge is now a flat, easy walk between narrow canyon walls. Along the way, the Pioneer Register preserves the carved names of 19th-century settlers and explorers who passed through, turning the hike into a walk through both geology and history.



4


A striking red-rock spire rising above Highway 24, Chimney Rock can be admired from the road or explored via a moderate 3.6-mile loop trail with sweeping views of the Fold. Nearby, a roadside boardwalk leads to a cliff face covered in Fremont petroglyphs, images pecked into the desert varnish centuries ago.



5


Close out the day at Panorama Point, an easy roadside overlook facing west across the Fold toward Boulder Mountain, then walk the short Sunset Point Trail nearby as the evening light sets the cliffs ablaze. Together they make one of the simplest, most rewarding sunset stops in the park.





Day 5 — Bryce Canyon National Park: Amphitheater of Hoodoos

Nothing in the previous four days quite prepares you for Bryce Canyon. Not a canyon at all but a series of natural amphitheaters carved into the edge of the Paunsaugunt Plateau, Bryce holds the largest concentration of hoodoos — narrow spires of banded orange and white rock — on Earth. At over 8,000 feet elevation, the air is noticeably cooler and thinner than the desert floor below, and the crowded forest of stone spires glows nearly fluorescent at sunrise.


1


Less crowded than the park's main overlooks, Fairyland Point offers a sweeping first view of the amphitheater and is a superb, quieter spot to watch sunrise ignite the hoodoos below. It also marks the trailhead for the Fairyland Loop, an 8-mile trail that descends directly into the formations for those wanting a longer, more immersive hike.



2


Widely regarded as the best short hike in the American national park system, this combined 2.9-mile loop descends into the amphitheater through Queens Garden's whimsical spires and returns via Navajo Loop's dramatic switchbacks between towering walls, including the famous slot known as Wall Street. Doing the loop counter-clockwise eases the climb back to the rim.



3


The Rim Trail connects the park's main overlooks — Sunrise, Sunset, and Inspiration Points — with an easy, mostly flat walk offering constant views into the amphitheater. Those with energy to spare can extend the day by descending into the Peekaboo Loop, a 5.5-mile trail that winds among the hoodoos at eye level, a genuinely different perspective than any rim view provides.



4


At the southern end of the park, this easy 1-mile loop winds through a forest of ancient bristlecone pines, some of the oldest living organisms on Earth, twisted by centuries of wind at the plateau's highest elevations. It's a shaded, peaceful contrast to the exposed hoodoo trails earlier in the day.



5


At the end of the park road, Rainbow Point and adjacent Yovimpa Point sit at over 9,100 feet, the highest overlooks in the park, with views stretching across three states on a clear day. It's a fitting final stop before the drive south toward Zion.





Day 6 — Zion National Park: Zion Canyon

The final destination is also the most visited national park in Utah, and for good reason. Zion Canyon is a half-mile-deep gorge of sheer Navajo sandstone cliffs carved by the Virgin River, with hanging gardens, seasonal waterfalls, and some of the most celebrated — and most exposed — hiking trails in the country. Today follows the canyon floor via the shuttle road, building toward the park's signature adrenaline hike in the afternoon.


1


A short stop near the canyon entrance, the museum offers context on the human and geologic history of Zion Canyon, along with one of the best unobstructed views of the canyon's entrance cliffs from its back porch. It's a useful orientation point before boarding the shuttle deeper into the canyon.



2


A network of trails leads to a series of pools and seasonal waterfalls fed by springs seeping from the canyon wall, passing beneath dripping alcoves hung with ferns and wildflowers. The lower pool is an easy walk suitable for nearly everyone, while the upper pool adds elevation gain and a more dramatic waterfall.



3


The most famous — and most demanding — trail in Zion climbs a series of steep switchbacks before narrowing to a knife-edge ridge with chain handholds and thousand-foot drops on either side. A permit is required for the final half-mile, but those who complete the 5-mile round-trip are rewarded with an unmatched view straight down Zion Canyon.



4


At the end of the shuttle road, the paved Riverside Walk follows the Virgin River for one easy mile to the mouth of the Zion Narrows, where the canyon walls close to as little as twenty feet apart and the river itself becomes the trail. Wading into the Narrows, even a short distance, is a quintessential Zion experience best done with water shoes and a walking stick.



5


Close the day with this moderate 3.3-mile round-trip trail near the visitor center, climbing to a bench with a sweeping view of the Watchman formation and the town of Springdale below. It's less crowded than the canyon's shuttle-stop trails and one of the best sunset vantage points in the park.





Day 7 — Zion National Park: Canyon Overlook & Kolob Canyons

Your final day trades the crowded canyon floor for Zion's quieter corners — the switchbacking highway east of the canyon and the park's remote, little-visited northwest section. Both offer a different, wider-angle perspective on the same Navajo sandstone that defined the week, a fitting way to close a journey through Utah's five great parks.


1


Reached from a small pullout just east of the Zion–Mount Carmel Tunnel, this 1-mile round-trip trail climbs through a narrow slot and along a cliffside ledge to a viewpoint overlooking the west end of Zion Canyon, including Pine Creek Canyon and the Towers of the Virgin. It packs an outsized view into a short, moderate hike.



2


Visible from the highway near the park's east entrance, this sandstone dome displays a striking checkerboard pattern formed by intersecting sets of erosional lines — vertical cracks from freeze-thaw cycles crossing horizontal bands left by ancient wind-blown dunes. No hike is required; it's an easy roadside stop worth slowing down for.



3


A separate entrance off Interstate 15 leads into Kolob Canyons, a set of narrow, finger-like red rock canyons that see a fraction of the crowds found in Zion Canyon proper. A 5-mile scenic drive climbs steadily to an overlook with far-reaching views, making it an easy, uncrowded way to see a different face of Zion entirely.



4


For those with energy left for one last long hike, the trail toward Kolob Arch leads through a quiet, forested canyon to one of the longest natural arch spans on Earth, rivaling Landscape Arch back in Arches National Park. At roughly 14 miles round-trip it's a full-day undertaking, best suited to travelers extending their stay, and a fitting bookend to a week that began beneath sandstone arches four hundred miles to the north.
