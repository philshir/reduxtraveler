Cruz Bay, St. John, USVI

St. John: Hiking, Snorkeling, and Plantation Ruins in the Heart of Virgin Islands National Park

3 Days

St. John is the smallest of the three main U.S. Virgin Islands, and about two-thirds of it is protected as Virgin Islands National Park. The island has no airport or cruise port; visitors arrive by a short ferry ride from Red Hook on St. Thomas, which keeps it quiet and unspoiled. Steep forested hills drop to white-sand bays, hiking trails lead to Danish sugar mill ruins and ancient Taíno petroglyphs, and coral reefs lie just offshore. This three-day itinerary explores Cruz Bay and the North Shore beaches on the first day, the south shore and Reef Bay on the second, and the quiet east end on the third. Roads are narrow, steep, and winding, and driving is on the left, so many visitors rent a Jeep or use the open-air safari taxis. A shorter two-day plan is available in the Virgin Islands National Park itinerary.

Day 1

The first day starts in Cruz Bay, the island's lively ferry town, then follows North Shore Road past a string of beautiful beaches. Bring snorkel gear, reef-safe sunscreen, and plenty of water.

1

Arrive by ferry at Cruz Bay and begin at the Virgin Islands National Park Visitor Center, a short walk from the dock. Exhibits introduce the park's natural and cultural history, and rangers can provide maps and current trail and beach conditions. Afterward, stroll Cruz Bay's colorful shops, cafés, and waterfront before renting a vehicle or catching a taxi for the North Shore.

2

The first beach along North Shore Road is Hawksnest Beach, a long crescent of white sand shaded by sea grape trees. The water is calm and clear, with seagrass beds and coral near the shore that are good for snorkeling, and the beach is much quieter than its famous neighbors. Picnic tables, restrooms, and parking are available.

3

Stop at Peace Hill for a short, easy walk to the ruins of an old sugar windmill on a hilltop above the sea. The viewpoint looks across to the British Virgin Islands and the cays of the North Shore, and the breeze here is a welcome break from the heat. It is a nice place for photographs, and for sunset if you are staying on the north side of the island.

4

End the day at Cinnamon Bay, a long, sandy beach backed by shady trees and the ruins of a former plantation. Swim in the calm water, rent a kayak or paddleboard, or follow the Cinnamon Bay Trail inland to the old sugar factory ruins. The beach has restrooms, a snack bar, and equipment rentals.

Day 2

The second day is dedicated to the south shore and the Reef Bay valley, the park's most rewarding hike. The hike is long and steep, so start early, carry plenty of water, and wear sturdy shoes. Many visitors take the ranger-guided hike, which includes a boat ride back to Cruz Bay.

1

Begin along Centerline Road at the Catherineberg Estate Ruins, the remains of a large sugar and cotton plantation. A windmill tower and ruined buildings stand on the hillside, and interpretive signs explain the history of the estate and the lives of the people enslaved here.

2

Follow the Reef Bay Trail, a steep, shaded path of about 2.5 miles that descends roughly 900 feet through tropical forest to the sea. Along the way you pass old plantation walls, giant tropical trees, and the Reef Bay Sugar Mill Ruins, a large, well-preserved steam mill complex set among the vines and trees. It is the most popular hike in the park, and the walk back is uphill if you are not taking the boat.

3

A short side trail leads to the Reef Bay Petroglyphs, a series of carvings in the rocks above a freshwater pool. Taíno people carved the petroglyphs more than 1,000 years ago, and the pool beneath the waterfall is a cool place to rest. The area is protected, so look but do not touch.

4

If you are not taking the boat ride back, drive to Little Lameshur Beach, a secluded, rocky cove on the south coast reached by a bumpy road. The calm water and nearby reef are good for snorkeling, and the shade of the sea grape trees is welcome after the hike. A four-wheel-drive vehicle is recommended for the final stretch of road.

Day 3

The final day explores the quieter east end, where history and snorkeling combine. Roads are narrow, so take your time, and bring snorkel gear and water shoes for the rocky entries.

1

Begin at Annaberg Sugar Plantation, one of the best-preserved sugar plantation ruins in the Caribbean. A windmill tower, boiling house, and stone slave quarters stand on a ridge overlooking Leinster Bay, and signs explain the history of the plantation. Cultural demonstrations are sometimes offered, and the view across to Tortola makes it a worthwhile stop.

2

From the plantation, follow the Leinster Bay Trail, an easy shoreline path of about 0.8 mile each way to Waterlemon Cay. The cay is one of the best snorkeling spots on the island, with a thriving reef, sea turtles, rays, and often starfish in the shallows. Enter the water at the end of the trail and swim around the cay.

3

Back on the road, stop at Francis Bay, a calm, shallow bay behind a salt pond that is good for bird watching and an easy swim. The Francis Bay Trail follows the edge of the pond and is a quiet place to see herons and other wading birds. Picnic tables and restrooms are available.

4

End your trip at the island's southeast tip, at Salt Pond Bay, a sheltered, reef-lined bay with good snorkeling and sea turtles. From here, the Ram Head Trail leads about 1 mile to a dramatic headland with sweeping views of the Caribbean. There is no shade, so hike in the late afternoon.
