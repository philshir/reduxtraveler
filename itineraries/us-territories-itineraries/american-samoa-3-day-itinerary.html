American Samoa: Rainforest, Reef, and the Remote Manu'a Islands

3 Days

The southernmost point in the United States, American Samoa is a scattering of steep, volcanic islands where rainforest tumbles directly into coral reef and Samoan tradition, or fa'a Samoa, still governs daily life more than any colonial import ever has. This 3-day itinerary spends two days on Tutuila, the main island, circling one of the most dramatically vertical harbors in the Pacific and threading through the rainforest and coastal units of American Samoa National Park, before a short flight out to the remote, sparsely populated Manu'a Islands for a final day among some of the least-visited land in the entire National Park System.

Day 1 — Pago Pago & Central Tutuila

Begin at Pago Pago Harbor, a flooded volcanic crater so steep-sided and deep it remains one of the best natural harbors in the South Pacific, ringed by jungle-covered peaks that rise almost directly from the water. The day moves between small museums, a rainforest tramway, and the mountainside village where much of American Samoa National Park's Tutuila unit begins.

1

Pago Pago serves as the territory's capital and main harbor town, its steep, forested crater walls dropping almost straight into the water in one of the most striking natural settings of any port in the Pacific. Nearby, the Jean P. Haydon Museum, housed in a former Navy commissary, traces Samoan history and material culture from early Polynesian settlement through the colonial and territorial periods.

2

Named for a former territorial governor, this small but informative marine education center focuses on the coral reef ecosystems surrounding American Samoa, offering a useful primer on the marine life visitors are likely to encounter while snorkeling around the islands.

3

Rising directly above Pago Pago Harbor, Mount Alava anchors the main rainforest unit of American Samoa National Park. The Mount Alava Adventure Trail climbs steeply through dense jungle to the 1,610-foot summit, rewarded with sweeping views back down over the harbor and out toward the open Pacific — one of the most physically demanding but rewarding hikes in the territory.

4

A short trail near the harbor mouth leads to Blunts Point, where two rusting coastal defense guns from World War II remain in place, aimed out toward the harbor entrance they were built to defend. Nearby Fatumafuti offers a quiet village and rocky shoreline with views back across the water to Rainmaker Mountain.

5

On the island's rugged north coast, the small village of Vatia sits framed between two dramatic volcanic sea stacks, Pola Island and Cockscomb, within another unit of American Samoa National Park. The nearby Tuafauna Trail offers a shorter, family-friendly rainforest walk with coastal views, along with reliable spots for local snacks and cold drinks like Palagi Beach and Sadies by the Sea to close out the day.

Day 2 — Western & Eastern Tutuila

Circle the rest of Tutuila today, from the legend-steeped southwestern coast near the airport to the easternmost harbor where boats depart for the outer islands. Expect a mix of protected coral reef, a beach tied to one of Samoa's most enduring legends, and a short boat trip to a small, dramatic island just offshore.

1

Near the village of Vaitogi, the beach known as Turtle and Shark is tied to one of the most famous legends in Samoan oral tradition, said to be the resting place of a mother and daughter who transformed into the two sea creatures. The adjacent Leala Shoreline Trail follows a rugged coastline of blowholes and lava rock, offering dramatic ocean views along the walk.

2

Set within a collapsed volcanic crater open to the sea, Fagatele Bay is one of the smallest units of the National Marine Sanctuary system and one of the most pristine reef environments in American Samoa, though it's reachable only by a steep hike or boat, protecting it from heavy visitor traffic.

3

This small southwestern village offers a quiet coastal trail down to a rocky, largely undeveloped beach, a low-key stop well off the route most visitors take and typical of Tutuila's south coast villages.

4

At Tutuila's westernmost tip, Cape Taputapu marks a remote, wind-battered point where the island's rainforest meets rugged, undeveloped coastline, offering a genuine sense of isolation just a short drive from the villages of central Tutuila.

5

On the island's eastern side, this popular local beach takes its name from the small entrance fee once charged by the village that maintains it, and offers calm, family-friendly swimming with shaded picnic areas along the sand.

6

From the small boat harbor at Auasi on Tutuila's eastern tip, a short longboat ride crosses to Aunu'u, a tiny volcanic island designated a National Natural Landmark for its rare crater lake, black-sand beaches, and red marsh — a quick but worthwhile detour before returning to the main island for the evening.

Day 3 — Manu'a Islands

Take a short flight out to the remote Manu'a Islands, roughly sixty miles east of Tutuila and home to some of the least-visited land in the entire U.S. National Park System. Life here moves at a distinctly slower pace than even Pago Pago, with a handful of small villages spread across three dramatic, jungle-covered islands ringed by coral reef.

1

Ofu Beach is widely considered one of the most beautiful and least-crowded beaches in the entire National Park System, its white sand and clear, reef-protected water framed by the sheer green peaks of Ofu and Olosega rising directly behind it. Coral reef just offshore offers some of the best snorkeling in American Samoa.

2

Connected to Ofu by a short bridge across a narrow channel, Olosega rises even more steeply from the sea, its volcanic peaks among the most dramatic in the territory. The Siu Point Trail climbs along Olosega's coastline for striking views back across the strait toward Ofu, a quiet, largely undisturbed hike through the American Samoa National Park unit here.

3

The largest and most remote of the Manu'a Islands, Ta'u holds the third unit of American Samoa National Park, protecting a mostly untouched rainforest ecosystem that climbs to Lata Mountain, the highest point in the territory at over 3,100 feet. According to Samoan tradition, Ta'u is considered the birthplace of Polynesia itself, giving this final, remote stop a fitting sense of arriving at the origin point of the culture encountered throughout the trip.
