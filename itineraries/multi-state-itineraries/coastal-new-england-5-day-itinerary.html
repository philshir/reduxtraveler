Coastal New England: Boston, the Cape, Newport & the Maine Coast

7 Days

New England is too much region for any single week — six states, mountain ranges, a 6,000-mile coastline, and centuries of American history all packed into an area smaller than many single Midwestern states. Rather than sprinting inland to Vermont's ski towns and the White Mountains only to double back for the coast, this route commits fully to New England's classic coastal thread: colonial Boston, witch-trial Salem, the sandy curl of Cape Cod, the Gilded Age mansions of Newport, and the working harbors of Maine, ending at the granite cliffs of Acadia National Park. Vermont, New Hampshire's mountains, Connecticut, Providence, and the Berkshires are wonderful but pull in the opposite direction geographically — better suited to a separate inland loop than squeezed into the final days of this one. Each day favors the handful of sights every guidebook agrees are essential, trimmed down from a much longer list of worthy possibilities.


Day 1 — Boston: The Freedom Trail

Boston's compact downtown holds more concentrated Revolutionary War history than almost anywhere else in the country, most of it strung along the 2.5-mile red-brick line of the Freedom Trail. Today follows that line from the Common to the harbor, tracing the events that led to American independence.


1


America's oldest public park has served as common grazing land, a British military camp, and a gathering place for public protest since 1634. At its edge, the gold-domed Massachusetts State House has overlooked the Common since 1798, its dome originally sheathed in copper by Paul Revere's own company.



2


Three signers of the Declaration of Independence — Samuel Adams, John Hancock, and Robert Treat Paine — rest in this small colonial cemetery, alongside Paul Revere and the victims of the Boston Massacre. It sits beside the 1809 Park Street Church, whose 217-foot steeple was once the first landmark sailors spotted approaching Boston Harbor.



3


Known as the "Cradle of Liberty" for the fiery town meetings held on its second floor before the Revolution, Faneuil Hall still functions as a public meeting space today. Its adjoining Quincy Market has been a working marketplace since 1826 and remains one of the liveliest lunch stops in the city.



4


In Boston's North End, the small wooden Paul Revere House, built around 1680, is the oldest surviving structure in downtown Boston and was Revere's home at the time of his midnight ride. A short walk away, Old North Church is where two lanterns were hung in April 1775 to warn "the British are coming" by sea rather than land.



5


Across the harbor in Charlestown, the USS Constitution — nicknamed "Old Ironsides" — is the oldest commissioned warship still afloat in the world, its hull said to have deflected cannonballs during the War of 1812. It's docked at the historic Boston Navy Yard, where an adjoining museum tells the ship's 225-year story.



6


A 221-foot granite obelisk marks the site of the Revolution's first major battle, where colonial militia inflicted heavy losses on British troops despite ultimately losing the hill. Climbing the monument's 294 steps rewards visitors with a wide view over Charlestown and the harbor below.





Day 2 — Boston: Back Bay, Beacon Hill & Cambridge

Beyond the colonial core, Boston's 19th-century neighborhoods and world-class museums reveal a very different city — gaslit brick rowhouses, grand Gilded Age architecture, and one of the great university towns in America just across the Charles River. Today moves from Beacon Hill through Back Bay before crossing into Cambridge.


1


Boston's most photographed neighborhood is a maze of narrow, gaslit streets lined with 19th-century brick rowhouses and purple-paned windows. At its heart, Louisburg Square is a private, gated park ringed by some of the most expensive real estate in the city — a genuine glimpse of old Boston wealth.



2


America's first public botanical garden sits just across from the Common, famous for its swan boats gliding across a small lagoon and the bronze Make Way for Ducklings statues honoring the beloved children's book. It's one of the most manicured green spaces in the city.



3


The heart of Back Bay, Copley Square is framed by Trinity Church — a Romanesque masterpiece considered one of the most important buildings in American architectural history — reflected dramatically in the glass facade of the neighboring skyscraper across the square.



4


One of the largest art museums in the country, the MFA holds an encyclopedic collection spanning ancient Egypt to contemporary art. Next door, the Gardner Museum recreates a Venetian palazzo around a plant-filled courtyard, its walls still hung with empty frames marking the spot of paintings stolen in an unsolved 1990 heist.



5


Home to the Red Sox since 1912, Fenway is the oldest ballpark still in use in Major League Baseball, famous for the 37-foot left field wall known as the Green Monster. Guided tours run year-round even on non-game days, covering the park's century of history.



6


A short ride across the Charles River, Harvard Yard is the historic core of America's oldest university, its brick dormitories and the statue of "Three Lies" — the John Harvard Statue — drawing visitors year-round. The surrounding Harvard Square blends bookshops, cafes, and street performers into one of the most walkable college-town centers in the country.





Day 3 — Salem: Witch Trials & Maritime History

Thirty minutes north of Boston, Salem carries a double identity: the site of the infamous 1692 witch trials, and one of colonial America's great seafaring ports, its merchant ships once trading as far as China. Today's short list covers both sides of the city's history.


1


Housed in a former church, this is Salem's most visited attraction, using life-size stage sets and narration to walk visitors through the hysteria of 1692, when twenty people were executed on charges of witchcraft. It's the essential first stop for understanding the events that made the city famous.



2


The only structure still standing in Salem with direct ties to the witch trials, this 1675 house belonged to Judge Jonathan Corwin, who conducted many of the preliminary witchcraft examinations inside its walls. Its steep gabled roof and dark wood beams offer a rare, unaltered look at 17th-century Puritan architecture.



3


America's first National Historic Site preserves Salem's working waterfront as it looked during the city's peak as a global trading port, including a reconstructed 1797 tall ship and the wharves where fortunes in spice, tea, and silk once changed hands. It's a quieter, less crowded counterpoint to the day's witch-trial history.





Day 4 — Plymouth & Cape Cod

South of Boston, the route passes through Plymouth, where the Pilgrims first established their colony in 1620, before continuing onto the curled sandy arm of Cape Cod and out to land's end at Provincetown. It's a day that moves from the earliest chapter of colonial America to the beach towns that have drawn New Englanders every summer since.


1


A modest granite boulder sheltered beneath a classical portico, Plymouth Rock marks the traditional landing spot of the Pilgrims in 1620 — its symbolic weight far exceeding its physical size. Docked nearby, the Mayflower II is a full-scale, seaworthy reproduction of the original ship, offering a vivid sense of just how small and cramped the 66-day crossing really was.



2


The oldest continuously operating public museum in the United States, Pilgrim Hall houses genuine 17th-century artifacts owned by the Mayflower passengers themselves — furniture, weapons, and personal belongings that survived the voyage, giving Plymouth's story a tangible weight beyond the Rock alone.



3


Protecting 40 miles of the Cape's outer beaches, this National Park Service unit preserves the wide sand dunes, salt marshes, and lighthouses that have defined Cape Cod's outer arm since long before it became a summer destination. It's the best stretch of coastline to stop and simply walk the beach on the drive out to Provincetown.



4


At the very tip of Cape Cod, Provincetown was actually the Pilgrims' first landing point before they crossed the bay to Plymouth, a history commemorated by the 252-foot Pilgrim Monument towering over town. Today it's better known for its lively arts scene and Commercial Street, a mile of galleries, shops, and restaurants that makes for a fitting, colorful close to the day.





Day 5 — Newport, Rhode Island: Gilded Age Mansions

A drive west brings the trip to Newport, a small harbor city that became the summer playground of America's wealthiest families in the late 1800s. The "cottages" they built along the cliffs — modest only by their own standards — remain some of the most extravagant private homes ever constructed in America.


1


The grandest of Newport's mansions, this 70-room Italian Renaissance–style palace was built in 1895 as the summer home of railroad magnate Cornelius Vanderbilt II. Its gilded ballrooms and ocean-facing terraces represent the absolute peak of Gilded Age excess and remain Newport's single most visited site.



2


Marble House was built in 1892 using more marble than the Palace of Versailles, a gift from William K. Vanderbilt to his wife Alva, who later used its grounds to host suffrage rallies. Nearby, The Elms recreates an 18th-century French chateau and is known for the below-stairs servant tours that reveal how the mansion actually operated.



3


This 3.5-mile public trail runs along the rocky Atlantic shoreline directly behind the mansions, offering free views of the estates' ocean-facing facades that even non-ticket-holders can enjoy. It's one of the best walks in New England, tracing the exact coastline the Vanderbilts and Astors once looked out upon.



4


Newport's colonial downtown predates the mansions by more than a century, with cobblestone streets and Colony House among its landmarks. Touro Synagogue, dedicated in 1763, is the oldest surviving synagogue building in America and a National Historic Site, reflecting Newport's early history of religious tolerance.





Day 6 — Portland & the Maine Coast

The drive north into Maine passes through Kennebunkport before reaching Portland, a small working harbor city that has become one of New England's best food towns without losing its lobster-boat waterfront. Today's stops trace that same balance between old maritime Maine and its modern revival.


1


This picturesque harbor village has long been a favorite summer retreat of the Bush family, whose Walker's Point estate sits on a rocky point visible from the shoreline. Dock Square's shops and the working lobster boats in the harbor make it a classic stop on the drive up the Maine coast.



2


Commissioned by George Washington in 1791, Portland Head Light is Maine's oldest lighthouse and among the most photographed in the country, its white tower rising from a dramatic rocky point at Fort Williams Park. It's an essential stop just south of downtown Portland.



3


Portland's historic waterfront district mixes 19th-century brick warehouses, cobblestone streets, and one of the best restaurant scenes in New England, all built around a still-working commercial fishing harbor. It's the natural place to spend an afternoon wandering and a late lunch of fresh Maine seafood.



4


Maine's largest and oldest public art institution holds a strong collection of works by artists who painted the state's coastline directly, including Winslow Homer, whose nearby studio is preserved as part of the museum's campus — a fitting close to a day spent tracing that same shoreline.





Day 7 — Acadia National Park & Bar Harbor

The trip's final leg pushes three hours further up the coast to Mount Desert Island, where granite peaks rise directly from the Atlantic inside Acadia National Park — the only national park in the Northeast and one of the smallest, most concentrated in the entire system. It's a fitting finale: the same rocky New England coastline that began in Boston Harbor, distilled to its wildest form.


1


At 1,530 feet, Cadillac Mountain is the tallest peak on the U.S. Atlantic coast and, for part of the year, the first place in the country to see the sunrise. A paved summit road makes the panoramic view over Frenchman Bay and the surrounding islands accessible without a hike, though a reservation is required during peak season.



2


This 27-mile scenic drive strings together nearly all of the park's coastal highlights, including Thunder Hole, a narrow ocean inlet that produces a booming thunderclap sound and a burst of spray when waves crash in at the right tide — best experienced an hour or two before high tide.



3


Famous for its glassy, crystal-clear water and the view of two rounded granite peaks known as The Bubbles, Jordan Pond is Acadia's most photographed inland spot. The easy 3.3-mile loop trail around its shore, followed by tea and popovers at the historic Jordan Pond House, is a quintessential Acadia afternoon.



4


A rare stretch of actual sand along Maine's mostly rocky coast, Sand Beach sits in a cove framed by granite headlands, though the water stays cold even in summer. A short distance south, Otter Cliff rises 110 feet directly out of the Atlantic, one of the highest coastal headlands on the Eastern Seaboard.



5


This easy, half-mile shoreline walk in downtown Bar Harbor passes grand 19th-century "cottages" and open views across Frenchman Bay to the Porcupine Islands. It's the perfect low-key close to both the day and the trip — the same New England coastline that started in Boston Harbor, now at its farthest and wildest point north.
