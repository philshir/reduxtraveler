Las Vegas & Los Angeles: Neon Lights to the Silver Screen

5 Days

This 5-day journey trades the desert glow of Las Vegas for the sprawling energy of Los Angeles, a city built on reinvention — from the gold-rush optimism of its historic downtown to the manufactured dreams of its film studios and the sun-bleached boardwalks of its Pacific beaches. After a fast-paced day soaking up Vegas's biggest icons, the road turns west across the Mojave Desert into a metropolis where century-old Art Deco landmarks sit blocks from glass concert halls, and a hillside sign has become as globally recognizable as any monument on earth. Because Los Angeles alone could fill a week, this itinerary focuses on its most iconic and geographically clustered attractions — Downtown, Hollywood, the Westside, and the beaches — while leaving more distant stops like Long Beach's Aquarium of the Pacific and Queen Mary for a future trip. Fly into Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas, pick up a rental car, and prepare for a week that swings between the Strip and the Pacific coastline.

Day 1

Your trip begins on the Las Vegas Strip, where a single day is enough to hit the landmarks that define the city's identity. From the iconic welcome sign to a cluster of themed mega-resorts, floral installations, dancing fountains, and a taste of ancient Rome and Paris, today moves at a brisk pace designed to capture the essence of Vegas before the drive west tomorrow. The evening winds down with dinner and a glimpse of the Strip's newest architectural marvel.

1

No trip to Las Vegas is complete without a photo beside this mid-century neon icon, standing at the south end of the Strip since 1959. Its retro starburst design and cheerful red script have become shorthand for the city itself, and an early-morning stop here — before the tour buses arrive — offers a quiet moment with one of the most photographed signs in America.

2

Clustered around a single intersection, this quartet of themed resorts showcases the imaginative excess that built modern Las Vegas. The Luxor's black glass pyramid and towering light beam sit across from Excalibur's turrets, while New York-New York recreates a miniature Manhattan skyline complete with a roller coaster weaving through its towers. MGM Grand, with its bronze lion statue and sprawling casino floor, anchors the corner. Walking this stretch in under an hour offers a crash course in the architectural theatrics that make the Strip unlike anywhere else on earth.

3

The Bellagio is one of Las Vegas's most iconic resorts, inspired by the elegance of an Italian lakeside village. Inside, the Bellagio Conservatory features massive seasonal displays made entirely from fresh flowers and plants. Outside, the Fountains of Bellagio present choreographed water performances set to music and lights on the resort's eight-acre lake, creating one of the most photographed attractions on the Strip. Time your visit near the top of the hour for a full show.

4

Caesars Palace brings ancient Rome to life through grand columns, statues, and opulent interiors. The resort is home to the Forum Shops, an upscale shopping mall designed like a Roman marketplace with painted sky ceilings that shift from dawn to dusk. Walking through Caesars offers an immersive experience that blends luxury retail, history-inspired décor, and classic Vegas glamour.

5

The Cosmopolitan stands out for its modern, artistic design, with open-air balconies offering prime views of the Bellagio Fountains next door. Just across the street, Paris Las Vegas recreates the charm of the French capital with cobblestone walkways and a half-scale Eiffel Tower that visitors can ride to the top of for panoramic Strip views — a romantic, photogenic pair of stops back to back.

6

Before dinner, swing north to see the Sphere, one of Las Vegas's newest and most technologically advanced landmarks. Its massive LED exterior displays dynamic visuals visible from miles away, and even a quick drive-by after dark is enough to appreciate why it has become the city's most talked-about attraction in years.

7

Close out your one full day in the city with dinner at one of Las Vegas's celebrated restaurants — from Michelin-recommended fine dining to celebrity-chef steakhouses and scenic dining rooms overlooking the Strip. It's a fitting send-off before the long drive to California tomorrow.

Day 2

Today covers the roughly four-hour drive across the Mojave Desert into Los Angeles, arriving by early afternoon in time to explore Downtown LA on foot. This compact historic core packs Art Deco landmarks, a bustling century-old market, and cutting-edge architecture within just a few walkable blocks — a fitting introduction to a city defined by contrast.

1

Opened in 1939 as "the last of the great railway stations" built in America, Union Station blends Spanish Colonial Revival, Mission Revival, and Art Deco styles into a soaring, sunlit terminal still in daily use. Its tiled courtyards, high-beamed ceilings, and leather chairs have made it a favorite filming location for decades, and it remains one of Downtown LA's most striking architectural landmarks.

2

Unassuming from the street, the Bradbury Building's 1893 interior is one of Downtown LA's best-kept secrets: a central atrium filled with natural light, ornate wrought-iron railings, open-cage elevators, and a glass roof, all of which made it an iconic backdrop in films like Blade Runner. A quick visit to the lobby offers a glimpse of one of the city's most photographed interiors.

3

Operating continuously since 1917, this bustling indoor market across from the Bradbury Building houses dozens of food stalls ranging from decades-old Mexican and Chinese counters to newer artisanal vendors. It's one of the best spots in Downtown LA for a casual, varied lunch while soaking up the neighborhood's energy.

4

This contemporary art museum, housed in a striking honeycomb-like concrete-and-glass building, holds one of the most significant collections of postwar and contemporary art on the West Coast, including major works by Jean-Michel Basquiat, Jeff Koons, and Yayoi Kusama. General admission is free, making it an easy add to any Downtown itinerary.

5

Frank Gehry's billowing stainless-steel curves make the Walt Disney Concert Hall one of the most recognizable pieces of architecture in Los Angeles, and home turf to the LA Philharmonic. Even without catching a performance, the building's sculptural exterior and adjacent gardens are worth a slow walk-around, especially as the metal panels catch the late-afternoon sun.

Day 3

Today is dedicated to Hollywood and the hills above it, where the myth-making machinery of the movie industry meets some of the city's best panoramic views. From the Walk of Fame's brass stars to a hillside sign visible for miles, the day builds toward sunset along one of the most famous drives in America.

1

More than 2,700 brass stars embedded in the sidewalk along Hollywood Boulevard honor entertainers from every era, and the walk itself passes street performers, historic theater marquees, and the forecourt of the TCL Chinese Theatre, where the hand- and footprints of movie stars dating back to the 1920s are preserved in concrete. It's touristy, crowded, and utterly essential to a first visit to Hollywood.

2

Part working movie studio and part theme park, Universal Studios Hollywood combines behind-the-scenes tram tours of active film sets with immersive rides based on the Wizarding World of Harry Potter, Jurassic World, and other blockbuster franchises. It's the most complete studio experience in the city, and worth carving out several hours of the day for.

3

Perched on the southern slope of Mount Hollywood, Griffith Observatory offers free public telescopes, planetarium shows, and — most famously — one of the best panoramic views of both the Hollywood Sign and the downtown skyline in the entire city. The surrounding Griffith Park, one of the largest urban parks in the country, laces the hillside with trails for those who want a closer vantage point.

4

The Hollywood Sign's letters, each roughly 45 feet tall, are best photographed from a distance rather than up close, and Runyon Canyon Park offers exactly that: a moderately steep network of trails just above Hollywood Boulevard with sweeping views of both the sign and the city grid below, popular with hikers, dog-walkers, and the occasional celebrity sighting.

5

This winding two-lane road traces the ridgeline of the Santa Monica Mountains, separating the San Fernando Valley from the Los Angeles Basin, and its scattered overlooks offer some of the most cinematic views in the city. Timing a drive here for sunset, as the lights of both valleys begin to flicker on below, is a quintessential LA experience made famous in countless films.

Day 4

Today moves from the museums and shops of Mid-Wilshire and Beverly Hills out to the Pacific coastline, ending with two of Southern California's most iconic beach towns. It's a day that captures LA's full range — high culture, high fashion, and the laid-back beach culture the city exports around the world.

1

The Los Angeles County Museum of Art anchors Museum Row with an encyclopedic collection spanning centuries and continents, along with its famous "Urban Light" installation of vintage street lamps out front. Next door, the La Brea Tar Pits sit atop naturally occurring asphalt seeps that have trapped and preserved Ice Age fossils for tens of thousands of years, with bubbling tar pools still visible on the surface today.

2

Wrapped in a striking ribbon of stainless steel, the Petersen sits directly across from LACMA and houses one of the world's most significant collections of automobiles, from vintage classics to celebrity-owned cars and cutting-edge concept vehicles. Even those without a strong interest in cars tend to find the building's design and rotating exhibits worth the stop.

3

Operating since 1934, the Original Farmers Market is a beloved open-air collection of food stalls, produce vendors, and casual restaurants, connected via a small trolley to The Grove, an upscale outdoor shopping and dining complex with a dancing fountain and frequent live entertainment. Together they make an easy, walkable lunch stop.

4

Rodeo Drive's three palm-lined blocks in Beverly Hills are synonymous with luxury shopping, flagship boutiques, and unabashed people-watching. A short drive away, the Sunset Strip section of Sunset Boulevard threads through West Hollywood past legendary music venues and giant hand-painted billboards, a stretch of road as tied to LA's rock-and-roll history as Hollywood Boulevard is to film.

5

The wide sandy beach and Pacific Park amusement rides atop Santa Monica Pier mark the traditional western end of Route 66, and the pier's Ferris wheel offers sweeping views up and down the coastline. It's an easy, walkable introduction to LA's beach culture before continuing south.

6

A short walk or bike ride south of Santa Monica, Venice Beach is famous for its boardwalk of street performers, muscle-bound weightlifters at the original Muscle Beach, and colorful graffiti-covered canals a few blocks inland. Arriving for sunset rewards visitors with a classic Southern California scene: silhouetted palm trees and skaters against an orange Pacific horizon.

Day 5

Your final morning takes in one last world-class museum on LA's Westside before the roughly four-hour drive back across the desert to Las Vegas. It's a quieter close to the trip, trading crowds for a hilltop garden and sweeping city views before returning the rental car and catching your flight home.

1

Perched on a hilltop in Brentwood and reached by a dedicated tram from the parking garage below, the Getty Museum pairs a world-class collection of European paintings, drawings, and sculpture with dramatic modern architecture and formal gardens overlooking the city and the Pacific beyond. General admission is free, and the sweeping views alone make it worth the visit. Travelers with extra time can instead opt for the Getty Villa in Pacific Palisades, a recreated Roman country house housing the collection's antiquities.

2

The drive back to Las Vegas retraces I-15 across the Mojave Desert, covering roughly four hours depending on traffic through the Cajon Pass and along the way. Return your rental car at Harry Reid International Airport in time for your flight home, closing the loop on a trip that spanned casino floors, film lots, and the Pacific shoreline.
