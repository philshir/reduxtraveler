Las Vegas & the Grand Canyon: From the Strip to the Edge of the Earth

5 Days

This 5-day journey pairs the electric spectacle of the Las Vegas Strip with one of the seven natural wonders of the world: the South Rim of the Grand Canyon. After a fast-paced introduction to Vegas's most iconic resorts, the road turns east across the Arizona high desert toward a mile-deep chasm carved by the Colorado River over six million years. This itinerary focuses entirely on the South Rim's most celebrated overlooks and rim-hugging paths — Desert View Drive, Grand Canyon Village, and the sunset-famous Hermit Road — keeping every stop safely along the canyon's edge rather than descending into it, so the focus stays on the sweeping views rather than strenuous inner-canyon trekking. Fly into Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas, pick up a rental car, and prepare for a trip that moves from neon towers to a canyon so vast it defies a single photograph.

Day 1

Your trip begins on the Las Vegas Strip, where a single day is enough to hit the landmarks that define the city's identity. From the iconic welcome sign to a cluster of themed mega-resorts, floral installations, dancing fountains, and a taste of ancient Rome and Paris, today moves at a brisk pace designed to capture the essence of Vegas before the long drive east tomorrow. The evening winds down with dinner and a glimpse of the Strip's newest architectural marvel.

1

No trip to Las Vegas is complete without a photo beside this mid-century neon icon, standing at the south end of the Strip since 1959. Its retro starburst design and cheerful red script have become shorthand for the city itself, and an early-morning stop here — before the tour buses arrive — offers a quiet moment with one of the most photographed signs in America.

2

Clustered around a single intersection, this quartet of themed resorts showcases the imaginative excess that built modern Las Vegas. The Luxor's black glass pyramid and towering light beam sit across from Excalibur's turrets, while New York-New York recreates a miniature Manhattan skyline complete with a roller coaster weaving through its towers. MGM Grand, with its bronze lion statue and sprawling casino floor, anchors the corner. Walking this stretch in under an hour offers a crash course in the architectural theatrics that make the Strip unlike anywhere else on earth.

3

The Bellagio is one of Las Vegas's most iconic resorts, inspired by the elegance of an Italian lakeside village. Inside, the Bellagio Conservatory features massive seasonal displays made entirely from fresh flowers and plants. Outside, the Fountains of Bellagio present choreographed water performances set to music and lights on the resort's eight-acre lake, creating one of the most photographed attractions on the Strip. Time your visit near the top of the hour for a full show.

4

Caesars Palace brings ancient Rome to life through grand columns, statues, and opulent interiors. The resort is home to the Forum Shops, an upscale shopping mall designed like a Roman marketplace with painted sky ceilings that shift from dawn to dusk. Walking through Caesars offers an immersive experience that blends luxury retail, history-inspired décor, and classic Vegas glamour.

5

The Cosmopolitan stands out for its modern, artistic design, with open-air balconies offering prime views of the Bellagio Fountains next door. Just across the street, Paris Las Vegas recreates the charm of the French capital with cobblestone walkways and a half-scale Eiffel Tower that visitors can ride to the top of for panoramic Strip views — a romantic, photogenic pair of stops back to back.

6

Before dinner, swing north to see the Sphere, one of Las Vegas's newest and most technologically advanced landmarks. Its massive LED exterior displays dynamic visuals visible from miles away, and even a quick drive-by after dark is enough to appreciate why it has become the city's most talked-about attraction in years.

7

Close out your one full day in the city with dinner at one of Las Vegas's celebrated restaurants — from Michelin-recommended fine dining to celebrity-chef steakhouses and scenic dining rooms overlooking the Strip. It's a fitting send-off before the long drive to Arizona tomorrow.

Day 2

Today is largely a travel day, covering the roughly four-and-a-half to five-hour drive from Las Vegas to the Grand Canyon's South Rim via Williams and the park's East Entrance. Rather than driving straight to the main village, this route enters through Desert View, allowing you to work your way west along the canyon rim, stopping at a string of overlooks and a historic watchtower before settling in for the night near Grand Canyon Village.

1

Designed by architect Mary Colter in 1932 and modeled after ancestral Puebloan towers of the Southwest, this 70-foot stone structure marks the eastern edge of the South Rim and offers one of the park's most sweeping panoramas, taking in the Colorado River as it bends through the canyon far below. Inside, hand-painted murals by Hopi artist Fred Kabotie add cultural depth to the view from the observation deck above.

2

A short drive west of Desert View, Lipan Point offers one of the widest unobstructed views of the Colorado River along the entire South Rim, along with a clear look at the canyon's layered rock strata, some dating back nearly two billion years. It's a favorite among photographers for its unusually broad, uncluttered vista.

3

This small museum sits beside the excavated remains of an 800-year-old ancestral Puebloan village, with a short, flat interpretive loop trail winding past the original stone foundations. It's a quiet, easy stop that adds historical context to a landscape often viewed only through a geological lens.

4

Named for painter Thomas Moran, whose sweeping canvases of the canyon helped convince Congress to protect it, this overlook captures the same dramatic interplay of light and layered rock that inspired his work — a fitting reminder of how deeply this landscape has shaped American art.

5

One of the highest points on the South Rim, Grandview Point offers a commanding view over a maze of buttes and side canyons, with the isolated peak of Horseshoe Mesa visible below. Historically the site of a copper-mining operation and one of the canyon's earliest tourist hotels, it's a fitting final stop before settling into Grand Canyon Village for the night.

Day 3

Today centers on Grand Canyon Village, the historic heart of the South Rim, where a paved rim-hugging path connects some of the park's most iconic overlooks with a cluster of early-20th-century lodges and landmarks. Every stop today stays along the rim itself, offering full appreciation of the canyon's scale without any descent into it.

1

Just steps from the main visitor center, Mather Point delivers most first-time visitors' introduction to the Grand Canyon — and it rarely disappoints. Perched on a promontory jutting out over the void, it offers a dramatic, wide-open first look at the canyon's scale, making it one of the most heavily visited yet consistently awe-inspiring overlooks on the South Rim.

2

A short walk along the Rim Trail from Mather Point, Yavapai Point houses a small geology museum with floor-to-ceiling windows framing the canyon directly behind exhibit displays. It's widely considered one of the best spots on the South Rim to understand how the canyon's rock layers formed over nearly two billion years, without leaving the rim itself.

3

The paved, mostly flat Rim Trail runs for roughly 13 miles along the canyon's edge, and the stretch near the village incorporates the Trail of Time, an outdoor geologic timeline where each meter walked represents a million years of the canyon's formation, marked by rock samples pulled from the corresponding layer below. It's an easy, family-friendly way to cover significant ground and multiple viewpoints without ever leaving the rim.

4

Rather than descending the Bright Angel Trail itself, this stop takes in the trailhead area and the adjacent Lookout Studio, a stone building designed to blend into the rim's edge with an observation deck offering one of the best views of the trail's first switchbacks winding down into the upper canyon.

5

Built in 1905 as a grand log-and-stone railway hotel, the El Tovar remains one of the National Park System's most historic lodges and a fitting spot for dinner with a canyon view. Nearby, Kolb Studio — once the home and photography business of the pioneering Kolb brothers, perched dramatically on the rim's edge — now houses a small gallery and bookstore worth a quick stop.

Day 4

Today follows Hermit Road, an eight-mile stretch of the South Rim west of the village widely regarded as the park's premier scenic drive, accessible by free shuttle for most of the year. The overlooks along this route are famous for their sunset views, so today's plan builds toward an evening finale at the road's namesake endpoint.

1

The first stop along Hermit Road offers a sweeping look back at Grand Canyon Village and the Bright Angel Trail's switchbacks tracing down into the canyon, along with a clear view west toward the buttes and temples that punctuate this stretch of the rim.

2

Jutting further out into the canyon than most overlooks along Hermit Road, Hopi Point offers one of the widest fields of view on the South Rim and is frequently cited as the single best sunset spot in the entire park, with unobstructed sightlines in nearly every direction.

3

A short distance from Hopi Point, Mohave Point offers glimpses of the Colorado River itself along with several distinctive rock formations, including the Vishnu Temple. It's a quieter alternative to its more crowded neighbor while still delivering exceptional views.

4

This overlook is named for Major John Wesley Powell, the one-armed Civil War veteran who led the first documented expedition down the Colorado River through the canyon in 1869. A stone memorial here commemorates his journey, and the view captures the same rugged terrain his party navigated more than 150 years ago.

5

The end of the road and the day's finale, Hermits Rest is another Mary Colter design — a rustic stone building built in 1914 to resemble a hermit's hand-built shelter, complete with an oversized stone fireplace inside. The overlook here offers a fittingly dramatic backdrop for watching the sun set over the western canyon, closing out your final full day at the Grand Canyon.

Day 5

Your final morning begins with sunrise at one of the South Rim's most celebrated viewpoints before the roughly four-and-a-half to five-hour drive back to Las Vegas. It's a fitting bookend to the trip — one last look into the canyon's depths before returning to the desert and catching your flight home.

1

Accessible only by shuttle or on foot, Yaki Point sees far fewer crowds than the overlooks near the village while offering one of the best sunrise views on the entire South Rim, with the South Kaibab Trailhead visible nearby tracing its first steps down along a narrow ridge. Arriving before dawn rewards early risers with a canyon that seems to glow from within as the light first hits its walls.

2

Before leaving the park, a final stop at the main visitor center near Mather Point offers exhibits on the canyon's geology and history, ranger-led programs, and one last chance to take in the view from a nearby overlook before the long drive west.

3

The drive back to Las Vegas retraces the route through Williams and across the Arizona-Nevada desert, covering roughly four and a half to five hours. Return your rental car at Harry Reid International Airport in time for your flight home, closing the loop on a trip that spanned neon towers and one of the most awe-inspiring landscapes on the planet.
