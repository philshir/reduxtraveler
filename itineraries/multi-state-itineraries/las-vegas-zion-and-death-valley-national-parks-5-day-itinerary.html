Las Vegas Zion And Death Valley National Parks 5 Day Itinerary

Las Vegas, Zion & Death Valley: Neon Lights to the Lowest Point in North America

5 Days

This 5-day journey pairs the glitter of the Las Vegas Strip with two of the Southwest's most extreme landscapes: the towering red walls of Zion National Park and the scorched, sea-level-shattering basin of Death Valley. In the span of less than a week, you'll walk beneath a half-scale Eiffel Tower, stand 282 feet below sea level on a floor of cracked salt, watch sunset paint a canyon of hoodoos and badlands in shifting color, and trace a river-carved gorge that has drawn adventurers for over a century. Because a single trip cannot do justice to every corner of two national parks and a world capital of entertainment, this itinerary has been distilled to the most iconic and accessible stops in each location. Fly into Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas, pick up a rental car, and prepare for a week that swings from casino floors to the hottest, driest, lowest terrain on the continent.

Day 1

Your trip begins on the Las Vegas Strip, where a single day is enough to hit the landmarks that define the city's identity. From the iconic welcome sign to a cluster of themed mega-resorts, floral installations, dancing fountains, and a taste of ancient Rome and Paris, today moves at a brisk pace designed to capture the essence of Vegas before you head into the desert tomorrow. The evening winds down with dinner and a glimpse of the Strip's newest architectural marvel.

1

No trip to Las Vegas is complete without a photo beside this mid-century neon icon, standing at the south end of the Strip since 1959. Its retro starburst design and cheerful red script have become shorthand for the city itself, and an early-morning stop here — before the tour buses arrive — offers a quiet moment with one of the most photographed signs in America.

2

Clustered around a single intersection, this quartet of themed resorts showcases the imaginative excess that built modern Las Vegas. The Luxor's black glass pyramid and towering light beam sit across from Excalibur's turrets, while New York-New York recreates a miniature Manhattan skyline complete with a roller coaster weaving through its towers. MGM Grand, with its bronze lion statue and sprawling casino floor, anchors the corner. Walking this stretch in under an hour offers a crash course in the architectural theatrics that make the Strip unlike anywhere else on earth.

3

The Bellagio is one of Las Vegas's most iconic resorts, inspired by the elegance of an Italian lakeside village. Inside, the Bellagio Conservatory features massive seasonal displays made entirely from fresh flowers and plants. Outside, the Fountains of Bellagio present choreographed water performances set to music and lights on the resort's eight-acre lake, creating one of the most photographed attractions on the Strip. Time your visit near the top of the hour for a full show.

4

Caesars Palace brings ancient Rome to life through grand columns, statues, and opulent interiors. The resort is home to the Forum Shops, an upscale shopping mall designed like a Roman marketplace with painted sky ceilings that shift from dawn to dusk. Walking through Caesars offers an immersive experience that blends luxury retail, history-inspired décor, and classic Vegas glamour.

5

The Cosmopolitan stands out for its modern, artistic design, with open-air balconies offering prime views of the Bellagio Fountains next door. Just across the street, Paris Las Vegas recreates the charm of the French capital with cobblestone walkways and a half-scale Eiffel Tower that visitors can ride to the top of for panoramic Strip views — a romantic, photogenic pair of stops back to back.

6

Before dinner, swing north to see the Sphere, one of Las Vegas's newest and most technologically advanced landmarks. Its massive LED exterior displays dynamic visuals visible from miles away, and even a quick drive-by after dark is enough to appreciate why it has become the city's most talked-about attraction in years.

7

Close out your one full day in the city with dinner at one of Las Vegas's celebrated restaurants — from Michelin-recommended fine dining to celebrity-chef steakhouses and scenic dining rooms overlooking the Strip. It's a fitting send-off before an early departure into the desert tomorrow.

Day 2

Leave the Strip behind this morning for a roughly two-hour drive west into Death Valley National Park, the hottest and driest place in North America. Today's route covers the park's central corridor around Furnace Creek, where salt flats, painted hillsides, and colorful badlands sit within a short drive of one another. Pack far more water than you think you'll need, start early to beat the heat, and prepare for scenery that looks more like another planet than anywhere else in the country.

1

A short paved path from the parking area leads to one of Death Valley's most photographed overlooks, where golden badlands ripple below in waves of gold, tan, and rust. The eroded hills here were once the bed of an ancient lake, and sunrise light — when the colors deepen and shadows carve the folds into sharp relief — makes this the ideal first stop of the day.

2

This moderate loop winds through a narrow canyon of golden mudstone before climbing into the badlands below Manly Beacon, a distinctive spire of banded rock. The trail can be extended to Zabriskie Point itself, but even the shorter loop offers an immersive walk through the same eroded formations you admired from above.

3

At 282 feet below sea level, Badwater Basin is the lowest point in North America — a vast expanse of cracked salt flats stretching to the base of the Panamint Mountains. Walking out onto the polygonal salt crust, with a small "sea level" sign visible high on the cliff behind you, offers a visceral sense of just how far below the ocean this desert basin sits.

4

A short drive from Badwater Basin, this jagged expanse of salt crystals has been sculpted by wind and rain into sharp, uneven spires so rugged that, as the National Park Service famously noted, "only the devil could play golf" on its surface. It's a strange, crunching landscape best appreciated on foot from the designated boardwalk area.

5

This scenic one-way drive winds through volcanic and sedimentary hills stained in surprising shades of pink, green, purple, and gold — the result of oxidized metals within the rock. The Artists Palette overlook, roughly midway along the drive, is the payoff: a hillside painted in pastel color unlike anything else in the park.

6

Furnace Creek serves as Death Valley's hub, home to the park's visitor center and the only concentration of dining and lodging within the park itself. Refuel here, check current conditions with rangers, and settle in for the night before tomorrow's push into the park's more remote northern reaches.

Day 3

Today covers Death Valley's northern reaches before a long but scenic drive east toward Zion National Park. From a mile-wide volcanic crater to some of the tallest sand dunes in California, the morning showcases the park's geological range, while the afternoon's roughly four-and-a-half-hour drive carries you from the lowest point on the continent back up into the red-rock highlands of southern Utah.

1

Just north of Furnace Creek, the ruins of this 1880s borax processing plant recall the era of twenty-mule teams hauling ore wagons nearly 165 miles across the desert to the nearest railroad. A short interpretive loop trail winds past rusted equipment and the old refinery foundation, offering a glimpse into the industry that first put Death Valley on the map.

2

This unpaved 2.7-mile loop road winds through a compact maze of golden badlands, named for the mule teams that once hauled borax through the region. Slow, scenic, and passable in most passenger vehicles, it offers an intimate detour through eroded hills without the crowds found at the park's more famous overlooks.

3

A steep drive up to 5,475 feet delivers one of the most dramatic panoramas in the park: Badwater Basin's white salt flats laid out directly below, framed by the Panamint Range across the valley. On clear days, both the highest point in the contiguous United States and the lowest are visible from nearly the same spot — a striking illustration of Death Valley's extremes.

4

Stovepipe Wells, the park's second small outpost, sits beside the Mesquite Flat Sand Dunes, a rippling field of tan dunes rising up to 100 feet that ranks among the most accessible and photogenic dune fields in the National Park System. Early morning or sunset light, when shadows trace the ridgelines, is the best time to wander out among them.

5

Roughly half a mile wide and 600 feet deep, this volcanic crater was formed by a violent steam explosion when rising magma met groundwater a few thousand years ago. A rim trail circles the crater's edge, with colorful bands of red and black volcanic ash visible in its walls — a fittingly dramatic final stop before leaving Death Valley.

6

The drive from Death Valley to Zion covers roughly four and a half hours, passing through Las Vegas and back out into the desert before climbing into southwestern Utah's red-rock country. The landscape transforms along the way — from Death Valley's stark, sea-level basin to Zion's towering sandstone cliffs — making for one of the more dramatic geological transitions of the trip.

Day 4

A full day dedicated to Zion National Park, Utah's oldest and most visited, where the Virgin River has carved a half-mile-deep gorge through Navajo sandstone over millions of years. Today's route moves from the accessible canyon floor to one of the park's most demanding — and most rewarding — trails, closing with sunset from a quiet overlook above the valley.

1

The shuttle-only Zion Canyon Scenic Drive winds between towering rock walls streaked with desert varnish, offering the park's signature introduction to a landscape that feels genuinely otherworldly after two days in the desert basin. Zion Canyon itself, a half-mile-deep gorge sliced by the Virgin River, sets the stage for everything else you'll see today.

2

This paved, easy path follows the Virgin River to the entrance of the Zion Narrows, where the canyon walls close in to less than 30 feet apart in places. Even without wading into the famous slot canyon itself, the Riverside Walk offers a shaded, family-friendly glimpse of the geology that makes the Narrows one of the most sought-after hikes in the American Southwest.

3

A moderate loop leads past a series of pools and seasonal waterfalls tucked beneath sandstone overhangs, connecting to the shaded Grotto Trail along the canyon floor. It's a manageable midday hike that rewards with hanging gardens of ferns and columbine clinging to damp canyon walls.

4

Angels Landing is Zion's most famous — and most feared — trail, climbing switchbacks known as Walter's Wiggles before a final half-mile along a knife-edge ridge secured by chains, ending on a summit with sheer 1,000-foot drops on either side. A permit is required for the final section. Travelers seeking a similar thrill with less exposure often choose the Hidden Canyon Trail instead, which offers its own chain-assisted scramble into a secluded hanging canyon.

5

Close out the day on this moderate 3.3-mile round-trip trail near the South Entrance, which climbs to a bench overlooking the Towers of the Virgin and the town of Springdale below. As the sun drops, the sandstone cliffs blaze orange and red — one of the most rewarding sunset spots in the park.

Day 5

Your final morning offers one more taste of Zion's dramatic scenery before the roughly two-and-a-half-hour drive back to Las Vegas. A shorter, quieter trail and a striking roadside geological curiosity round out the trip before you return the rental car and catch your flight home, carrying with you memories that span from casino floors to salt flats to sandstone cliffs.

1

Just past the Zion-Mount Carmel Tunnel, this short but rewarding trail leads to a viewpoint overlooking lower Zion Canyon without the crowds of the valley floor. It's an ideal way to catch one more sweeping view of the park's signature scenery before hitting the road.

2

Continuing east on the scenic highway, Checkerboard Mesa's cross-hatched sandstone pattern — the result of both wind and water erosion over eons — makes for an easy roadside stop and one of Zion's most photographed geological curiosities.

3

The drive back to Las Vegas covers roughly two and a half hours along I-15, retracing the desert corridor that opened the trip. Return your rental car at Harry Reid International Airport in time for your flight home, closing the loop on a trip that spanned neon towers, the lowest point in North America, and one of the most spectacular canyons in the country.
