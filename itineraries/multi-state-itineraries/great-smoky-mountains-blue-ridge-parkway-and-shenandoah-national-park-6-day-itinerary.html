Great Smoky Mountains Blue Ridge Parkway And Shenandoah National Park 6 Day Itinerary

Smokies to Shenandoah: A Blue Ridge Mountain Odyssey

6 Days

Long before there were roads, the ridges of the southern and central Appalachians were already ancient — worn smooth by more time than the Rockies or the Alps have existed, cloaked in a blue haze of mist that gave these mountains their name. This 6‑day journey traces that spine of high country from the mountain‑resort energy of Gatlinburg, through the deep coves and thundering waterfalls of the Great Smoky Mountains, along the unhurried curves of the Blue Ridge Parkway, and up onto the open, wildflower‑studded ridgeline of Shenandoah National Park. Along the way you'll ride a chairlift into the clouds, stand on the highest point in Tennessee, walk behind a waterfall, drive an 11‑mile loop where black bears outnumber cars, and scramble hand‑over‑hand to a rocky summit with a 360‑degree view of forever. Grouped by geography rather than by list order, this itinerary is built to minimize backtracking and maximize the two things these mountains do best: scenery and quiet.

Day 1

Your journey begins in Gatlinburg, the lively mountain town that has served as the front door to the Great Smoky Mountains for nearly a century. Tucked into a narrow valley with the park's peaks rising directly behind Main Street, Gatlinburg is part alpine village, part Southern boardwalk — a place where fudge shops, moonshine distilleries, and salt‑water taffy sit beneath genuinely spectacular mountain views. Today is about getting your bearings and your first taste of elevation, with two rides that lift you straight out of the valley and into the clouds, followed by an evening in neighboring Pigeon Forge, the region's larger and even more exuberant sister city.

1

Wedged into a bend of the Little Pigeon River at the very entrance to the national park, Gatlinburg has been welcoming mountain travelers since the 1920s, when it was little more than a scattering of farmsteads at the end of a dirt road. Today its downtown strip is a dense, walkable stretch of candy kitchens, art galleries, and Southern comfort food, all backed by a wall of green ridgeline that reminds you exactly why everyone is here. Spend your first afternoon strolling the Parkway, riding the Gatlinburg Space Needle for an orientation view of the valley, and settling into the rhythm of a town built entirely around its mountains.

2

A chairlift ride up Crockett Mountain delivers one of the most photographed views in the Smokies: downtown Gatlinburg laid out below like a model railroad, with the park's peaks stacked in receding shades of blue behind it. At the top, the SkyBridge — North America's longest pedestrian suspension bridge — stretches 680 feet across the mountainside with a glass-paneled section underfoot for those brave enough to look straight down. It's touristy in the best sense: quick, scenic, and a genuinely thrilling way to see the lay of the land before heading into the backcountry.

3

Ride the aerial tramway from the edge of downtown up to Ober Gatlinburg, a mountaintop resort that shifts with the seasons — skiing and snow tubing in winter, an alpine slide, black bear habitat, and amusement rides in the warmer months. Even without doing a single activity, the tram ride alone is worth it, gliding nearly two miles up and over the treetops with the whole valley opening up beneath you. It's a favorite for families splitting time between the wilder trails of the park and something a little more relaxed.

4

A short drive north, Pigeon Forge trades Gatlinburg's mountain‑village charm for full‑blown Smoky Mountain spectacle: Dollywood, dinner theaters, go‑kart tracks, and a skyline of oversized attractions strung along the Parkway. It's the region's entertainment hub, and an easy place to spend an evening with dinner and a show before an early start into the park tomorrow. Even if theme parks aren't your priority, Pigeon Forge is worth passing through simply to see how thoroughly these mountains have shaped — and been shaped by — American road‑trip culture.

Day 2

Today you leave the town behind and enter the Great Smoky Mountains National Park proper, focusing on the cluster of waterfalls and historic trails closest to Gatlinburg on the slopes of Mount LeConte. This corner of the park earns its nickname — the Smokies are named for the perpetual, ethereal mist that rises off the dense forest canopy — and nowhere is that more visible than along the narrow, winding Roaring Fork Motor Nature Trail. Expect old-growth hemlocks, rushing mountain streams, and at least one waterfall you can walk behind.

1

This 5.5‑mile, one‑way loop road climbs out of Gatlinburg and into a dense, junglelike cove forest, following the tumbling Roaring Fork stream past preserved log cabins, an old grist mill, and some of the tallest trees in the park. Closed to trailers and RVs and capped at a leisurely pace, it's built for stopping — pull off wherever the water looks good or a historic homestead catches your eye. Several of the day's best trailheads, including Grotto Falls, branch directly off this road, making it the natural spine of the morning.

2

A moderate 2.6‑mile round‑trip hike through a cool, shaded hemlock forest leads to the one waterfall in the entire park you can walk directly behind. Grotto Falls drops 25 feet over a rock overhang, and the trail continues on as Trillium Gap Trail all the way to the summit of Mount LeConte — keep an eye out for the llama trains that still pack supplies up to LeConte Lodge several times a week, a genuinely rare sight on any hiking trail in the country.

3

Named for the mist‑born rainbows that appear in its spray on sunny afternoons, Rainbow Falls is the tallest single‑drop waterfall in the Smokies at roughly 80 feet. The hike in is a steady, rocky 5.4‑mile round trip along Le Conte Creek — more demanding than Grotto Falls, but rewarded with a much bigger payoff at the end. For those with energy to spare, the trail continues upward toward the summit of Mount LeConte itself.

4

Tucked into the quieter Greenbrier section of the park, this strenuous 8‑mile round‑trip hike passes through one of the last stands of old‑growth forest in the eastern United States before arriving at Ramsey Cascades, the tallest waterfall in the Smokies at about 100 feet, tumbling over a jumble of car‑sized boulders. It's a serious afternoon commitment with real elevation gain, best suited to those with hiking boots already broken in — but the combination of ancient trees and a genuinely dramatic waterfall makes it one of the park's most rewarding trails for anyone willing to earn it.

Day 3

Today is the Smokies' signature drive: up and over Newfound Gap Road, the only paved route that crosses the spine of the park, followed by an afternoon in Cades Cove, the broad mountain valley that is both the park's most popular destination and its best place to spot wildlife. Along the way you'll pass easy family waterfalls, climb to the literal highest point in Tennessee, and choose between two of the Smokies' most iconic — and most different — trails.

1

One of the most popular trails in the entire national park system, the paved 2.6‑mile round trip to Laurel Falls is an easy, family‑friendly walk to an 80‑foot cascading waterfall split by a footbridge, where the trail actually crosses right in front of the falls. Its accessibility is exactly why it draws crowds — arrive at or before sunrise for anything close to solitude — but the reward‑to‑effort ratio is hard to beat anywhere else in the park.

2

Two easy, low‑commitment stops near Sugarlands Visitor Center round out the morning. The Sinks is a roadside waterfall and swirling swimming hole on the Little River — legend has it a logging company dynamited the river here in the 1900s trying to clear a log jam, and instead created this dramatic plunge pool. Cataract Falls, by contrast, is a short, nearly flat half‑mile stroll from the visitor center itself, making it one of the easiest waterfalls in the entire park to reach.

3

At 5,046 feet, Newfound Gap marks the lowest drivable pass across the main crest of the Smokies and the border between Tennessee and North Carolina — you can literally stand with one foot in each state. It's also the site of the Rockefeller Memorial, where President Franklin D. Roosevelt formally dedicated the national park in 1940. The panoramic overlook here, with layered ridgelines fading into blue haze, is one of the most iconic views in the entire Appalachian range.

4

A short but steep 7‑mile spur road off Newfound Gap leads to Clingmans Dome, the highest point in the Great Smoky Mountains, the highest point in Tennessee, and the highest point along the entire 2,190‑mile Appalachian Trail at 6,643 feet. A half‑mile paved (and genuinely steep) path climbs to a striking, UFO‑shaped observation tower where, on a clear day, visibility stretches over 100 miles across seven states. Even on a hazier day, standing above the cloud line here is an experience unlike anywhere else in the park.

5

Two of the most famous trailheads in the park sit along Newfound Gap Road, and most visitors pick one rather than both. Chimney Tops offers a steep, rocky climb to twin rock pinnacles with sweeping views back down into the valley. Alum Cave Trail, slightly longer, passes through the narrow sandstone corridor of Arch Rock and beneath the massive overhanging bluff of Alum Cave before continuing on toward Mount LeConte — a favorite for hikers who want dramatic geology as much as a summit view.

6

No visit to the Smokies is complete without Cades Cove, an 11‑mile, one‑way loop road circling a broad, pastoral valley ringed by mountains, dotted with restored 19th‑century churches, cabins, and a working gristmill. It's the single most visited destination in the park and, not coincidentally, the best place to spot white‑tailed deer, wild turkeys, and black bears grazing right along the road at dawn and dusk. For those with time and energy left, the moderate 5‑mile round‑trip Abrams Falls Trail branches off the loop to a wide, powerful 20‑foot waterfall pouring into a deep swimming hole — plan for a full afternoon here, as the loop road alone can take two to four hours in peak season traffic.

Day 4

Crossing back over Newfound Gap into North Carolina, today shifts the trip's center of gravity — from Tennessee's resort towns to the quieter, Cherokee‑influenced valleys on the park's southern edge, and then onto the northern tip of the Blue Ridge Parkway, the 469‑mile ridgeline drive that will carry you toward Virginia over the next two days. Expect a slower pace: historic buildings, a roadside waterfall on tribal land, and mile after mile of unhurried, overlook‑studded parkway.

1

On the North Carolina side of Newfound Gap Road, the Oconaluftee Visitor Center sits beside the Mountain Farm Museum, a collection of authentic 19th‑century log structures — a farmhouse, barn, smokehouse, and springhouse — relocated here to preserve a picture of Appalachian mountain life before the park existed. The broad river valley outside is also one of the most reliable places in the park to spot elk, reintroduced here in the early 2000s and now grazing the open fields most mornings and evenings.

2

A short walk from Oconaluftee, Mingus Mill is a still-operating, water-powered gristmill built in 1886, using a wooden flume and turbine rather than a traditional wheel to grind corn into cornmeal. It's a quick, easy stop, but a genuinely interesting glimpse into the mountain economy this whole region ran on for over a century before tourism took over.

3

A short drive south onto the Qualla Boundary, the sovereign land of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, brings you to Mingo Falls — at roughly 120 feet, one of the tallest and most dramatic waterfalls in the entire southern Appalachians. A steep but short quarter‑mile climb of stairs leads to a footbridge with a face‑on view of the falls crashing down a rock face. It's a fitting way to close out your time in Cherokee country before turning north.

4

Just north of Cherokee, the Blue Ridge Parkway begins its 469‑mile run toward Shenandoah, and this first stretch is among its most scenic — climbing immediately to elevations over 5,000 feet, threading through the Balsam Mountains, and crossing the dramatic Linn Cove Viaduct as it wraps around Grandfather Mountain. With no commercial traffic and a leisurely 45‑mph speed limit, this is a drive meant to be taken slowly, with frequent stops at overlooks where the layered ridgelines really do turn the namesake shade of blue.

Day 5

A dedicated driving day, but not a wasted one — the Blue Ridge Parkway is designed entirely around its views, and today covers the long middle stretch through Virginia, from the mountains above Asheville up past Roanoke and on toward the parkway's northern terminus at Rockfish Gap, the doorstep of Shenandoah National Park. Pace yourself, take the overlooks you're drawn to, and let the drive itself be the destination.

1

This central section of the parkway rolls through some of its highest terrain, past working mountain farms, split‑rail fences, and rhododendron thickets that turn the roadside pink and purple in late spring. Landmarks along the way include the Peaks of Otter and Roanoke's Mill Mountain overlook, both classic parkway stops with picnic areas and short trails if you want to stretch your legs between long stretches behind the wheel.

2

The final leg of the parkway grows narrower and more pastoral as it approaches its northern end, with long views over the Shenandoah Valley opening up to the west. At Rockfish Gap, the Blue Ridge Parkway officially ends and Skyline Drive begins — a seamless, almost symbolic handoff between two of the most scenic roads in the country. Plan to arrive in the late afternoon so you can settle in near the park entrance before an early start tomorrow.

Day 6

Your final day trades the deep, forested coves of the Smokies for something entirely different: Shenandoah National Park's open, wildflower‑covered ridgeline, strung together by the 105‑mile Skyline Drive with its 75 overlooks onto the Shenandoah Valley to the west and the Piedmont to the east. It's a fitting close to the trip — one last full day of summits, waterfalls, and sweeping views before the journey ends.

1

Skyline Drive runs the entire 105‑mile length of Shenandoah National Park along the crest of the Blue Ridge, and every trailhead in the park today branches directly off it. Built in the 1930s as a Depression‑era public works project, the road itself is a destination — 75 numbered overlooks offer alternating views west into the farmland of the Shenandoah Valley and east across the rolling Piedmont, with black bears, white‑tailed deer, and wild turkeys a common sight along the shoulders at dawn and dusk.

2

At 4,011 feet, Stony Man is the park's second‑highest peak, but it's reached by one of its easiest summit hikes — a 1.6‑mile round trip on the Appalachian Trail with a modest climb. The reward is disproportionate to the effort: an open, rocky summit with a panoramic view often cited as one of the best in Shenandoah, making it an ideal warm‑up for the more demanding hikes later in the day.

3

At 4,051 feet, Hawksbill is the highest point in all of Shenandoah National Park, and the shortest routes to its summit cover under 3 miles round trip with a steady but manageable climb. The payoff is a 360‑degree view unmatched anywhere else in the park, including a clear look back at Old Rag's distinctive profile to the east — on a clear day, it's easy to see why early park planners chose this exact ridge for the drive below.

4

Shenandoah's most famous — and most demanding — hike is a roughly 9‑mile loop that climbs through a mile‑long boulder scramble, using hands as much as feet to navigate rock chutes and squeeze‑throughs before reaching a broad, exposed summit. A day‑use ticket is required in addition to the park entrance fee and should be reserved in advance, as the trail's popularity and safety concerns around overcrowding led the park to cap daily hikers. It's a full‑day commitment best suited to those in solid hiking shape, but it's routinely ranked among the best day hikes on the entire East Coast.

5

The closest waterfall to Skyline Drive, Dark Hollow Falls tumbles 70 feet down a series of rock ledges and is reachable via a short but steep 1.4‑mile round‑trip trail. It's popular for good reason — a genuinely photogenic cascade with a relatively low time commitment — so an early‑morning or late‑afternoon visit helps avoid the midday crowds that build up in summer.

6

Rising above Thornton Gap, Marys Rock is reached by a roughly 3.7‑mile round‑trip hike along the Appalachian Trail to a rocky summit with commanding views over the northern half of Skyline Drive and the valley below. As one of the last stops on the trip, it's a fitting closing image — one more high, open ledge looking back over the same ridgeline that carried you here from Tennessee, six days and hundreds of miles ago.
