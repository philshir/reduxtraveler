Savannah & Charleston: Spanish Moss, Cobblestones, and the Lowcountry Coast

4 Days

This 4-day journey through two of the American South's most storied cities moves between Savannah's oak-canopied squares and Charleston's pastel-colored row houses, tracing more than three centuries of Lowcountry history along the way. From candlelit cemeteries draped in Spanish moss to a Civil War fort still standing sentinel in the harbor, this itinerary blends architecture, history, and coastal scenery in equal measure. Because Charleston alone is home to more historic plantations, churches, and museums than any four-day trip could reasonably cover, this itinerary focuses on each city's most iconic downtown landmarks and its single best outlying excursion, leaving the Ashley River plantations, Angel Oak, and a few of the more distant state parks for a future visit. Fly into Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport, pick up a rental car, and plan to return it in Charleston at the end of the trip, roughly two hours north up the coast.

Day 1

Your journey begins in Savannah's historic district, a meticulously planned 18th-century grid of 22 shaded public squares that remains one of the best-preserved examples of colonial urban design in the country. Today moves on foot between architectural landmarks, historic churches, and a lively riverfront, easing you into the city's slow, moss-draped rhythm.

1

Anchored by an ornate white fountain installed in 1858, Forsyth Park sits at the southern edge of Savannah's historic district and serves as the city's grandest green space. From here, a walk north through the famous Squares of Savannah — each one a small shaded park ringed by historic homes, churches, and monuments — offers the best introduction to the city's distinctive urban design.

2

The twin Gothic spires of the Cathedral Basilica rise above Lafayette Square, its interior filled with stained glass and hand-painted murals restored after a 1898 fire. A short walk away, Congregation Mickve Israel is home to the third-oldest Jewish congregation in America and the only Gothic-style synagogue in the country, with a small museum detailing its 1733 founding by Sephardic settlers.

3

In use from 1750 to 1853, this shaded cemetery holds the remains of early colonists, Revolutionary War soldiers, and yellow fever victims beneath a canopy of live oaks. Weathered headstones — some bearing scars from Union soldiers who camped here during the Civil War — make it one of the most atmospheric stops in the historic district.

4

Housed in a Regency-style mansion, the Telfair Academy holds the South's oldest public art museum, with a collection spanning American Impressionism to European works. A few blocks away, the Owens-Thomas House offers one of the most complete surviving examples of urban slave quarters in the country, presenting an unflinching look at the enslaved labor that built much of Savannah's historic architecture.

5

Topped with a gold-leafed dome visible from much of the riverfront, Savannah City Hall has anchored the foot of Bull Street since 1906. A short walk east along the bluff, Emmet Park offers a quiet, tree-lined stretch overlooking the Savannah River, with monuments to the city's maritime and immigrant history tucked among its paths.

6

Cobblestone River Street runs along the Savannah River beneath restored 19th-century cotton warehouses, now filled with shops, taverns, and outdoor cafes overlooking passing cargo ships. Close out the evening with dinner at the Olde Pink House, an 18th-century mansion turned candlelit restaurant that remains one of Savannah's most beloved dining destinations.

Day 2

Today ventures beyond the historic district to Savannah's most atmospheric cemetery and out to the coast, where a Civil War-era fort and a lighthouse-topped barrier island round out your time in the city. It's a day that trades cobblestones for salt air and marsh views.

1

Made famous by "Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil," this sprawling cemetery on a bluff above the Wilmington River is filled with elaborate Victorian-era statuary draped in Spanish moss. Winding paths pass the graves of poets, songwriters, and Savannah's old families beneath one of the most photographed canopies of live oaks in the South.

2

Surrounded by a moat and built with more than 25 million bricks, this mid-19th-century fort was considered impregnable until Union forces breached its walls in 1862 using new rifled cannon technology — a battle that changed military engineering forever. Visitors can walk the ramparts, explore the fort's vaulted casemates, and still see cannonball damage scarring the exterior walls.

3

A short drive from Fort Pulaski, this beach town at the mouth of the Savannah River has served as the city's coastal retreat since the 1880s. Climbing the 178 steps of the Tybee Island Light Station, one of America's oldest and tallest lighthouses still in operation, rewards visitors with sweeping views over the Atlantic and the surrounding marshland — a relaxed way to close out your time in Savannah before heading north to Charleston tomorrow.

Day 3

Today covers the roughly two-hour drive north up the coast to Charleston, arriving in the early afternoon to explore its historic downtown peninsula. Charleston's compact core packs centuries of colonial and antebellum architecture into a walkable grid, from candy-colored row houses to some of the oldest churches in the American South.

1

This stretch of thirteen colorful colonial-era row houses along East Bay Street ranks among the most photographed streets in the South. Originally built as merchant shops with living quarters above, the buildings were restored and painted in pastel hues in the early 20th century, and today form one of the longest cohesive rows of Georgian architecture in the United States.

2

At the peninsula's southern tip, a seawall promenade known as the Battery offers sweeping harbor views once used to defend the city from naval attack. The adjacent White Point Garden is shaded by live oaks and lined with Civil War-era cannons and monuments, and on clear days offers a distant glimpse of Fort Sumter out in the harbor.

3

The steeple of St. Philip's Church, Charleston's oldest congregation, has served as a navigational landmark for ships entering the harbor since the 18th century, and its churchyard holds the graves of statesman John C. Calhoun and other notable early Charlestonians. A block away, the French Huguenot Church remains the only independent Huguenot congregation still active in the country, its Gothic Revival sanctuary a quiet contrast to the bustle of Church Street outside.

4

Built in 1771, the Old Exchange served as a customs house and later a Revolutionary War prison, with a dark, vaulted dungeon beneath it still open for tours. A short walk away, the Old Slave Mart Museum occupies the last known building in South Carolina that once housed a slave auction gallery, presenting the history of Charleston's domestic slave trade with unflinching clarity.

5

Operating since the early 1800s, the covered Charleston City Market stretches four blocks and remains famous for its handwoven sweetgrass baskets, a craft tradition passed down through generations of Gullah artisans. Close the day at Waterfront Park along the Cooper River, where the iconic Pineapple Fountain and a long pier make for a relaxed evening stroll.

Day 4

Your final day crosses the harbor to Mount Pleasant and Sullivan's Island, where a Revolutionary War fort, a decommissioned aircraft carrier, and one of the South's most iconic plantation avenues round out the trip before you head to the airport for departure.

1

Crossing the Cooper River on a soaring cable-stayed span completed in 2005, the Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge offers one of the most dramatic approaches to Mount Pleasant, with sweeping views back over the Charleston peninsula. On the far side, Patriots Point houses the retired aircraft carrier USS Yorktown, open for self-guided tours across its flight deck and below-decks quarters, along with a submarine and destroyer docked alongside.

2

This quieter waterfront park offers a long fishing pier extending out into the harbor with views back toward the Ravenel Bridge and downtown Charleston, making it a pleasant, less-crowded alternative to the peninsula's own waterfront for a mid-morning stop.

3

Founded in 1681, Boone Hall is best known for its three-quarter-mile Avenue of Oaks, planted in 1743 and still standing as one of the most photographed entrances in the South. The grounds also preserve a row of original brick slave cabins, now used to present exhibits on the lives of the enslaved people who worked the plantation's cotton and pecan fields.

4

This barrier island's quiet beaches and small-town main street offer a relaxed final stop before departure. Fort Moultrie, part of Fort Sumter and Fort Moultrie National Historical Park, traces 171 years of American seacoast defense on this single site, from a palmetto-log fort that repelled a British fleet in 1776 to World War II-era gun emplacements — a fitting bookend to a trip that began with Savannah's own Revolutionary and Civil War history. Ferries to Fort Sumter National Monument itself, the island fortress where the Civil War's first shots were fired, depart from Patriots Point and downtown Charleston for those with extra time before their flight home.
