Ocean in View: Retracing the Lewis and Clark Expedition

7 Days

In May 1804, the Corps of Discovery pushed off from the banks of the Missouri River near St. Louis with orders from President Jefferson to find a water route to the Pacific and document everything they encountered along the way. What followed was a nearly two-and-a-half-year, roughly 8,000-mile round trip through territory no U.S. citizen had ever mapped — a winter among the Mandan, a near-starvation crossing of the Bitterroot Mountains, and finally, in November 1805, the first sight of the Pacific Ocean. This 7-day road trip follows the outbound leg of that journey across ten states, from the Gateway Arch to the Oregon coast, grouped by the river basins and mountain ranges the expedition itself moved through. Given the sheer scale of the route — more than 2,500 driving miles even along modern highways — expect long days behind the wheel connecting a remarkable string of sites where the Corps of Discovery's own journals can still be matched almost word for word to the landscape in front of you.

Day 1 — Missouri & Kansas: Setting Out from St. Louis

The expedition's true starting point sits just outside St. Louis, where the Corps of Discovery spent a final week provisioning before committing to the unknown. Today follows the Missouri River upstream through the first towns and landmarks the explorers noted in their earliest journal entries.

1

Completed in 1965, the 630-foot stainless steel Gateway Arch was built specifically to commemorate St. Louis's role as the launching point for westward expansion, and the Corps of Discovery's expedition sits at the very center of that story. The Museum at the Gateway Arch, built into the grounds below, traces the expedition's full route through interactive exhibits, a natural first stop before following the Missouri River out of the city.

2

After departing Camp Dubois across the river in Illinois on May 14, 1804, the expedition's very first stop was St. Charles, where they spent nearly a week taking on final supplies and recruiting additional men while Meriwether Lewis, delayed in St. Louis on business, caught up with the group. The town's preserved historic Main Street — cobblestone, brick storefronts, and river views largely unchanged — remains one of the best-preserved frontier river towns on the entire route.

3

Passing this bluff above the Missouri River in June 1804, William Clark noted in his journal that it would make an excellent site for a fort — a suggestion he acted on himself four years later, building Fort Osage here in 1808 as one of the first U.S. military and trading posts on the western frontier. The reconstructed fort standing today lets visitors see the exact defensive position Clark identified from the water.

4

Where the Kansas (Kaw) River meets the Missouri, the expedition made camp on June 26, 1804, and their journals record the site as another well-chosen defensive position — high, dry ground with a clear view up and down both rivers. Today's Kaw Point Park sits at the exact confluence, marking the future site of Kansas City and closing out a first day spent almost entirely within the first two weeks of the entire journey's journal entries.

Day 2 — Nebraska, Iowa & South Dakota: Up the Missouri to Council Bluff

The river corridor through Nebraska, Iowa, and South Dakota carried the expedition through its first real contact with Plains tribes, its only fatality, and its first brush with regional Native legend. It's a long day of driving along the river's modern route, but one packed with some of the journey's most consequential early events.

1

Serving as the National Park Service's administrative headquarters for the entire 3,700-mile Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail, this Omaha visitor center is the natural place to get a full overview of the route before diving into the individual sites along it — exhibits here cover the trail's complete scope, from St. Louis to the Pacific, in a way no single regional site can.

2

This Omaha riverfront park marks the expedition's passage along the stretch of the Missouri that would one day become the city, with public art and interpretive markers connecting the modern skyline to the largely unsettled landscape the Corps of Discovery actually passed through in July 1804.

3

Near this site in August 1804, the expedition held its first formal council with Native Americans, meeting with leaders of the Otoe and Missouria tribes at a location they named "Council Bluff." The site's significance was reinforced in 1820 when the Army built Fort Atkinson here, the first U.S. military post west of the Missouri River — a reconstructed fort and living history programs now bring both chapters of the site's history to life.

4

Located in Nebraska City, this center focuses on the broader Missouri River basin the expedition depended on for transportation, food, and navigation for the entire outbound journey — exhibits on the river's ecology and the expedition's reliance on it add useful context before continuing north along its banks.

5

Overlooking the river near Sioux City, this bluff is the burial site of Sergeant Charles Floyd, who died on August 20, 1804, almost certainly from a ruptured appendix — a condition no medicine of the era could have treated. He was the only member of the entire expedition to die during the journey, and the 100-foot sandstone obelisk marking his grave was designated the first National Historic Landmark in the United States in 1960.

6

Local tribes had long warned travelers away from this solitary hill, believed to be inhabited by small, dangerous spirits — a legend Lewis and Clark found compelling enough to detour and investigate personally in August 1804. They found no spirits, but did find one of the richest wildlife-viewing stops of the early trip, and the restored native prairie today looks remarkably close to what the explorers themselves described.

7

Set along Lewis and Clark Lake, a Missouri River reservoir near Yankton, this modern recreation area commemorates the expedition's passage through the region with trails and overlooks along the same general river corridor the Corps of Discovery navigated by keelboat and pirogue. It's a fitting, more relaxed close to a long day of river-corridor driving before tomorrow's push into North Dakota.

Day 3 — North Dakota: Wintering with the Mandan

North Dakota holds some of the most pivotal events of the entire expedition — the site where the Corps of Discovery spent their first brutal winter, and the earthlodge village where they met the young Shoshone woman who would prove essential to the journey's success.

1

Near present-day Washburn, the Corps of Discovery built this triangular log fort in the fall of 1804 and spent five brutal months here, enduring temperatures that reportedly dropped below negative 40 degrees Fahrenheit. It was during this winter that the expedition hired French-Canadian trader Toussaint Charbonneau and his young Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, as interpreters — a decision that would prove essential once the expedition needed to negotiate for horses in the Rocky Mountains. A faithful reconstruction now stands near the original site.

2

This site preserves the remains of Hidatsa and Mandan earthlodge villages that stood at the height of a thriving trade network long before Lewis and Clark ever arrived, and it was in one of these villages that Sacagawea was living — having been captured years earlier from her Shoshone homeland — when the expedition met her during the Fort Mandan winter. Reconstructed earthlodges and depression outlines of the original village give a vivid sense of a settled, sophisticated Native community the expedition depended on for information and survival.

3

Reached in April 1805 after breaking camp at Fort Mandan, the meeting point of the Missouri and Yellowstone Rivers struck the expedition as one of the most strategically significant spots on the entire journey — so significant that decades later the Army built Fort Buford here, the same post where Sitting Bull would eventually surrender in 1881. The interpretive center near Williston ties together the site's layered history, from the expedition's passage to its later role in the Indian Wars, closing out a day centered on North Dakota's outsized role in the expedition's story.

Day 4 — Montana: The Upper Missouri & the Great Falls

Montana holds more sites connected to the expedition than any other state, and today covers its first major stretch — the dramatic, still largely unchanged White Cliffs section of the Missouri River, and the series of waterfalls that forced the Corps of Discovery into one of the most physically punishing detours of the entire trip.

1

This dramatic stretch of the Missouri, protected as a national monument since 2001, has changed so little since 1805 that Lewis's journal description of the eroded white sandstone cliffs — comparing them to ruined castles and towers in a scene of "visionary enchantment" — still matches the view almost exactly. It remains most fully experienced by float trip, though highway overlooks offer a substantial taste of the same scenery the expedition marveled at.

2

Fort Benton, later famous as a booming 19th-century steamboat port sometimes called the "Birthplace of Montana," sits along the same stretch of river the expedition passed through in the spring of 1805. This memorial commemorates that passage, offering a quieter historical counterpoint to the town's later fur-trade and steamboat-era heyday.

3

The city takes its name directly from the series of five major waterfalls on the Missouri River that the expedition encountered here in June 1805 — a discovery that was equal parts awe-inspiring and devastating, since it meant an 18-mile overland portage around the falls that took nearly a month of grueling labor, complete with makeshift wagons built from cottonwood trees to haul boats and supplies around the obstruction.

4

Overlooking the Missouri River near the falls themselves, this museum is dedicated entirely to the expedition's story, with detailed exhibits on the brutal portage, the expedition's scientific documentation of previously unrecorded plants and animals, and the daily reality of life for the roughly thirty-three members of the Corps of Discovery. It's one of the most comprehensive single-site museums anywhere along the trail, and a fitting place to reflect on the journey's hardest physical stretch before continuing west tomorrow.

Day 5 — Montana: Three Forks, the Divide & a Detour to Pompeys Pillar

Continuing upstream, today reaches the true source of the Missouri River and the moment Sacagawea's presence became irreplaceable — she recognized the landscape ahead as her childhood homeland, reassuring the expedition that Shoshone territory, and the horses they desperately needed, lay close by. The day also includes a worthwhile detour east to a site technically visited on the expedition's return journey rather than the outbound leg.

1

Approaching this dramatic limestone canyon near Helena in July 1805, Lewis wrote that the towering cliffs appeared to close behind the expedition's boats like massive gates — a description so apt it gave the canyon its name. The site remains accessible primarily by boat tour today, much as it was for the Corps of Discovery, offering one of the most visually dramatic and least-changed landscapes on the entire trail.

2

This distinctive volcanic rock outcropping served as a navigational landmark noted in the expedition's journals as they pushed further upstream — a small but useful waypoint amid the increasingly mountainous terrain signaling their approach to the Rockies proper.

3

Near Three Forks, the expedition identified the true source of the Missouri River — the confluence of three smaller rivers, which they named the Jefferson, Madison, and Gallatin after the president, the future president, and the secretary of the treasury who had backed the expedition's funding. Reaching this spot meant the "Missouri River" portion of the journey was officially complete, with only the harder mountain crossing still ahead.

4

When Sacagawea recognized this distinctive rock formation as landmark near her childhood home, it was one of the most important moments of reassurance in the entire expedition — confirmation that Shoshone territory, and the horses the Corps of Discovery would need to survive the mountain crossing ahead, lay just beyond. Her role here went well past translation, providing genuine geographic knowledge no one else in the party possessed.

5

Montana's first state park preserves an extensive limestone cave system that, despite the name, the expedition itself never actually discovered or visited — it was named in their honor decades later. It remains a worthwhile stop regardless, with guided tours through dramatic stalactite- and stalagmite-filled chambers offering a break from the trail's outdoor, river-focused sites.

6

Near present-day Lolo, this campsite gave the expedition genuine rest both outbound in September 1805, just before their brutal Bitterroot Mountain crossing, and again on the return trip in 1806, when it served as the staging point where Lewis and Clark famously split the Corps of Discovery into separate groups to explore different routes home. Archaeological evidence, including traces of a latrine matching period medical treatments, has confirmed this as one of the most precisely located campsites on the entire trail.

7

Worth noting honestly: this sandstone butte along the Yellowstone River, near Billings, requires a significant detour east from the rest of today's route and technically belongs to the expedition's return journey rather than its outbound leg — Clark passed this way in July 1806 while exploring a separate route home along the Yellowstone. It's included here because of its unique significance: Clark carved his name and the date directly into the rock, and it remains the only physical, on-site evidence of the expedition's passage still visible in its original location anywhere along the entire trail. Clark named the formation after Sacagawea's infant son, whom the expedition had affectionately nicknamed "Pomp."

Day 6 — Idaho: Over the Bitterroots

Idaho holds the single hardest stretch of the entire outbound journey — a brutal, near-starvation crossing of the Bitterroot Mountains along a route far more difficult than anything the expedition had anticipated. Today follows that crossing from the Continental Divide to the relief of finally reaching Nez Perce territory on the other side.

1

Crossing the Continental Divide here in August 1805, Lewis expected to find the headwaters of a river flowing smoothly to the Pacific — instead, he later wrote of seeing nothing but range after range of snow-capped mountains stretching to the horizon, a genuinely crushing moment that revealed just how much harder the remaining journey would be than anyone in the expedition had imagined. The pass remains remote and largely undeveloped today, offering essentially the same humbling view.

2

The expedition's route over the Bitterroot Range via Lolo Pass turned out to be one of the most physically punishing stretches of the entire trip — freezing temperatures, deep snow even in September, and dwindling food supplies forced the party to eventually kill and eat several of their own horses to survive. Modern travelers cross the same pass on a paved highway, a stark contrast to the near-disaster the Corps of Discovery experienced here.

3

Emerging from the mountains starving and exhausted in late September 1805, the expedition finally reached this open camas prairie and encountered the Nez Perce people, who fed and sheltered the desperate travelers rather than treating them as a threat — a decision of genuine historical consequence, since a hostile reception here could easily have ended the entire expedition. The open, wildflower-dotted prairie today looks much as it did to the relieved explorers who stumbled onto it.

4

Spread across several sites around Orofino and Spalding, this park preserves the deeper history of the Nez Perce people whose aid at Weippe Prairie proved essential to the expedition's survival — the relationship built here would remain significant for decades afterward, through subsequent trade, treaties, and eventually the tragic 1877 Nez Perce War. Exhibits here center Nez Perce perspective and history rather than treating the tribe only as a supporting chapter in someone else's journey, a valuable counterbalance after five days spent following the expedition's own journals.

Day 7 — Washington & Oregon: Ocean in View

Your final day follows the expedition's last push down the Columbia River to the Pacific — the moment William Clark wrote his famous, slightly premature journal entry "Ocean in view! O! the joy," followed by the reality of a difficult, rain-soaked winter still to come before the return journey home.

1

This massive volcanic monolith along the Columbia River Gorge marks the spot where the expedition first noticed tidal influence in the water in early November 1805 — a small but electrifying detail confirming the ocean was finally close. Clark named it Beacon Rock in his journal, and a switchback trail built in the early 1900s now allows visitors to climb to the summit for sweeping gorge views the expedition itself never had time to enjoy.

2

This park along the Washington side of the trail commemorates the expedition's final stretch toward the coast, with old-growth forest trails offering a quieter, greener counterpoint to the dramatic river and coastal scenery still ahead.

3

Despite its gloomy name — bestowed decades earlier by a British explorer who failed to locate the Columbia River's mouth from offshore — this headland is where the Corps of Discovery finally confirmed they'd reached the Pacific Ocean in November 1805, prompting Clark's famous journal exclamation "Ocean in view! O! the joy." Two historic lighthouses and sweeping views over the notoriously treacherous mouth of the Columbia make it one of the most dramatic stops on the entire trail.

4

Crossing into Oregon, this park preserves Fort Clatsop, a reconstructed log stockade where the expedition spent a miserable, near-constantly rainy winter of 1805–06 — by their own count, it rained all but twelve days of their entire stay. Despite the discomfort, this was also where the expedition did some of its most important scientific and diplomatic work, cataloging new species and building relationships with local Clatsop and Chinook peoples before the long journey home.

5

Founded in 1811, several years after the expedition's visit, as a fur trading post and the first permanent U.S. settlement on the Pacific coast, Astoria sits right beside the mouth of the Columbia the expedition had traveled so far to reach. Its Columbia River Maritime Museum and riverfront walk make it a natural base for exploring the final cluster of coastal sites.

6

A small detachment of the expedition set up camp here in Seaside through the winter, tending large iron kettles around the clock to boil seawater down into salt — an essential preservative with no other local source, and grueling, tedious work that nonetheless kept the expedition supplied with cured meat through the winter months. A replica of the salt-making cairn marks the exact site today.

7

In January 1806, word reached Fort Clatsop of a whale carcass washed ashore near present-day Cannon Beach, and Clark led a small party — including, at her own insistence, Sacagawea, who argued she'd come too far not to see the ocean and its wonders for herself — on a difficult coastal hike to see it. That trek marked roughly the westernmost point the expedition ever explored, and the dramatic, forested headlands and sweeping Pacific views at Ecola State Park today make for a genuinely fitting final stop to a week spent following the Corps of Discovery from the banks of the Missouri all the way to this literal edge of the continent.
