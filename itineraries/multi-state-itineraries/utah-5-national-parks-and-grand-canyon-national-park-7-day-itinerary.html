Utah's Mighty 5 & the Grand Canyon: A Colorado Plateau Odyssey

7 Days

This 7-day journey traces the Colorado Plateau through six of the American Southwest's most celebrated national parks, moving from Utah's five signature landscapes into Arizona's mile-deep Grand Canyon. The route begins among Zion's soaring red walls, climbs into Bryce Canyon's amphitheater of glowing hoodoos, threads the quiet folds of Capitol Reef, stands beneath the delicate arches of Moab's slickrock desert, and finally turns south to the rim of the canyon that inspired the entire national park system. Because six parks in a week demands hard choices, this itinerary is distilled to each park's most iconic, accessible highlights rather than attempting comprehensive coverage — and it keeps every Grand Canyon stop safely along the rim rather than descending into the canyon itself. Fly into Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas, pick up a rental car for the week, and prepare for long, scenic drives between some of the most dramatic landscapes on the continent.

Day 1

Your journey begins with the roughly three-hour drive northeast from Las Vegas into southwestern Utah, where Zion National Park's canyon walls rise 2,000 feet above the Virgin River. Zion is Utah's oldest and most visited national park, and today's route moves from the accessible canyon floor to one of its most demanding trails, closing with sunset from a quiet overlook above the valley.

1

The shuttle-only Zion Canyon Scenic Drive winds between towering sandstone walls streaked with desert varnish, offering the park's signature introduction to a landscape carved by the Virgin River over millions of years. At road's end, the paved Riverside Walk follows the river to the entrance of the Zion Narrows, where the canyon closes to less than 30 feet wide in places.

2

A moderate loop leads past a series of pools and seasonal waterfalls tucked beneath sandstone overhangs, connecting to the shaded Grotto Trail along the canyon floor. It's a manageable midday hike that rewards with hanging gardens of ferns and columbine clinging to damp canyon walls.

3

Angels Landing is Zion's most famous — and most feared — trail, climbing switchbacks known as Walter's Wiggles before a final half-mile along a knife-edge ridge secured by chains, ending on a summit with sheer 1,000-foot drops on either side. A permit is required for the final section. Travelers seeking a similar thrill with less exposure often choose the Hidden Canyon Trail instead, which offers its own chain-assisted scramble into a secluded hanging canyon.

4

Just past the Zion-Mount Carmel Tunnel, this short trail leads to a viewpoint over lower Zion Canyon without the crowds of the valley floor. Continuing east, Checkerboard Mesa's cross-hatched sandstone pattern — carved by both wind and water — makes for an easy roadside stop and one of Zion's most photographed geological curiosities.

5

Close out the day on this moderate 3.3-mile round-trip trail near the South Entrance, which climbs to a bench overlooking the Towers of the Virgin and the town of Springdale below. As the sun drops, the sandstone cliffs blaze orange and red — one of the most rewarding sunset spots in the park.

Day 2

A roughly 1.5 to 2-hour drive climbs out of Zion's red-rock desert into the ponderosa pine forest surrounding Bryce Canyon, home to the largest concentration of hoodoos on earth. Today combines a descent among the spires themselves with a string of overlooks along the park's rim, showcasing the amphitheater from every angle.

1

Often combined into a single figure-eight route, the Queens Garden Trail and Navajo Loop descend directly into the amphitheater, passing beneath towering hoodoos and through the switchbacks of Wall Street, where slot-canyon walls rise nearly 200 feet on either side. This is widely considered Bryce's best short hike, offering an up-close encounter with formations you can only appreciate from the rim otherwise.

2

These two adjacent overlooks bookend the main amphitheater and, despite their names, offer stunning views at any hour. Connected by a short stretch of the Rim Trail, they provide the classic first look at Bryce's densest cluster of hoodoos glowing in shades of orange, pink, and white.

3

Inspiration Point's three tiered overlooks reveal the amphitheater from increasingly dramatic angles along the Rim Trail. Fairyland Point, tucked north of the main entrance, sees a fraction of the crowds and offers a similarly striking view over its own field of hoodoos, worth the short detour for quieter photography.

4

At the far end of the park's scenic drive, Rainbow Point and Yovimpa Point sit at over 9,100 feet, the highest overlooks in Bryce Canyon, with views stretching across three states on a clear day. The nearby Bristlecone Loop Trail winds through a forest of ancient bristlecone pines, some over 1,700 years old, making this a fitting final stop before continuing east tomorrow.

Day 3

The roughly two-hour drive east on Scenic Byway 12 is itself one of Utah's great road trips, crossing the slickrock spine of Boulder Mountain before descending into Capitol Reef National Park, the state's most underrated park. Today explores the historic Fruita oasis and the twisting Waterpocket Fold, a 100-mile wrinkle in the earth's crust that gives the park its name.

1

Settled by Mormon pioneers in the 1880s, the Fruita Historic District preserves orchards, a one-room schoolhouse, and homestead buildings tucked improbably into a canyon of red cliffs. The Capitol Reef Scenic Drive threads through this same corridor, offering an easy introduction to the park's blend of human history and geological drama.

2

This moderate two-mile round-trip hike climbs to a 133-foot natural bridge spanning a dry wash, one of the most accessible and rewarding short trails in the park. The route also offers views over the Fremont River valley and the Fruita orchards below.

3

Before paved roads existed, Capitol Gorge served as the only wagon route through this stretch of the Waterpocket Fold, and the easy walk to the Pioneer Register still reveals names and dates carved into the canyon walls by early settlers and travelers passing through more than a century ago.

4

A short drive and easy walk lead to Panorama Point, with wide views over the folded reef, while the adjacent Goosenecks Overlook peers straight down into a twisting, deeply incised meander of Sulphur Creek far below. Both make an ideal, low-effort pair of stops to close out the day before continuing east toward Moab tomorrow.

Day 4

The roughly two-and-a-half to three-hour drive from Capitol Reef to Moab follows Utah's high desert plateau before dropping into the Colorado River valley, with the La Sal Mountains rising in the distance. Arches National Park packs more than 2,000 documented sandstone arches into a single compact landscape, and today moves through its most iconic clusters before ending beneath the state's most famous formation at sunset.

1

The trail through Park Avenue winds between towering rock fins resembling a canyon of skyscrapers, setting the tone for the park's scale right at the entrance. Further along the main road, Balanced Rock — a boulder the size of three school buses perched atop a narrow eroding pedestal — makes for one of the park's easiest and most photographed roadside stops.

2

This cluster of massive arches sits within a short, easy walk of one another, including the twin openings of North and South Window and the soaring twin spans of Double Arch, one of the tallest and longest arches in the park. It's an efficient way to see several of Arches' most dramatic formations in under an hour.

3

The trailhead at the end of the park road leads into Devils Garden, home to the greatest concentration of arches in the park. A relatively flat 1.6-mile round-trip walk leads to Landscape Arch, a ribbon of sandstone stretching an astonishing 306 feet — one of the longest natural rock spans in the world, and thin enough in places to seem almost impossibly fragile.

4

No image represents Utah more than Delicate Arch, the freestanding 65-foot span featured on the state's license plates. The three-mile round-trip hike across open slickrock is moderately strenuous but delivers travelers to a natural amphitheater where the arch frames the La Sal Mountains beyond. Sunset, when the sandstone turns deep red-orange, is the classic time to arrive — just bring a headlamp for the walk back.

Day 5

A sunrise start at Canyonlands National Park, just a short drive from Moab, is followed by the trip's longest travel day: a roughly five-and-a-half to six-hour drive south across the Navajo Nation to the Grand Canyon's South Rim. It's a long haul, but one that carries you from the mesa tops of southern Utah to the edge of one of the seven natural wonders of the world.

1

A short, easy half-mile trail leads to this compact arch perched directly on the edge of a cliff, framing a thousand-foot drop into Buck Canyon beyond. At sunrise, light reflecting off the canyon walls below sets the underside of the arch aglow in deep orange — a moment photographers travel across the country to capture.

2

The Island in the Sky mesa sits more than 1,000 feet above the surrounding terrain, offering some of the most expansive views in the entire national park system. At Grand View Point, the Colorado and Green Rivers have carved a labyrinth of canyons, buttes, and mesas stretching to the horizon — a fitting last look at Utah's canyon country before the drive south.

3

This unusual crater-like formation has puzzled geologists for decades, with theories ranging from an ancient salt dome collapse to a meteor impact. A short walk to the overlook reveals its strange, colorful rings of exposed rock before you head back toward the highway.

4

The long drive south passes through the red-rock country around Kayenta and Cameron before entering the Grand Canyon's East Entrance. Time your arrival for late afternoon at Desert View Watchtower, a 70-foot stone tower designed by architect Mary Colter and modeled after ancestral Puebloan structures, offering a sweeping first look at the canyon and the Colorado River far below before settling in near Grand Canyon Village for the night.

Day 6

A full day dedicated to the Grand Canyon's South Rim, moving from the historic heart of Grand Canyon Village to Hermit Road, one of the most celebrated scenic drives in the National Park System. Every stop today stays along the canyon's rim, offering full appreciation of its scale without any descent into it.

1

Just steps from the main visitor center, Mather Point delivers most first-time visitors' introduction to the Grand Canyon — a dramatic, wide-open first look at the canyon's scale. A short walk along the Rim Trail leads to Yavapai Point, home to a small geology museum with floor-to-ceiling windows framing the canyon directly behind exhibits explaining nearly two billion years of rock layers.

2

The paved, mostly flat Rim Trail runs for roughly 13 miles along the canyon's edge, and the stretch near the village incorporates the Trail of Time, an outdoor geologic timeline where each meter walked represents a million years of the canyon's formation, marked by rock samples pulled from the corresponding layer below.

3

Built in 1905 as a grand log-and-stone railway hotel, the El Tovar remains one of the National Park System's most historic lodges and a fitting spot for lunch with a canyon view. Nearby, the Bright Angel Trailhead and adjacent Lookout Studio offer a close look at the trail's first switchbacks winding down into the upper canyon, without needing to hike below the rim.

4

Along Hermit Road west of the village, Hopi Point juts further into the canyon than most overlooks, offering one of the widest fields of view on the South Rim and frequently cited as the single best sunset spot in the park. Nearby Mohave Point adds glimpses of the Colorado River itself and several distinctive rock formations, including the Vishnu Temple.

5

The end of Hermit Road and the day's finale, Hermits Rest is another Mary Colter design — a rustic stone building built in 1914 to resemble a hermit's hand-built shelter, complete with an oversized stone fireplace inside. The overlook here offers a fittingly dramatic backdrop for watching the sun set over the western canyon.

Day 7

Your final morning begins with sunrise at one of the South Rim's quieter, most celebrated viewpoints before the roughly four-and-a-half to five-hour drive back to Las Vegas. It's a fitting bookend to a week that spanned six national parks — one last look into the canyon's depths before returning to the desert and catching your flight home.

1

Accessible only by shuttle or on foot, Yaki Point sees far fewer crowds than the overlooks near the village while offering one of the best sunrise views on the entire South Rim, with the South Kaibab Trailhead visible nearby tracing its first steps down along a narrow ridge. Arriving before dawn rewards early risers with a canyon that seems to glow from within as the light first hits its walls.

2

Before leaving the park, a final stop at the main visitor center near Mather Point offers exhibits on the canyon's geology and history and one last chance to take in the view before the long drive west.

3

The drive back to Las Vegas retraces the route through Williams and across the Arizona-Nevada desert, covering roughly four and a half to five hours. Return your rental car at Harry Reid International Airport in time for your flight home, closing the loop on a week that carried you across six national parks and some of the most dramatic geology on the continent.
