Fall Colors in New England: A Seven-Day Foliage Journey

7 Days

For a few weeks each October, New England's hillsides ignite — sugar maples turning scarlet, birches gold, and oaks a deep burnt orange, all rolling across mountains that seem built specifically for the purpose of being looked at from a scenic overlook. This route threads together the region's most celebrated leaf-peeping ground, moving from the rolling farmland of Connecticut's Litchfield Hills up through the Berkshires, across Vermont's classic village-and-mountain country, into New Hampshire's dramatic White Mountains, and finally out to Maine's quiet lake country before ending at the Atlantic edge of Acadia National Park. Timing matters more on this trip than any other: peak color generally moves south to north and low to high elevation through the first three weeks of October, so this itinerary follows that same wave north, chasing the color at its best rather than arriving early or late in any one place.


Day 1 — Litchfield Hills, Connecticut

The trip begins in Connecticut's quiet northwest corner, a landscape of stone walls, covered bridges, and white-steepled village greens that peaks in color slightly earlier than the mountains further north — often the last two weeks of October. It's a gentle, rolling introduction before the terrain turns dramatic.


1


The unofficial heart of the Litchfield Hills, Kent pairs a walkable New England village center with the Housatonic River running just beyond it, its banks lined with sugar maples that turn a particularly vivid red each fall. Art galleries and small antique shops make it a natural place to slow down and start the trip.



2


A short walk up a wooden staircase follows Falls Brook as it tumbles roughly 250 feet over a series of cascades — one of the most photographed waterfalls in Connecticut, especially once the surrounding hillside turns gold and crimson in October.



3


A scenic drive around Lake Waramaug offers water-level views of hillside color reflected on the surface, while the nearby Bulls Bridge — one of Connecticut's two remaining covered bridges open to vehicle traffic — spans the Housatonic in a classic New England postcard scene, best photographed in late-afternoon light.





Day 2 — The Berkshires, Massachusetts

North and east of the Litchfield Hills, western Massachusetts rises into the Berkshires, a range of rounded, forested peaks long favored by writers and composers for their quiet beauty. Today follows the region's most scenic drive to its highest summit, then settles into its two classic villages.


1


One of America's first scenic auto routes, the Mohawk Trail winds 63 miles through the Berkshires along a road first used as a Native American trading path. Its famous Hairpin Turn, a sharp switchback with a sweeping overlook, is one of the single best roadside foliage views in Massachusetts.



2


At 3,489 feet, Mount Greylock is the highest point in Massachusetts, its summit road climbing through changing bands of color as elevation increases. The war memorial tower at the top offers views into five states on a clear autumn day, making it the trip's first true mountaintop panorama.



3


These two south Berkshire towns anchor the region's cultural reputation — Stockbridge for its Main Street, immortalized by Norman Rockwell in his painting of the same view, and Lenox for its grand Gilded Age "cottages" and the outdoor Tanglewood concert grounds nearby. Both make an easy, low-key end to the day.





Day 3 — Woodstock & Quechee Gorge, Vermont

Crossing into Vermont, the trip reaches the state most synonymous with fall foliage tourism. Woodstock is frequently cited as the most picturesque village in New England, and it sits just a few miles from one of Vermont's most dramatic natural features — a deep gorge cut straight through the hillside.


1


Woodstock's classic New England village green, white church steeple, and covered Middle Bridge over the Ottauquechee River have made it one of the most photographed small towns in Vermont. Elm-lined streets and brick storefronts frame the hillside color beyond, and the pace here rewards simply walking the village on foot.



2


Known as "Vermont's Little Grand Canyon," Quechee Gorge drops 165 feet where the Ottauquechee River has carved through solid rock over 13,000 years. A roadside bridge offers an easy overlook, while a short trail descends to the river's edge for a closer view of the canyon walls framed by turning foliage on both rims.





Day 4 — Stowe & Smugglers' Notch, Vermont

Further north in Vermont, Stowe sits at the base of the state's tallest mountain, framed by white-steepled charm on one side and dramatic granite cliffs on the other. Today combines the resort village's classic postcard views with one of the most thrilling mountain drives in New England.


1


Stowe's white church steeple set against Mount Mansfield's ridgeline is one of the most iconic images in Vermont, especially in October when the surrounding hills blaze with color. Just outside town, the Trapp Family Lodge — founded by the real Von Trapp family of Sound of Music fame — offers rolling meadow views and mountain-framed hiking trails.



2


Vermont's highest peak, its ridgeline famously said to resemble a human face in profile, offers panoramic foliage views either via the auto toll road that climbs to near the summit or the gondola that rises from Stowe Mountain Resort — both delivering a wide look over the surrounding valley's changing colors.



3


This narrow mountain pass squeezes between sheer 1,000-foot cliffs, its hairpin turns threading past massive boulders once used to smuggle goods during the War of 1812 trade embargo. The road closes to vehicles in winter, making a fall drive through the notch — cliffs on either side draped in color — one of the trip's most dramatic short drives.





Day 5 — White Mountains, New Hampshire

Crossing into New Hampshire, the terrain turns rugged and alpine. The White Mountains hold the tallest peak in the Northeast and some of the most celebrated scenic drives in the country, and today's route strings together the region's essential stops from valley floor to mountaintop.


1


Widely considered New England's premier fall foliage drive, the 34.5-mile "Kanc" climbs over Kancamagus Pass through unbroken national forest, with pull-offs at Sabbaday Falls and the Hancock and Kancamagus overlooks framing valley after valley of turning maple and birch. No New England foliage trip is complete without it.



2


The gateway town to the eastern White Mountains, North Conway and Conway combine mountain views with a walkable village center, making a natural stop for lunch or a base for the day between the Kancamagus and Franconia Notch.



3


This mountain pass squeezes between the Franconia and Kinsman Ranges, holding Flume Gorge's narrow granite chasm and Echo Lake's mirror-still reflections of the surrounding peaks. It's the most concentrated stretch of dramatic scenery in the White Mountains, all accessible from a single parkway.



4


The tallest peak in the Northeast at 6,288 feet, Mount Washington is famous for recording some of the most extreme weather on Earth, but on a clear fall day its Auto Road or historic Cog Railway deliver a view over an entire ocean of turning color across four states — a fitting high point, literally, to close the day.





Day 6 — Rangeley Lakes Region, Maine

Crossing into Maine, the trip trades mountain drama for quiet lake country. The Rangeley Lakes region is far less crowded than the White Mountains or Vermont's resort towns, but locals and longtime leaf-peepers often rank its views among the best in New England precisely because so few visitors make the trip this far inland.


1


Regularly named one of the most beautiful roadside views in America, this pullout on Route 17 looks out over Mooselookmeguntic Lake and the surrounding Rangeley Lakes basin from nearly 2,300 feet, an unbroken carpet of forest running to the horizon that turns brilliant gold and red each October.



2


Set along the shore of Rangeley Lake itself, this quiet park offers easy walking trails and picnic areas directly on the water, with mountain backdrops that catch the day's changing light — a peaceful, uncrowded contrast to the more famous overlooks elsewhere on the trip.



3


This small lakeside village has served fishermen, hunters, and outdoor guides for well over a century, and its unhurried Main Street makes a good final stop before continuing east toward the coast — the last inland town before Acadia's ocean views take over the trip's final day.





Day 7 — Acadia National Park, Maine

The trip ends where the mountains finally meet the sea. Acadia is one of the only places in the country where granite peaks rise directly from the Atlantic, and in fall its hardwood forests turn against a backdrop of blue ocean and pink granite rather than the inland hillsides seen all week — a genuinely different kind of foliage experience to close on.


1


For part of the year, Cadillac Mountain's 1,530-foot summit is the first place in the United States to see the sunrise, and doing it here on the trip's final morning means watching first light hit both the turning forest below and the open Atlantic beyond it simultaneously — a reservation is required during the fall season, so plan ahead.



2


This 27-mile scenic drive strings together nearly all of Acadia's coastal highlights, alternating between hardwood forest ablaze with color and open granite headlands looking straight out to sea — a combination of mountain-style foliage and ocean views found almost nowhere else in New England.



3


The trip's final stop is this small harbor town, where grand 19th-century "cottages" line a shoreline path looking out over Frenchman Bay, its islands framed by the last color of the trip. It's a fitting, quiet close to a week that began in Connecticut farmland and ended on the rocky edge of the Atlantic.
