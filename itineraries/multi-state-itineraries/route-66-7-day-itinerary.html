The Mother Road: Chicago to Santa Monica on Route 66

7 Days

Commissioned in 1926 and immortalized in song, literature, and a thousand road trip photographs since, Route 66 was never the fastest way across the country — it was the most interesting one. This 7-day journey follows the Mother Road roughly 2,300 miles from its official start in Chicago to its end at the Santa Monica Pier, moving state by state in the same order the original highway signs did. Expect giant fiberglass statues, Art Deco gas stations, roadside curio shops, and diners that have been serving the same recipes since before interstates existed — reordered here and there from strict document order to match the highway's actual geography, but otherwise a comprehensive, roadside-Americana-heavy trip built for long, happy days behind the wheel.

Day 1 — Illinois: Chicago to the Prairie

Every Route 66 trip begins on the same downtown Chicago corner, and today follows the highway's original path south through small prairie towns that built an entire tourism economy around keeping drivers entertained between gas stops — giant statues, polka-dotted drive-ins, and a family maple syrup operation that's been running since before the road itself existed.

1

The official "Begin Historic Route 66" sign near Grant Park marks mile zero of the entire 2,448-mile highway, making it the essential first photo of the whole trip. A block away, Lou Mitchell's has been serving breakfast since 1923 and still hands out free donut holes and Milk Duds to customers waiting for a table — a tradition nearly as old as the road itself.

2

Standing 30 feet tall outside the former Launching Pad Drive-In since 1965, this fiberglass "Muffler Man" clutching a silver rocket is one of the most photographed roadside giants on the entire route — a leftover from an era when a giant statue was considered a perfectly reasonable way to get drivers to stop for a milkshake.

3

Serving burgers since 1956 under a roadside menagerie of statues — Elvis, Marilyn Monroe, and the Blues Brothers all make an appearance — this polka-dot-themed drive-in leans fully into roadside kitsch, and it's exactly that unapologetic enthusiasm that's kept it a Route 66 favorite for decades.

4

Operating continuously from 1933 until 1999, this small white gas station is one of the oldest surviving service stations on the entire route, now restored as a visitor center. Its simple, boxy design was the industry standard of its era, before the more elaborate architectural flourishes seen further along the route became common.

5

Restored to its 1932 appearance, this English cottage-style station is a beautifully preserved example of the era's more decorative gas station architecture, designed to make roadside commerce feel a little more welcoming to passing families than a bare metal shed.

6

Run by the same family since 1891, this maple syrup operation predates Route 66 itself by more than three decades, tapping trees in the surrounding grove each spring. The deliberately old-fashioned spelling — "sirup" rather than "syrup" — dates to a mid-20th-century labeling distinction from corn-syrup blends, one the family has proudly kept ever since.

7

Open continuously since 1928, the Dixie Truckers Home and its restaurant claim to be one of the longest continuously operating stops on the entire highway, having fed generations of travelers exactly the kind of straightforward, hearty diner food Route 66 became famous for.

8

Illinois's capital carries a strong claim to Route 66 heritage, and the Route 66 Association of Illinois maintains a small info center here celebrating it. Just down the road, Cozy Dog Drive-In has served its signature battered, deep-fried hot dog on a stick — one of several claimants to inventing the corn dog — since 1949, while Maid-Rite keeps the classic Midwestern loose-meat sandwich tradition alive nearby.

9

Opened in 1924, the Ariston Café is one of the oldest continuously operating restaurants anywhere on Route 66, run by the same family for generations and still serving classic American and Italian comfort food in a building that has itself moved twice to stay adjacent to the highway's shifting alignment — a fitting, historic close to Illinois before crossing into Missouri tomorrow.

Day 2 — Missouri: Across the Ozarks

Missouri's stretch of Route 66 trades Illinois's flat prairie for the rolling, wooded Ozark foothills, along with some of the route's most legendary attractions — a cave system with outlaw legends attached, a motel that's been welcoming guests since the Depression, and a folk-art ghost town built entirely from salvaged buildings.

1

This distinctive bridge across the Mississippi carried Route 66 traffic from 1936 to 1968, its unusual 22-degree bend partway across built to accommodate solid bedrock conditions on the riverbed. It's now a pedestrian and cycling bridge, offering a rare chance to walk the exact path drivers once took into Missouri. Afterward, Ted Drewes Frozen Custard — serving its famously thick "concretes" since 1930 — is a mandatory St. Louis stop.

2

This park sits on the former site of Times Beach, a small town evacuated and demolished in the 1980s after dioxin contamination made it uninhabitable. Its visitor center, housed in a restored 1935 roadhouse, tells both the highway's story and the town's cautionary environmental one.

3

Advertised for decades on painted barn roofs across the Midwest, this extensive cave system draws visitors with dramatic underground formations and a colorful, only loosely verified legend that outlaw Jesse James once used the caverns as a hideout. The adjacent wax museum leans fully into that legend, whether or not the history holds up.

4

Opened in 1936 and still welcoming guests today, this distinctive stone-walled motor court is one of the oldest continuously operating motels on the entire highway, its rustic construction and neon sign essentially unchanged since the Depression era.

5

This sharp bend in the Big Piney River made log rafting treacherous enough for 19th-century loggers to give it its ominous name, and the small historic village that grew up around the old bridge here retains a genuinely tucked-away, bypassed-by-time feel. Nearby, a memorial marks the region's much older and darker history as part of the forced 1830s removal route now known as the Trail of Tears.

6

Once billed as the world's largest before being surpassed elsewhere, this genuinely enormous rocking chair towers outside a roadside gift shop and remains one of the most reliably fun photo stops on the entire Missouri stretch — exactly the kind of good-natured, oversized novelty Route 66 has always specialized in.

7

Springfield, Missouri holds a genuine claim to fame: it's the city where the highway was officially christened "Route 66" at a 1926 meeting, rather than the numeral originally proposed. A downtown marker and mural commemorate the moment a name that would become genuinely iconic was first put to paper.

8

This lovingly recreated 1930s filling station was the life's work of the late Gary Turner, a beloved fixture of the Route 66 community who greeted travelers here for decades; his family carries on the tradition today. It's less a formal attraction than a genuine, personal labor of love, and a favorite stop among longtime Route 66 road trippers for exactly that reason.

9

Opened in 1949 and still screening films today, the 66 Drive-In is one of the last classic outdoor theaters left on the route. Nearby, folk artist Lowell Davis spent decades building Red Oak II, an entire recreated ghost town assembled from historic buildings relocated from his own hometown — an unusual, deeply personal art installation and a fitting, offbeat close to a day full of exactly this kind of roadside devotion.

Day 3 — Kansas & Oklahoma: The Shortest State to the Longest Stretch

Kansas contributes just 13 miles to the entire highway — barely enough time to finish a cup of coffee — before Oklahoma opens up into the longest single-state stretch of Route 66 anywhere on the map. Today covers both, ending in the middle of Oklahoma's largest city on the route.

1

Kansas's brief stretch of the Mother Road opens in Galena with Cars on the Route, home to a rusted 1950s tow truck reportedly one of the inspirations for "Tow Mater" in Pixar's Cars. The Galena Mining & Historical Museum, housed in a former depot, tells the story of the lead and zinc boom that built the town, while Route 66 Cruisers rounds out a compact cluster of quirky small-town stops.

2

This rare 1923 marsh arch "Rainbow Bridge" is one of the only surviving examples of its design left anywhere on Route 66. Just down the road, Nelson's Old Riverton Store has operated since 1925 and remains a beloved sandwich stop for cyclists and drivers tracing the highway's original path.

3

Housed in a historic bank building once robbed by the Jesse James gang, this small visitor center marks the end of Kansas's short but proud 13-mile stretch of the highway before the road crosses into Oklahoma just ahead.

4

Crossing into Oklahoma, this cultural center honors the heritage of the Quapaw Nation, whose tribal land the highway passes directly through — a reminder that Route 66's route runs across a landscape with a far older history than the highway itself.

5

Folk artist Ed Galloway spent eleven years, starting in 1937, building the world's largest concrete totem pole here — a 90-foot tower covered in colorful carved imagery. It remains one of the most ambitious pieces of roadside folk art anywhere on the highway.

6

Dedicated to Oklahoma's most beloved native son — humorist, actor, and folk philosopher Will Rogers — this museum houses his personal artifacts and final resting place, offering a deeper look at one of the era's most genuinely famous Americans.

7

Built in the early 1970s by Hugh Davis as an anniversary gift for his wife, this cheerfully grinning giant blue whale originally sat beside a swimming hole and remains one of the most beloved and photographed roadside icons on the entire Mother Road.

8

Standing 75 feet tall at the fairgrounds since 1966, the Golden Driller pays tribute to Tulsa's oil industry roots. Downtown, Cyrus Avery Centennial Plaza honors the man widely credited as "the Father of Route 66," featuring dramatic bronze sculptures at the historic bridge where the highway crosses the Arkansas River.

9

This center preserves the memory of Tulsa's Greenwood District, once known as "Black Wall Street" for its thriving Black-owned businesses, and the site of the devastating 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre. It's a sobering, essential stop that adds crucial historical weight to a route otherwise dominated by lighter roadside attractions.

10

A giant space-suited "Muffler Man" astronaut greets visitors outside Buck Atom's Cosmic Curios, a retro-futuristic souvenir shop in the historic Blue Dome District, named for a distinctive blue-domed former gas station and now Tulsa's liveliest entertainment strip. Nearby, the modern Mother Road Market food hall gives the day's lunch stop a distinctly Route 66 theme.

11

Built in 1939 from stones excavated during the original construction of Route 66 itself, this café survived a devastating 2008 fire and was rebuilt by its determined owners. It's widely believed to have inspired Sally's diner in Pixar's Cars — a fitting legacy for a building literally made from the road it sits beside.

12

Housed in a restored 1930s armory building, this museum walks through the highway's full Oklahoma history, from its Dust Bowl-era role as an escape route west to its later reinvention as a nostalgia-driven tourist destination.

13

This restored 1921 gas station now houses a vintage motorcycle collection, a niche but genuinely well-curated stop for anyone interested in the two-wheeled side of American road trip history.

14

Built in 1898, the Arcadia Round Barn is a genuinely rare true round barn that was nearly lost to collapse before a dedicated restoration effort saved it, and it remains one of Route 66's most architecturally distinctive landmarks. Just down the road, POPS 66 is impossible to miss — a 66-foot illuminated soda bottle sculpture towers over a shop stocking hundreds of soda flavors.

15

This tiny, triangular corner building is topped with an oversized concrete milk bottle, a classic piece of early 20th-century "programmatic architecture" designed to advertise exactly what was sold inside. It's a quick but charming stop marking the day's arrival in Oklahoma City, one of the largest cities the entire highway passes directly through.

Day 4 — Western Oklahoma & the Texas Panhandle

Today closes out Oklahoma's long stretch of the Mother Road before crossing into Texas, where the landscape flattens dramatically into genuine cattle country and the roadside attractions get, if anything, even bigger and stranger — Cadillacs planted nose-first in a field, a leaning water tower, and a diner marking the exact midpoint of the entire highway.

1

Restored to its 1929 appearance, this small gas station was run for 59 years by Lucille Hamons, affectionately known as the "Mother of the Mother Road," who kept her station open around the clock to help stranded travelers well into her later years. Her legacy is one of the most beloved personal stories anywhere along the highway.

2

This comprehensive museum walks visitors through Route 66's history decade by decade, from its Dust Bowl-era role carrying desperate migrant families west to its postwar heyday as America's favorite road trip and its eventual decline after the interstate system took over.

3

This large complex pairs a dedicated Route 66 museum with a broader transportation museum, giving visitors a wider view of how the highway fit into the bigger story of 20th-century American mobility before the final push toward the Texas border.

4

Crossing into Texas, the restored Magnolia Gas Station's white-and-green Art Deco tilework offers an early taste of the panhandle's love of eye-catching roadside design. Just across the street, the U-Drop Inn Café is even more striking, its stepped Art Deco tower reportedly the inspiration behind Ramone's Body Shop in Pixar's Cars.

5

This carefully restored vintage Phillips 66 station preserves another chapter of the highway's early service-station architecture, while the adjoining Devil's Rope Museum takes on an unexpectedly specific subject — the history of barbed wire — that turns out to be a surprisingly essential piece of the story of how the American West was actually settled and fenced.

6

Originally built at a deliberate tilt as an eye-catching gimmick to draw drivers into a nearby truck stop, this water tower has outlasted the business it was built to promote and now stands as a stand-alone roadside oddity, genuinely leaning and genuinely worth the quick photo stop.

7

Ten Cadillacs half-buried nose-first in a dusty field since 1974 make up one of the most iconic pieces of public art in the country, endlessly repainted by visitors who are actively encouraged to bring their own spray cans. Afterward, the Big Texan Steak Ranch offers the ultimate Texas dining challenge: finish a 72-ounce steak dinner in under an hour, and it's free.

8

A cheerfully unsubtle homage to its more famous Amarillo neighbor, Bug Ranch buries a row of old Volkswagen Beetles nose-down in the ground instead of Cadillacs — a quick, funny stop that knows exactly what joke it's telling.

9

A sign outside marks this exact spot as the true midpoint of Route 66 — 1,139 miles back to Chicago, 1,139 miles ahead to Santa Monica — making it both a geographic milestone and, thanks to its well-regarded pie, a genuinely worthwhile stop in its own right.

10

Straddling the Texas-New Mexico state line, Glenrio was abandoned almost overnight when Interstate 40 bypassed it in the 1970s, leaving behind an eerie, perfectly preserved collection of empty motels, gas stations, and cafés slowly weathering in the panhandle wind. It's one of the most genuinely haunting ghost towns on the entire route, and a fitting, quiet close to the day before New Mexico begins tomorrow.

Day 5 — New Mexico: Neon, Diners & the Continental Divide

New Mexico offers some of the best-preserved neon signage anywhere on Route 66, along with a genuine desert swimming hole, a Pueblo Deco movie palace, and the second crossing of the Continental Divide on this trip (the first being Wyoming, on an entirely different itinerary). It's a full day of classic Southwestern roadside Americana.

1

Tee Pee Curios has sold souvenirs from its distinctive teepee-shaped storefront since 1944, while the Blue Swallow Motel, built in 1939, is one of the most beautifully preserved neon-lit motor courts left on the entire highway — a genuine favorite among photographers chasing the classic Route 66 look after dark.

2

Santa Rosa's classic car museum showcases beautifully restored vehicles from the highway's heyday, but the town's real showstopper is the Blue Hole — a startlingly clear, deep-blue natural artesian spring so unexpectedly deep and clear that it's a popular scuba diving spot despite sitting in the middle of the New Mexico desert.

3

Opened in 1927, the KiMo Theatre is a stunning example of "Pueblo Deco" style, blending Art Deco flourishes with Native American design motifs into one of the most visually striking movie palaces anywhere in the Southwest. Elsewhere along Central Avenue, a Madonna of the Trail statue — one of twelve identical monuments erected nationwide in the 1920s honoring pioneer women — predates Route 66 itself by several years.

4

Albuquerque's stretch of historic Central Avenue holds two separate, similarly named retro diners, both leaning hard into classic 1950s Americana with chrome trim, red vinyl booths, and comfort-food menus built for exactly this kind of road trip lunch stop.

5

At 7,275 feet, this trading post marks the exact spot where Route 66 crosses the Continental Divide, the ridge separating water flowing toward the Pacific from water flowing toward the Atlantic. It's a classic roadside souvenir stop with a genuinely significant geographic claim attached.

6

Built in 1937 specifically to house Hollywood film crews shooting Westerns in the surrounding red-rock landscape, this grand hotel's guest rooms are each named for the stars who once stayed in them — John Wayne, Katharine Hepburn, and Ronald Reagan among them. Its massive log-beamed lobby remains one of the most atmospheric places to spend a night anywhere on the route, a fitting close to New Mexico before Arizona begins tomorrow.

Day 6 — Arizona: The Best-Preserved Stretch of the Mother Road

Arizona is where Route 66 nostalgia reaches its peak — a National Park the highway runs directly through, the longest uninterrupted original stretch of pavement left anywhere on the route, and Kingman, a genuine capital of Route 66 preservation with multiple museums dedicated to keeping the highway's memory alive.

1

One of the few national parks Route 66 ever ran directly through, Petrified Forest preserves vast deposits of 200-million-year-old fossilized wood alongside the colorful, striped badlands of the Painted Desert. Original sections of the historic highway are still visible within the park boundaries, a rare chance to stand on genuinely untouched Route 66 pavement.

2

Built in 1950, this Wigwam Village motel rents out individual concrete teepee-shaped rooms, with vintage cars parked outside each one for maximum period effect — one of the most recognizable and photographed lodging concepts on the entire highway. Just down the road, Rainbow Rock Shop pairs mineral and gem displays with an unexpected lineup of large dinosaur statues.

3

Famous for its "Here It Is" billboard campaign that ran for hundreds of miles in both directions, this trading post's giant jackrabbit statue — perfect for a straddling photo — has been a Route 66 essential since long before social media made it even more popular.

4

A short detour south of Winslow leads to Meteor Crater, the best-preserved meteorite impact site on Earth, nearly a mile wide and 50,000 years old. Just north of town, Homolovi State Park preserves the remains of ancestral Hopi pueblo villages, adding a much older layer of history to the same stretch of desert.

5

Marked by two enormous arrows appearing to have shot down directly into the earth beside the road, this mid-century trading post is a partially restored but still striking piece of classic Route 66 roadside advertising design.

6

The Galaxy Diner leans into full retro, space-age diner styling for a classic Route 66 lunch stop, while the historic Hotel Monte Vista, built in 1927, has hosted numerous Hollywood stars filming Westerns in the surrounding red-rock country — and carries a long-standing reputation, whether or not you believe it, for being haunted.

7

This vintage gas station turned museum in Williams — the last town on Route 66 to be bypassed by the interstate, in 1984 — packs its small space with genuine period memorabilia from the highway's mid-century service-station era.

8

Run by the Delgadillo family since 1953, this whimsical drive-in is famous for the good-natured practical jokes its staff plays on customers placing orders — a tradition started by founder Juan Delgadillo, whose brother Angel is widely credited as the "Father of the Historic Route 66" for leading the movement to save the highway from complete obscurity in the 1980s.

9

Sitting on one of the longest uninterrupted original stretches of Route 66 pavement left anywhere, this old general store and gas station is draped floor to roofline in vintage signs, license plates, and antique cars — genuinely one of the most photogenic single stops on the entire highway.

10

Kingman functions as something close to a capital city for Route 66 preservation, home to three separate attractions dedicated to the highway's memory — the official Arizona Route 66 Museum, housed in the historic Powerhouse Visitor Center, alongside additional shops and exhibits celebrating the road's ongoing cultural legacy.

11

Near the Arizona-California border, this roadside stop pairs a typical souvenir shop with a genuinely unexpected feature: resident emus and llamas that visitors can view up close. It's a lighthearted, slightly random final stop before crossing into California and the desert stretch toward the Pacific tomorrow.

Day 7 — California: Across the Mojave to Journey's End

Your final day crosses the Mojave Desert's harshest, most isolated stretch of the entire highway before descending into the Los Angeles basin and finally reaching the Pacific — a fitting, dramatic close to 2,300 miles that began on a downtown Chicago street corner a week ago.

1

Crossing the Colorado River into California, the grand El Garces Depot was once one of the celebrated Fred Harvey chain's finest railroad hotels, its elegant restored facade a striking contrast to the harsh desert surrounding it. The nearby 66 Motel keeps the neon-lit motor-court aesthetic alive for travelers about to enter the toughest stretch of desert on the entire trip.

2

Roy's towering googie-style sign is visible for miles across the flat Mojave and remains one of the single most photographed images on the entire highway, even though the surrounding town is now largely a ghost stop. Just beyond it, Amboy Crater's extinct volcanic cone offers a short, worthwhile hike to its rim for anyone willing to brave the desert heat.

3

This unassuming desert diner became internationally famous after starring in the 1987 German cult film of the same name, and it now draws a steady stream of visitors — especially from Europe and Japan — making a genuine pilgrimage to a small stop that most American road trippers might otherwise pass right by.

4

Housed in a historic train depot, this museum focuses on the highway's grueling desert stretch through the Mojave — a section that claimed more than a few overheated radiators and exhausted travelers in the days before reliable air conditioning made the crossing far more forgiving.

5

The late Elmer Long spent years building this sprawling folk art installation, dozens of welded metal "trees" each hung with dozens of colorful vintage glass bottles, catching the desert light in a way that photographs beautifully at nearly any time of day.

6

This museum covers the highway's specifically Californian chapter, including its pivotal role during the Dust Bowl migration immortalized in John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath, when Route 66 carried hundreds of thousands of displaced families west in search of work.

7

The very first McDonald's restaurant opened here in 1940, though the golden arches design most people picture came later — the site is now a memorabilia-filled museum rather than a working restaurant. San Bernardino's own Wigwam Motel, a sister property to the one in Holbrook, Arizona, offers travelers one more chance to spend a night in a concrete teepee.

8

Designed by architect Robert Stacy-Judd and completed in 1925, this striking Mayan Revival building brings a genuinely unusual architectural style to the highway's final stretch — geometric, pre-Columbian-inspired ornamentation unlike anything else on the entire route.

9

This distinctive "googie" style gas station, all sweeping angles and mid-century optimism, marks the highway's unlikely final stretch through some of the most famous real estate in the country before the last few miles to the coast.

10

Route 66 officially ends here, marked by an "End of the Trail" sign near the entrance to the pier, with the Pacific Ocean stretching out beyond it exactly as generations of exhausted, exhilarated travelers first saw it after crossing the entire country. The pier's historic carousel and amusement park make for a genuinely celebratory finish, and the beach itself — sand finally underfoot after 2,300 miles of asphalt — is the only fitting way to end a week spent chasing the Mother Road from one coast to the other.
