We Shall Overcome: An American Civil Rights Journey

6 Days (+2 Optional Days to Washington, D.C.)

Between the mid-1950s and late 1960s, ordinary people across the American South — students, ministers, seamstresses, sharecroppers, and schoolchildren — faced fire hoses, bombings, and mob violence to demand the equal rights the Constitution had promised them a century earlier. This 6-day journey traces that history from Little Rock, Arkansas to Atlanta, Georgia, moving through the Mississippi Delta and the heart of Alabama's Black Belt along the way, grouped by city and driving distance to follow roughly the same geographic arc many of the movement's key events did. Given the seriousness of these sites, this trip moves at a more deliberate pace than a typical sightseeing itinerary — several museums and memorials here genuinely warrant two or three hours of unhurried, reflective time rather than a quick photo stop. Two optional additional days extend the journey to Washington, D.C., where the movement's story reaches the National Mall itself.

Day 1 — Little Rock, Arkansas & Memphis, Tennessee

The journey opens at the site of one of the movement's first major confrontations over school desegregation, then follows Highway 40 east into Memphis, where it will also end — at the exact spot where Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated, and the church where he delivered his final speech the night before.

1

In September 1957, nine Black students attempting to integrate this still-operating high school were blocked by the Arkansas National Guard on the governor's orders, then escorted inside three weeks later by soldiers of the U.S. Army's 101st Airborne Division after President Eisenhower federalized the Guard to enforce the Supreme Court's Brown v. Board of Education ruling. The visitor center across the street tells the story of the "Little Rock Nine" in vivid detail, an essential starting point for understanding just how directly the federal government had to intervene to make integration a reality.

2

On the night of April 3, 1968, Dr. King addressed a crowd here in the middle of a violent thunderstorm, delivering what became known as his "I've Been to the Mountaintop" speech — a speech that, in retrospect, reads as an eerily direct premonition of his own death the very next day. It remains one of the most powerful and haunting oratories of the entire movement, delivered in a building that still functions as an active church headquarters.

3

Built around the carefully preserved Lorraine Motel, this museum marks the exact site — including Room 306, left frozen in time — where Dr. King was assassinated on the balcony on April 4, 1968, less than 24 hours after his Mason Temple speech. Rather than stopping at that single tragic moment, the museum's extensive exhibits trace the full arc of the movement, from the transatlantic slave trade through Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and into the present day, making it one of the most comprehensive single-site museums on this entire trip.

Day 2 — Mississippi: The Delta to Jackson

Today crosses the Mississippi Delta, where the 1955 lynching of a 14-year-old boy became one of the movement's founding catalysts, before continuing south to Jackson, a city whose civil rights history spans a martyred NAACP leader, a historically Black college that served as an organizing sanctuary, and one of the country's most acclaimed civil rights museums.

1

In August 1955, 14-year-old Emmett Till was abducted, tortured, and murdered near Money, Mississippi, after being accused of offending a white woman in a local store — a killing so brutal that his mother, Mamie Till-Mobley, insisted on an open-casket funeral back home in Chicago so the world could see exactly what had been done to her son. Photographs of his mutilated body, published in Jet magazine, are widely credited with galvanizing a generation of activists; designated a national monument in 2023, the sites here include Graball Landing on the Tallahatchie River and the Sumner courthouse where his killers were acquitted by an all-white jury before later confessing in a magazine interview, protected from further prosecution by double jeopardy.

2

NAACP field secretary Medgar Evers was assassinated in the driveway of this modest home in June 1963, shot in the back by a white supremacist as he returned from a late-night meeting, his wife and children inside. Myrlie Evers, who witnessed the aftermath, went on to a decades-long career in civil rights leadership of her own, and the home — designated a national monument in 2020 — preserves both the tragedy and the resilience that followed it.

3

Opened side by side in 2017, these twin museums let visitors move between Mississippi's broader state history and its specific, often painful civil rights history in a single visit. The civil rights museum's central gallery, a luminous space that brightens as visitors move through the exhibits toward the movement's hard-won victories, is widely regarded as one of the finest civil rights museums in the country.

4

Housed in Mississippi's first public school built for African American children, opened in 1894, this museum celebrates Jackson's African American history and culture — including its most famous alumnus, novelist Richard Wright, whose own writing about growing up in the Jim Crow South remains some of the most searing literature the era produced.

5

Founded in 1869, this historically Black college served as a crucial organizing sanctuary throughout the movement — its campus was one of the few places in Jackson where activists could meet, strategize, and shelter with relative safety from the violence and surveillance they faced elsewhere in the city. Freedom Riders, voter registration workers, and student organizers all passed through Tougaloo's campus at one point or another, making it a fitting, quieter close to a day spent tracing both tragedy and resistance across the Delta.

Day 3 — Selma, Alabama: Bloody Sunday & the Voting Rights Trail

Few single days in the movement's history carry more weight than March 7, 1965, when peaceful voting rights marchers were beaten by state troopers on a bridge at the edge of Selma — a day of violence broadcast into American living rooms that helped push the Voting Rights Act through Congress just months later. Today traces that story from its organizing headquarters to the bridge itself and out along the historic march route.

1

This small city on the Alabama River became the epicenter of the voting rights movement in early 1965, when local activists and organizers from the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee mounted a sustained campaign against the discriminatory practices — literacy tests, poll taxes, outright intimidation — that kept the overwhelming majority of Selma's Black residents off the voter rolls despite making up most of the county's population.

2

Serving as the organizing headquarters and starting point for all three Selma-to-Montgomery march attempts, this church hosted the mass meetings, strategy sessions, and moments of both fear and resolve that preceded one of the movement's most consequential campaigns. It remains an active congregation today, its role in the voting rights movement marked but not diminished by the passage of time.

3

On March 7, 1965 — now remembered as Bloody Sunday — some 600 marchers led by John Lewis and Hosea Williams crossed this bridge intending to walk all the way to Montgomery, only to be met on the far side by state troopers who attacked them with billy clubs and tear gas as television cameras captured the violence for a national audience. The images shocked the country, and just eight days later President Johnson introduced the legislation that would become the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Walking the bridge today, still called by its original name honoring a Confederate general and Klan leader, remains one of the most powerful experiences on the entire trip.

4

After two failed attempts, a third march finally completed the full 54-mile route from Selma to the steps of the Alabama State Capitol between March 21 and 25, 1965, this time protected by federalized National Guard troops after a federal judge ruled the marchers' First Amendment rights had to be honored. Roughly halfway along the route, the Lowndes Interpretive Center sits in a county once so notoriously dangerous for civil rights workers that it was nicknamed "Bloody Lowndes," and includes a recreation of the campsite where marchers slept under guard each night. Driving this same stretch of highway today, with historical markers along the shoulder, is the natural bridge into tomorrow's stop in Montgomery.

Day 4 — Montgomery, Alabama: Cradle of the Movement

Montgomery carries an almost unmatched concentration of civil rights history within a few compact downtown blocks — the church where Dr. King began his ministry and his movement, the spot where Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat, and, more recently, two of the most powerful memorials to slavery and racial terror ever built in the United States. This is easily the most content-dense day of the entire trip; budget accordingly.

1

A young Dr. King served as pastor of this church, in direct view of the Alabama State Capitol, from 1954 to 1960, and it was from this pulpit and the surrounding congregation that much of the Montgomery Bus Boycott's organizing energy first took shape. The nearby parsonage where King and his young family lived was bombed in January 1956 in retaliation for his leadership — none of them were harmed, but the scorch marks and shrapnel damage, preserved in places, make the danger the family lived under starkly tangible.

2

On December 1, 1955, seamstress and longtime NAACP activist Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat to a white passenger at this exact stop, an act of quiet, deliberate defiance that sparked the 381-day Montgomery Bus Boycott and helped launch Dr. King into national leadership. The museum's exhibits, including a recreated bus interior, walk visitors through the incident and the remarkable, sustained organizing campaign that followed it.

3

This historic square carries a difficult double history — it once hosted one of the largest slave markets in the American South, and decades later became a central bus stop during the boycott, meaning the same ground once used to buy and sell human beings became a staging ground for the movement that would help end the system of segregation their descendants still lived under.

4

Housed in the former Greyhound bus station, this museum marks the site where a mob attacked Freedom Riders in May 1961 — interracial groups of activists who rode buses through the South specifically to test enforcement of federal rulings desegregating interstate travel. The violence they faced here, following an earlier bus burning outside Anniston, drew national attention to just how far local authorities were willing to look away from mob violence against nonviolent protesters.

5

Opened in 2018 by the Equal Justice Initiative, the Legacy Museum is built on the site of a former slave warehouse and traces a direct, unflinching line from slavery through lynching, Jim Crow, and into modern mass incarceration. Nearby, the National Memorial for Peace and Justice is the country's first memorial dedicated specifically to victims of racial terror lynching, its more than 800 hanging steel monuments — one for each U.S. county where a documented lynching occurred — creating a physically and emotionally overwhelming space that has become one of the most talked-about new memorials in the country.

6

Designed by Maya Lin, who also created the Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington, this memorial's black granite table lists the names of 41 people killed during the civil rights movement alongside a chronological timeline of the era, with water continuously flowing across its surface. It's a quiet, contemplative counterpoint to the more immersive experiences of the Legacy Museum and lynching memorial nearby.

7

The steps of this capitol carry a genuinely jarring double history: Jefferson Davis was inaugurated as president of the Confederacy here in 1861, and just over a century later, in March 1965, Dr. King addressed the crowd that had completed the Selma-to-Montgomery march with his "How Long, Not Long" speech, declaring that the arc of the moral universe, though long, bends toward justice. Few single sites in the country capture the distance the movement traveled quite as starkly.

8

Worth noting: this site sits about 40 miles east of Montgomery in Tuskegee, a detour from tomorrow's route north to Birmingham but a genuinely worthwhile one for those with extra time. It preserves Moton Field, where the Tuskegee Airmen — the first Black military aviators in U.S. history — trained during World War II, proving their skill in combat despite facing segregation within their own armed forces, a legacy of excellence under discrimination that helped lay groundwork for the postwar push toward full civil rights.

Day 5 — Birmingham & Tuscaloosa, Alabama

Birmingham earned the grim nickname "Bombingham" for the sheer number of racially motivated bombings that took place here, and today's stops center on the 1963 Birmingham Campaign — the confrontation that, more than perhaps any other single event, turned American and international public opinion decisively against segregation.

1

On the morning of September 15, 1963, members of the Ku Klux Klan planted dynamite that detonated during Sunday services, killing four young girls — Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley, Carole Robertson, and Carol Denise McNair — in an act of violence so shocking it helped shift American public opinion decisively against segregation. The church remains an active congregation today, and its continued presence, rebuilt and resilient, stands as its own quiet testament to survival.

2

During the 1963 Birmingham Campaign, this park became a flashpoint when Public Safety Commissioner Bull Connor ordered police to turn fire hoses and attack dogs on demonstrators, including schoolchildren who had joined what became known as the Children's Crusade. Sculptures throughout the park today directly depict these confrontations, walking visitors through a path designed to evoke exactly what unfolded here.

3

Sitting directly across from Kelly Ingram Park and 16th Street Baptist Church, this museum places the Birmingham Campaign in its fullest context — the city's deeply entrenched segregation, the strategic decisions behind Dr. King's famous "Letter from Birmingham Jail," and the international media coverage that made Birmingham's confrontation a turning point far beyond Alabama's borders.

4

This Black-owned motel served as movement headquarters throughout the Birmingham Campaign, where Dr. King and other leaders strategized in Room 30 — a room later damaged in a retaliatory bombing that, remarkably, King had left just hours before it occurred. Its preservation and ongoing restoration keep alive one of the movement's key behind-the-scenes command centers.

5

This large municipal auditorium hosted mass meetings during the Birmingham Campaign, where organizers rallied the community's support and prepared demonstrators — many of them teenagers and children — for the marches and sit-ins that would follow.

6

A detour southwest of Birmingham leads to Tuscaloosa, where in June 1963 Governor George Wallace physically stood in a doorway at the University of Alabama in a staged attempt to block two Black students from enrolling — the infamous "Stand in the Schoolhouse Door." Wallace stepped aside only after being confronted by federalized National Guard troops, a direct echo of the standoff in Little Rock six years earlier that opened this entire trip, bringing the week's journey full circle before heading toward Atlanta tomorrow.

Day 6 — Anniston to Atlanta, Georgia: Birthplace of Dr. King

Your final day passes through Anniston, site of one of the Freedom Rides' most violent attacks, before arriving in Atlanta — the city where Dr. King was born, raised, and ordained, and where his legacy is now preserved across an entire historic district on Auburn Avenue.

1

On May 14, 1961, a mob outside Anniston firebombed a Greyhound bus carrying Freedom Riders, forcing passengers to flee the burning vehicle only to be beaten by the waiting crowd; a separate group of riders was attacked the same day at the Anniston Trailways station. Designated a national monument in 2017, this site marks one of the ugliest single days of mob violence the Freedom Rides encountered anywhere in the South, a fitting, sobering stop on the way toward Atlanta.

2

This National Park Service site spans several stops along Atlanta's historic Sweet Auburn corridor: the preserved Queen Anne-style home where King was born on January 15, 1929, and spent his early childhood; Ebenezer Baptist Church, where his father and grandfather both served as pastors before King himself co-pastored there; and the King Center, founded by Coretta Scott King in 1968, which includes King's tomb and an eternal flame. Together they offer the fullest possible picture of the man before he became a national figure.

3

This modern museum deliberately connects the American civil rights movement to global human rights struggles past and present, and includes a genuinely visceral lunch-counter sit-in simulation that puts visitors in a seat and has them experience, however briefly and safely, a small fraction of the intimidation activists faced during sit-in protests.

4

The African-American Panoramic Experience Museum focuses specifically on the deeper history and culture of Atlanta's Black community, offering context for the Sweet Auburn district's rise as one of the wealthiest and most influential African American commercial and cultural centers in the country during the era of segregation.

5

Fire Station No. 6 was the last segregated fire station in Atlanta before integration, now preserved as a small museum on the Sweet Auburn district's history. Nearby, the historic Municipal Market — established as a marketplace African American vendors and shoppers could use when barred from downtown's main market — remains an active market today, a living link to the segregation-era economy Sweet Auburn was built around.

6

This broader history museum situates the civil rights movement within Atlanta's fuller story, from its founding and Civil War-era destruction through its 20th-century growth into "the city too busy to hate" — a self-branded slogan the city used to distinguish itself from the more violently resistant segregationist image of Birmingham and Selma.

7

Worth noting: the Tubman Museum is actually located in Macon, about 85 miles south of Atlanta, making it a half-day round-trip detour rather than a stop within the city itself. For those with the extra time, it's the largest museum in the Southeast dedicated to African American art, history, and culture, named for Harriet Tubman and covering a sweep of history well beyond the civil rights era alone — a worthwhile bookend for a trip that began with slavery's legacy in Montgomery and ends by tracing that same throughline into the present.

Optional Day 7 — The Drive to Washington, D.C.

For those with extra time, the movement's story has one more major chapter — the 1963 March on Washington, where Dr. King delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech before a quarter of a million people on the National Mall. Getting there from Atlanta means a genuinely long drive, roughly 600 miles up through the Carolinas and Virginia, so treat today as a dedicated travel day with an overnight stop wherever fits your pace.

1

This is a full-day, interstate-heavy drive with no dedicated civil rights stops of its own along the way, mostly following I-85 and I-95 north through the Carolinas and Virginia. Consider breaking up the drive with an overnight stop around Charlotte, Richmond, or another convenient point roughly two-thirds of the way, arriving in Washington fresh for a full day at the National Mall tomorrow rather than rushing in exhausted the same evening.

Optional Day 8 — Washington, D.C.: The National Mall

The trip's optional final day arrives at the movement's most nationally visible moment — the 1963 March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom — along with a museum and memorial that carry the story forward into the present and back into the deeper history of American slavery and abolition.

1

On August 28, 1963, roughly a quarter of a million people gathered along the Reflecting Pool stretching out from the Lincoln Memorial for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, where Dr. King delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech from the memorial's steps — deliberately choosing to speak in the shadow of the president who had signed the Emancipation Proclamation exactly a century earlier. A marker on the steps today commemorates the precise spot where he stood.

2

Dedicated in 2011 along the Tidal Basin, this memorial centers on the "Stone of Hope," a granite figure of King emerging from a boulder split in two — a direct visual reference to his own words about carving "a stone of hope" from "a mountain of despair." It's the first memorial on the National Mall dedicated to a Black man, and to someone who was never a U.S. president.

3

Opened in 2016 and wrapped in a bronze, three-tiered corona inspired by Yoruban art, this museum traces the full African American experience from the transatlantic slave trade through the civil rights era and into contemporary culture, its underground history galleries deliberately designed so visitors descend into the hardest chapters before rising back up into floors celebrating art, music, and achievement. Reserve a timed entry pass online well in advance, as same-day passes are often unavailable.

4

Across the Anacostia River, Cedar Hill was the final home of Frederick Douglass, the escaped slave turned abolitionist, orator, and statesman whose 19th-century writing and activism laid essential intellectual groundwork for the civil rights movement more than half a century before it began. Standing in his study, lined with the books of a man once legally forbidden to read, makes for a fitting final stop to a journey that began with school desegregation in Little Rock and now closes with the abolitionist whose work made that later fight for equality possible in the first place.
