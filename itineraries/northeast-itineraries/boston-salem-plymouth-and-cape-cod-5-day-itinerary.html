Boston, Salem, Plymouth & Cape Cod: A 5-Day Massachusetts Coastal Journey

5 Days

This week traces the earliest chapters of American history along one compact stretch of Massachusetts coastline. It begins in Boston, where the Revolution was planned and fought street by street, swings north to Salem's 17th-century witch-trial history and old China-trade wharves, then turns south to Plymouth, where the Pilgrims first built a colony in 1620, before ending out on the sandy curl of Cape Cod. Four destinations, three centuries of history, and one continuous shoreline — this itinerary keeps the pace unhurried, with a full day or two devoted to each stop rather than rushing between them.


Day 1 — Boston: The Freedom Trail

Boston's compact downtown holds more concentrated Revolutionary War history than almost anywhere else in the country, most of it strung along the 2.5-mile red-brick line of the Freedom Trail. Today follows that line from the Common to the harbor, tracing the events that led to American independence.


1


America's oldest public park has served as common grazing land, a British military camp, and a gathering place for public protest since 1634. At its edge, the gold-domed Massachusetts State House has overlooked the Common since 1798, its dome originally sheathed in copper by Paul Revere's own company.



2


Three signers of the Declaration of Independence — Samuel Adams, John Hancock, and Robert Treat Paine — rest in this small colonial cemetery, alongside Paul Revere and the victims of the Boston Massacre. It sits beside the 1809 Park Street Church, whose 217-foot steeple was once the first landmark sailors spotted approaching Boston Harbor.



3


Known as the "Cradle of Liberty" for the fiery town meetings held on its second floor before the Revolution, Faneuil Hall still functions as a public meeting space today. Its adjoining Quincy Market has been a working marketplace since 1826 and remains one of the liveliest lunch stops in the city.



4


In Boston's North End, the small wooden Paul Revere House, built around 1680, is the oldest surviving structure in downtown Boston and was Revere's home at the time of his midnight ride. A short walk away, Old North Church is where two lanterns were hung in April 1775 to warn "the British are coming" by sea rather than land.



5


Across the harbor in Charlestown, the USS Constitution — nicknamed "Old Ironsides" — is the oldest commissioned warship still afloat in the world, its hull said to have deflected cannonballs during the War of 1812. It's docked at the historic Boston Navy Yard, where an adjoining museum tells the ship's 225-year story.



6


A 221-foot granite obelisk marks the site of the Revolution's first major battle, where colonial militia inflicted heavy losses on British troops despite ultimately losing the hill. Climbing the monument's 294 steps rewards visitors with a wide view over Charlestown and the harbor below.





Day 2 — Boston: Back Bay, Beacon Hill & Cambridge

Beyond the colonial core, Boston's 19th-century neighborhoods and world-class museums reveal a very different city — gaslit brick rowhouses, grand Gilded Age architecture, and one of the great university towns in America just across the Charles River. Today moves from Beacon Hill through Back Bay before crossing into Cambridge.


1


Boston's most photographed neighborhood is a maze of narrow, gaslit streets lined with 19th-century brick rowhouses and purple-paned windows. At its heart, Louisburg Square is a private, gated park ringed by some of the most expensive real estate in the city — a genuine glimpse of old Boston wealth.



2


America's first public botanical garden sits just across from the Common, famous for its swan boats gliding across a small lagoon and the bronze Make Way for Ducklings statues honoring the beloved children's book. It's one of the most manicured green spaces in the city.



3


The heart of Back Bay, Copley Square is framed by Trinity Church — a Romanesque masterpiece considered one of the most important buildings in American architectural history — reflected dramatically in the glass facade of the neighboring skyscraper across the square.



4


One of the largest art museums in the country, the MFA holds an encyclopedic collection spanning ancient Egypt to contemporary art. Next door, the Gardner Museum recreates a Venetian palazzo around a plant-filled courtyard, its walls still hung with empty frames marking the spot of paintings stolen in an unsolved 1990 heist.



5


Home to the Red Sox since 1912, Fenway is the oldest ballpark still in use in Major League Baseball, famous for the 37-foot left field wall known as the Green Monster. Guided tours run year-round even on non-game days, covering the park's century of history.



6


A short ride across the Charles River, Harvard Yard is the historic core of America's oldest university, its brick dormitories and the statue of "Three Lies" — the John Harvard Statue — drawing visitors year-round. The surrounding Harvard Square blends bookshops, cafes, and street performers into one of the most walkable college-town centers in the country.





Day 3 — Salem: Witch Trials & Maritime History

Thirty minutes north of Boston, Salem carries a double identity: the site of the infamous 1692 witch trials, and one of colonial America's great seafaring ports, its merchant ships once trading as far as China. Today's short list covers both sides of the city's history.


1


Housed in a former church, this is Salem's most visited attraction, using life-size stage sets and narration to walk visitors through the hysteria of 1692, when twenty people were executed on charges of witchcraft. It's the essential first stop for understanding the events that made the city famous.



2


The only structure still standing in Salem with direct ties to the witch trials, this 1675 house belonged to Judge Jonathan Corwin, who conducted many of the preliminary witchcraft examinations inside its walls. Its steep gabled roof and dark wood beams offer a rare, unaltered look at 17th-century Puritan architecture.



3


America's first National Historic Site preserves Salem's working waterfront as it looked during the city's peak as a global trading port, including a reconstructed 1797 tall ship and the wharves where fortunes in spice, tea, and silk once changed hands. It's a quieter, less crowded counterpoint to the day's witch-trial history.





Day 4 — Plymouth: Where the Pilgrims Landed

An hour south of Boston, Plymouth is where 102 Mayflower passengers established the first permanent English settlement in New England in 1620. The town has preserved that founding story more thoroughly than almost anywhere else in the country, and today moves through its waterfront landmarks, museums, and burial grounds in roughly the order the history itself unfolded.


1


A modest granite boulder sheltered beneath a classical portico, Plymouth Rock marks the traditional landing spot of the Pilgrims in 1620 — its symbolic weight far exceeding its physical size. On the hill directly above it, Coles Hill holds the unmarked graves of Pilgrims who died during that first brutal winter, along with a bronze statue of Massasoit, the Wampanoag leader whose alliance made the colony's survival possible.



2


Docked just steps from Plymouth Rock, the Mayflower II is a full-scale, seaworthy reproduction of the original ship, built in England in the 1950s and sailed across the Atlantic to retrace the voyage. Walking its cramped lower deck gives a vivid sense of just how small and difficult the 66-day crossing really was for 102 passengers and crew.



3


The oldest continuously operating public museum in the United States, Pilgrim Hall houses genuine 17th-century artifacts owned by the Mayflower passengers themselves — furniture, weapons, and personal belongings that survived the voyage, giving Plymouth's story a tangible weight beyond the Rock and the ship alone.



4


A short drive from downtown, this living history museum recreates both the 1627 English colonial village and a Wampanoag homesite, staffed by costumed interpreters portraying real Mayflower passengers and Native historians speaking in their own voices. In town, the working Plimoth Grist Mill, a reconstruction of the colony's original 1636 mill, still grinds corn using its water-powered stones.



5


The Jabez Howland House is the only surviving structure in Plymouth that Mayflower passengers are known to have lived in, built by the son of John and Elizabeth Howland. Nearby, Burial Hill — the town's original 1620 fort site — holds the graves of several Mayflower passengers and offers a sweeping view over the harbor below.



6


At 81 feet, this is the largest solid granite monument in the United States, dedicated in 1889 to honor the Pilgrims' civil and religious principles. Its allegorical figures representing Faith, Morality, Law, Education, and Liberty make a fitting, monumental close to a day spent tracing the colony's founding story.





Day 5 — Cape Cod & Provincetown

The trip's final day continues south and east onto Cape Cod, the sandy, curled arm of land that has drawn New Englanders to its beaches every summer for generations, ending at land's end in Provincetown. It's a day built for slowing down — open beaches, small harbor towns, and views across Cape Cod Bay.


1


Protecting 40 miles of the Cape's outer beaches, this National Park Service unit preserves the wide sand dunes, salt marshes, and lighthouses that have defined Cape Cod's outer arm since long before it became a summer destination. It's the best stretch of coastline to stop and simply walk the beach on the drive out toward Provincetown.



2


These two Provincetown beaches sit at the very outermost curl of the Cape, with wide open sand and views that stretch uninterrupted to the horizon — Race Point faces due north, making it one of the rare East Coast spots where the sun both rises and sets over open water.



3


Provincetown was actually the Pilgrims' first landing point before they crossed the bay to Plymouth, a history commemorated by this 252-foot granite tower — the tallest all-granite structure in the United States. The observation deck at its top offers a sweeping view back across the entire curl of Cape Cod.



4


Provincetown's mile-long main street is lined with galleries, shops, and restaurants reflecting the town's long-running artist colony and its status as one of the oldest LGBTQ+ vacation destinations in the country. MacMillan Pier, still home to a working fishing fleet and whale-watching boats, makes a fitting final stop before the drive back — the trip's last look at the working Massachusetts waterfront that began five days earlier in Boston Harbor.
