Seven Days Across Ohio: River Cities, Amish Farmland & the Great Lakes Shore

7 Days

Ohio rarely gets credit for its range, but a week spent crossing it reveals a state of genuine variety: a German-and-riverboat river city on the Ohio, the birthplace of powered flight, a capital built around a gilded statehouse, a sandstone gorge cut deep into the Appalachian foothills, the largest Amish settlement in the world, the home of professional football, a national park tucked between two industrial cities, a lake city turned museum powerhouse, and a Glass City on the shores of Lake Erie. This route moves in a clockwise loop — south to central to southeast to east-central to northeast to northwest — devoting each day to a single region or a pair of nearby stops, and keeping the list to each area's true must-sees rather than every possible attraction.


Day 1 — Cincinnati: The Queen City on the River

Built on seven hills above a bend in the Ohio River, Cincinnati is a city of 19th-century German beer barons, Underground Railroad history, and one of the oldest zoos in the country. Today stays close to the riverfront and downtown, hitting the handful of sights that consistently top every visitor's list.


1


A landscaped stretch of gardens, fountains, and swings along the Ohio River, Smale Riverfront Park sits directly beneath the John A. Roebling Suspension Bridge, the 1866 span that predates — and inspired — Roebling's later Brooklyn Bridge. Walking across it on foot offers one of the best views of the Cincinnati skyline reflected in the river.



2


Sitting on the riverbank where enslaved people once crossed into free territory, this museum tells the story of the Underground Railroad and the broader fight for freedom in America, anchored by a preserved 1830s slave pen relocated from a Kentucky farm.



3


Housed in a sweeping Art Deco former train station, the Museum Center holds a natural history museum, a children's museum, and a history museum under one half-dome roof, itself a National Historic Landmark. The rotunda mosaics alone are worth the stop.



4


Ohio's oldest continuously operated public market has anchored the Over-the-Rhine neighborhood since 1855, its indoor hall and outdoor stalls selling produce, meats, and prepared foods from generations of local vendors. It's the best lunch stop in the city.



5


The second-oldest zoo in the United States remains one of its best, known for successful breeding programs and lush botanical grounds that make it feel as much like a garden as an animal park — consistently the single highest-rated attraction in the city.





Day 2 — Dayton: The Birthplace of Aviation

An hour north of Cincinnati, Dayton holds an outsized place in American innovation — this is where Wilbur and Orville Wright designed and tested the world's first airplane. Today focuses on the trio of sites that make up Dayton's aviation legacy, the reason most travelers come here at all.


1


The largest military aviation museum in the world, this free museum spans four massive hangars housing more than 350 aircraft and missiles, from a Wright Flyer replica to the presidential aircraft that carried FDR. Plan for at least half a day — it's genuinely that expansive.



2


This National Park Service site preserves the Wright brothers' original bicycle shop and print shop, telling the story of the two Dayton mechanics whose careful engineering — more than any dramatic leap of genius — led to powered flight.



3


This outdoor history park gathers historic buildings beneath a 151-foot bell tower, including the original 1905 Wright Flyer III — the world's first practical airplane — displayed in the very building where Orville Wright helped restore it. It's the essential third stop that completes Dayton's aviation story.





Day 3 — Columbus: The Capital on the Scioto

An hour east of Dayton, Columbus is Ohio's largest city and its seat of government. The capital's downtown blends 19th-century civic architecture with art and greenery, and today's route hits the small handful of sights that define a first visit.


1


Built over more than two decades in the mid-1800s, the Greek Revival Ohio Statehouse is notable for its flat, unadorned dome rather than the ornate domes typical of other state capitols. Free guided tours cover its restored legislative chambers.



2


Columbus's oldest continuously running public market dates to 1876 and today gathers dozens of local vendors and restaurants under one roof, making it the natural lunch stop for a day spent downtown.



3


Designed by architect Allied Works and opened in 2018, this striking curved-concrete building on the riverfront honors veterans of every branch and era through personal stories rather than a chronological military history.



4


A short walk northeast of downtown, this museum holds a strong collection of American and European modern art, with particular depth in French Impressionist works, set within a walkable, sculpture-dotted campus.



5


This 1895 glass conservatory houses biomes ranging from a Pacific island rainforest to a desert, alongside outdoor gardens that have hosted permanent glass installations by artist Dale Chihuly — a lush, colorful way to close a day among civic architecture.





Day 4 — Hocking Hills State Park

An hour southeast of Columbus, the landscape changes entirely. Hocking Hills is a region of deep sandstone gorges and waterfalls carved into the edge of the Appalachian Plateau, and today is built around the park's three signature trails — each centered on a single, unmistakable rock formation.


1


The park's most visited and most photographed trail descends through a narrow sandstone gorge past a series of waterfalls, a natural rock bridge, and the deep recess cave that gives the trail its name. The roughly one-mile main loop offers the single best introduction to the region's geology.



2


Despite its name, Cedar Falls flows past a grove of hemlock trees once mistaken for cedars by early settlers. It's the highest-volume waterfall in the region and connects to Old Man's Cave via a scenic gorge trail.



3


The largest recess cave in Ohio, Ash Cave forms a horseshoe-shaped rock shelter roughly 700 feet across and 100 feet deep. A short, flat, fully accessible trail leads to its base, making it a fitting, easy final stop for the day.





Day 5 — Ohio's Amish Country & Canton

East of Hocking Hills, the hills of Holmes County hold the largest Amish settlement in the world, a landscape of horse-drawn buggies and farm stands centered on the town of Berlin. Heading north from there, the drive continues to Canton, home to the single most famous museum in American sports.


1


The unofficial capital of Ohio's Amish Country, Berlin's small downtown is lined with furniture makers, cheese shops, and family-run bakeries, with horse-drawn buggies still a common sight alongside cars. It's the best base for exploring the surrounding countryside.



2


Behalt houses a 265-foot cyclorama painting depicting the 450-year history of the Amish and Mennonite people, from their origins in the Swiss Anabaptist movement through their migration to America — the best historical grounding available in the area.



3


Yoder's offers guided tours of two working farmhouses, a barn, and a schoolhouse, giving a genuine look at daily Amish life and farming practices — the most visited single Amish attraction in the region.



4


About an hour north of Berlin, Canton is home to the Pro Football Hall of Fame, honoring the sport's greatest players and franchises beneath its iconic gold-domed rotunda. Canton was chosen as the site in 1963 because the National Football League itself was founded here in 1920. It's an easy, worthwhile detour on the drive north toward Cleveland.





Day 6 — Cuyahoga Valley National Park & Cleveland

North of Canton, the Cuyahoga River winds through a green corridor of waterfalls and forest sandwiched between Cleveland and Akron — one of the least-known national parks in the system, and one of the most convenient to reach. The day continues into downtown Cleveland for its signature museums and market.


1


Cuyahoga Valley's most famous sight, Brandywine Falls drops 65 feet over a horseshoe-shaped ledge of rock, viewable from a boardwalk that puts visitors nearly level with the falls themselves. It's the single most-visited spot in the park and a short, easy walk from the parking area.



2


A 2.5-mile loop trail winds along and beneath massive sandstone outcrops, some rising 90 feet overhead, with narrow passages squeezing between rock walls draped in moss and hemlock. The overlook at its high point offers one of the best views in the park.



3


I.M. Pei's glass pyramid on the Lake Erie shoreline honors the artists who shaped rock music, with galleries of stage costumes, handwritten lyrics, and instruments spanning the genre's full history — Cleveland's single most iconic attraction.



4


Free to enter and consistently ranked among the finest art museums in the country, the Cleveland Museum of Art holds an encyclopedic collection anchored by strong Asian, Egyptian, and European painting galleries, wrapped around a light-filled glass atrium.



5


Opened in 1912 beneath a distinctive clock tower, the West Side Market remains Cleveland's largest and oldest indoor-outdoor market, its vaulted tile ceiling sheltering more than 100 vendors — a fitting, flavorful close to a day spent between national park and city.





Day 7 — Sandusky & Toledo: Along the Lake Erie Shore

The trip closes with a run west along the Lake Erie shoreline, from Sandusky's famous amusement peninsula to Toledo's "Glass City" museum campus near the Michigan border. It's a lighter, more playful final day after a week heavy on museums and history.


1


Sitting on a narrow peninsula jutting into Lake Erie, Cedar Point has earned the nickname "America's Roller Coast" for its record-setting collection of coasters, several of which have held world records for height or speed since opening. Even non-riders can enjoy walking the peninsula's boardwalk with open water on both sides.



2


An hour west of Sandusky, the free Toledo Museum of Art is renowned for its glass collection — fitting for a city nicknamed the "Glass City" for its 19th-century glassmaking industry — including the striking, all-glass Glass Pavilion building itself.



3


Consistently rated one of the best zoos in the country, the Toledo Zoo pairs historic 1930s WPA-era buildings with modern habitats and a large aquarium, making for a relaxed final stop before heading home along the lake.
