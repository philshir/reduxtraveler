Kings Canyon National Park: Giant Sequoias, a Deep Glacial Canyon, and Sierra Waterfalls

2 Days

Next door to Sequoia National Park, Kings Canyon National Park protects one of the deepest canyons in North America, carved by the Kings River and ancient glaciers through the granite of the Sierra Nevada. The park has two very different main areas: Grant Grove, home to the General Grant Tree, the nation's official Christmas tree, and Cedar Grove, a glacier-carved valley of towering granite cliffs, meadows, and waterfalls at the end of a spectacular scenic byway. This two-day itinerary spends the first day among the giant sequoias of Grant Grove and the second day exploring Cedar Grove along the Kings River. Backpacking routes such as the Rae Lakes Loop and the remote upper South Fork of the Kings River are left out, as they require overnight trips into the wilderness.

Day 1

The first day centers on Grant Grove, the park's western entrance area, where the visitor center, lodging, a market, and restaurants are located. A vehicle pass costs $35, is valid for 7 days, and covers both Kings Canyon and Sequoia National Parks. Grant Grove is open year-round, though snow is common in winter and the road to Panoramic Point closes in the snowy months. Pets are not allowed on trails.

1

Begin your morning at the Kings Canyon Visitor Center in Grant Grove Village, where exhibits introduce the park's giant sequoias, its deep canyons, and the history of the efforts to protect them. Rangers can share current conditions for the Kings Canyon Scenic Byway and Cedar Grove, along with the day's ranger programs. Grant Grove was first protected by Congress in 1890 as General Grant National Park, which later became part of Kings Canyon National Park.

2

A short drive from the village, walk the half-mile paved loop to the General Grant Tree, the second-largest tree in the world by volume, about 267 feet tall with a base circumference of 107 feet and an age of nearly 1,650 years. It was proclaimed the nation's Christmas tree in 1926 and named a National Shrine in 1956, the only living memorial to those who have died in war. Along the loop, walk through the hollow trunk of the Fallen Monarch, which has served as a shelter and even a stable, and see the Robert E. Lee Tree.

3

For a quieter experience, continue on the North Grove Loop Trail, an easy 1.3-mile loop that begins from the lower end of the General Grant Tree parking area. The trail leads through a peaceful mixed conifer forest of giant sequoias, sugar pines, and white firs, with far fewer people than the main grove. Interpretive markers explain the role of fire, climate, and time in shaping the sequoia forest, and the sunlight filtering through the canopy is especially striking in the morning and late afternoon.

4

After lunch in Grant Grove Village, drive to the Big Stump Trail near the Big Stump Entrance. This easy, mostly shaded loop of about 2 miles passes giant sequoia stumps left from logging between 1883 and 1891, including the Mark Twain Stump, cut in 1891 for display in museums, as well as the Smith-Comstock mill site. Young sequoias now grow among the old stumps, telling a powerful story of what was lost and why the groves were protected.

5

End the day at Panoramic Point, reached by a narrow road above Grant Grove Village and a short, paved, uphill walk from the parking area. From the overlook, a sweeping view stretches across the high peaks of the Sierra Nevada and down into the depths of Kings Canyon, where you will be heading tomorrow. As the sun sets, the granite peaks glow in warm light. The road is not recommended for trailers or RVs and is closed in winter.

Day 2

The second day follows the Kings Canyon Scenic Byway (Highway 180) about 30 miles from Grant Grove down to Cedar Grove and Roads End, a drive of about an hour each way without stops. The road beyond Hume Lake Road is generally open from late spring through fall and closes in winter. Cedar Grove sits at about 4,600 feet and is warmer than the sequoia groves, so start early for the Mist Falls hike, bring plenty of water, and store food properly, since black bears are active in the valley.

1

Set out early on the Kings Canyon Scenic Byway, one of the most scenic drives in the Sierra Nevada. Leaving Grant Grove, the road winds through Sequoia National Forest and Giant Sequoia National Monument, then descends steeply into the canyon of the South Fork of the Kings River, with dramatic views of the rugged granite walls. Pull over at Junction View for a sweeping look into one of the deepest canyons in North America before following the rushing river into Cedar Grove.

2

Drive to Roads End at the end of the byway to hike the Mist Falls Trail, one of the most rewarding day hikes in the park, about 8.4 miles round trip with about 900 feet of elevation gain. The trail follows the South Fork of the Kings River through forest and open stretches beneath towering granite walls before climbing to Mist Falls, which plunges over polished rock into a misty basin. The falls are most powerful in late spring and early summer, when snowmelt feeds the river. Allow 4 to 5 hours, and start early to avoid the afternoon heat.

3

After your hike, stop at Roaring River Falls, an easy, paved walk of about 0.3 mile round trip. The falls rush through a narrow granite chute into a deep, clear pool, making this a cool and refreshing stop on a warm afternoon. The short trail is suitable for all ages.

4

End the day with a walk on the Zumwalt Meadow Trail, an easy 1.5-mile loop that circles a lush meadow along the Kings River. Boardwalks cross the wetter sections, and the trail passes through talus slopes at the base of the cliffs, with stunning views of North Dome and Grand Sentinel rising about 3,500 feet above the valley floor. In the late afternoon, deer often graze in the meadow as the light softens on the granite walls, a peaceful finale before the drive back to Grant Grove.
