Yosemite, Sequoia & Kings Canyon: Granite Cliffs and Ancient Giants

5 Days

This 5-day journey through California's High Sierra moves from the sheer granite walls of Yosemite Valley to groves of sequoias so massive they rank among the largest living things on Earth, closing in the dramatic glacially carved depths of Kings Canyon. Along the way you'll stand beneath a 620-foot waterfall, look up at a rock face so vertical that climbers spend days scaling it, walk beside a tree wider than most city streets, and peer down into a canyon deeper than the Grand Canyon itself. Because Yosemite's high country along Tioga Road sits in the opposite direction from the route south to Sequoia and Kings Canyon — and is only open a few months each year — this itinerary focuses on Yosemite Valley and the Wawona/Glacier Point area before continuing south, leaving Tuolumne Meadows and the high country for a dedicated future trip. Expect long, scenic mountain drives between parks and plenty of time on foot among some of the most iconic granite and giant sequoia landscapes in the country.

Day 1

Your journey begins on the floor of Yosemite Valley, a seven-mile-long trough carved by glaciers into sheer granite walls that rise thousands of feet on either side. Today moves counterclockwise around the valley, taking in its most iconic overlooks, waterfalls, and rock formations at a relaxed, walkable pace.

1

Emerging from the Wawona Tunnel, this classic overlook frames Yosemite Valley in a single sweeping vista: El Capitan's sheer face on the left, Bridalveil Fall cascading on the right, and Half Dome anchoring the distance. It's the same view that greeted early visitors arriving by stagecoach, and remains the single most photographed vantage point in the park.

2

A short, paved path leads to the base of this 620-foot waterfall, one of the first features visitors see entering the valley. Its wispy, wind-blown spray gave the fall its name, and it runs strongest in spring, though it flows in some form nearly year-round.

3

Rising 3,000 feet in a single unbroken sweep of granite, El Capitan is one of the largest monoliths in the world and a mecca for rock climbers, some of whom spend days on the wall sleeping in portable ledges bolted to the rock. From the meadow below, a pair of binoculars often reveals tiny, colorful figures inching up its face.

4

Yosemite Village serves as the valley's hub, home to the visitor center, museum, and a small cluster of shops and dining. A short walk away, Yosemite Falls plunges 2,425 feet in three tiers, making it the tallest waterfall in North America — a paved loop trail leads to the base of the lower fall for an up-close view of its thundering spray.

5

On the valley's east end, a flat trail leads to Mirror Lake, a shallow pool that reflects the sheer face of Half Dome and the surrounding cliffs on calm mornings. The route back passes beneath Royal Arches, a series of curved granite bands formed by exfoliating rock, and connects to a stretch of the Valley Loop Trail, an easy, mostly flat path that circles the valley floor.

Day 2

Today climbs out of the valley in two directions: first along the Mist Trail to a pair of thundering waterfalls, then up Glacier Point Road for some of the best panoramic views in the park. The afternoon winds south to Wawona and the Mariposa Grove, home to some of Yosemite's largest giant sequoias, setting up tomorrow's continuation south toward Sequoia National Park.

1

One of the most rewarding hikes in the park, the Mist Trail climbs a granite staircase alongside Vernal Fall, often soaking hikers in cool spray on hot days, before continuing to the base of the taller, more distant Nevada Fall. Even turning back after Vernal Fall's footbridge offers a satisfying reward for a relatively short, steep climb.

2

Perched 3,200 feet above the valley floor, Glacier Point offers what many consider the single best view in Yosemite: Half Dome at eye level, Yosemite Falls across the way, and the High Sierra stretching to the horizon. Ambitious hikers can descend the Panorama Trail from here back into the valley, passing both Illilouette Fall and the top of Nevada Fall along the way.

3

Just off Glacier Point Road, a short but rewarding hike leads to the bald granite summit of Sentinel Dome, with 360-degree views that rival Glacier Point itself. A separate short trail leads to Taft Point, where sheer cliffs and narrow fissures called "the Fissures" drop straight down toward the valley floor, offering a dramatic — and slightly vertigo-inducing — perspective.

4

Home to over 500 mature giant sequoias, the Mariposa Grove includes the Grizzly Giant, estimated to be nearly 3,000 years old, reachable via an easy paved loop near the grove entrance. Nearby, the small community of Wawona preserves a slice of Yosemite's early tourism history, including a historic hotel and a covered bridge, and makes a convenient stop before continuing south toward the Sequoia and Kings Canyon parks tomorrow.

Day 3

Today covers a long but scenic drive south through the San Joaquin Valley foothills toward Sequoia National Park, arriving in the afternoon at Giant Forest, home to the greatest concentration of giant sequoias on the planet. Rather than a park of dramatic granite cliffs, Sequoia's centerpiece is scale of a different kind — living trees so massive they seem to belong to another age entirely.

1

By volume, the General Sherman Tree is the largest known living single-stem tree on Earth, standing over 274 feet tall with a trunk more than 36 feet in diameter at its base. A short paved trail from the parking area leads down to its base, where the sheer scale of the trunk — wider than most city streets — is genuinely difficult to comprehend until you're standing beneath it.

2

This paved two-mile loop starting near the General Sherman Tree winds through some of the densest sequoia groves in the park, passing named clusters like the House and Senate Groups, along with the Parker Group, a tight cluster of mature trees that illustrates how sequoias often grow in close family stands. It's one of the best ways to fully immerse yourself in the forest beyond the single famous tree.

3

Housed in a rustic 1928 building, the Giant Forest Museum offers exhibits on sequoia biology and the history of the surrounding grove. Just outside, the Big Trees Trail loops around Round Meadow on a flat, accessible boardwalk, offering an easy introduction to the forest for visitors of any ability level.

4

A steep but short staircase of roughly 400 steps, built directly into the granite in the 1930s, climbs to the summit of this 6,725-foot dome, rewarding hikers with a sweeping view over the Great Western Divide and the canyon of the Middle Fork Kaweah River. Late afternoon light makes this a fitting close to your first day among the giant sequoias.

Day 4

Today continues exploring Sequoia National Park before following the Generals Highway north toward Kings Canyon, one of the most scenic mountain drives in California. A cave tour and a couple of quieter historical and viewpoint stops round out the day's mix of underground marvels and sweeping overlooks.

1

Discovered in 1918, this marble cavern features polished, glimmering formations carved by an underground stream over roughly ten thousand years. Guided tours (which require advance tickets) lead visitors past stalactites, stalagmites, and delicate draperies of stone, offering a striking contrast to the towering trees above ground.

2

This roadside stop preserves pictographs and grinding rocks left by the Western Mono and Yokuts peoples who once lived along the Kaweah River, offering a glimpse into the human history of the Sierra foothills that predates the park by thousands of years.

3

A moderate three-and-a-half-mile round-trip hike along the Generals Highway climbs to this granite dome's bare summit, offering views over the Kaweah River watershed and the Great Western Divide beyond — a rewarding stop for those wanting one more sweeping panorama before heading north.

4

The Generals Highway winds north through dense mixed-conifer forest connecting Sequoia National Park to Kings Canyon, passing scenic overlooks and dramatic elevation changes along the way. The drive itself, named for the General Sherman and General Grant Trees it connects, is considered one of the most beautiful mountain roads in the Sierra.

Day 5

Your final day explores Kings Canyon National Park, whose namesake canyon plunges deeper than the Grand Canyon in places. Today begins among more giant sequoias at Grant Grove before following the Kings Canyon Scenic Byway down into the canyon itself, ending among granite walls and cascading rivers at Cedar Grove.

1

Home to the General Grant Tree — the second-largest tree in the world and officially designated the "Nation's Christmas Tree" — Grant Grove offers an easy paved loop past several other massive sequoias. The adjacent North Grove Loop Trail extends the walk through quieter, less-visited stands of the same ancient forest.

2

This easy loop near the park's entrance winds through a grove that was heavily logged in the late 1800s before the area was protected, with massive stumps — some wide enough to hold barn dances, according to old photographs — offering a sobering reminder of how much old-growth forest was lost before conservation efforts began.

3

Descending nearly a mile in elevation over roughly 30 miles, this designated National Scenic Byway is considered one of the most dramatic drives in California, switchbacking along canyon walls with the Kings River visible far below. A roadside overlook near Yucca Point offers a striking view down into the canyon's depths, among the steepest in North America.

4

At the bottom of the canyon, Cedar Grove offers a glacially carved valley reminiscent of Yosemite but with a fraction of the visitors. A short, flat loop trail through Zumwalt Meadow winds beneath towering granite walls, including North Dome and Grand Sentinel, with the Kings River meandering through wildflower-filled meadows alongside the path.

5

A short, paved five-minute walk leads to this powerful cascade, where the Roaring River squeezes through a narrow granite chute before crashing into a churning pool below. It's an easy, high-reward stop to close out your time in Kings Canyon before heading home.
