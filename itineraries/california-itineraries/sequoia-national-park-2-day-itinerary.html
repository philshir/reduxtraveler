Sequoia National Park 2 Day Itinerary

  1. Itineraries
  2. Sequoia National Park 2 Day Itinerary

Congress Trail, Sequoia National Park, CA

Sequoia National Park: Giant Sequoias, Granite Domes, and Marble Caverns

2 Days

In the southern Sierra Nevada of California, Sequoia National Park protects some of the largest and oldest living things on Earth. Established in 1890 as the nation's second national park, it is home to the Giant Forest, where five of the ten largest trees in the world grow, including the General Sherman Tree, the largest tree on Earth by volume. Beyond the sequoia groves, the park rises from warm foothills to granite domes and high peaks, with views stretching across the Great Western Divide. This two-day itinerary spends the first day exploring the Giant Forest and its most famous trees and viewpoints, then spends the second day hiking to a granite summit, touring Crystal Cave, and descending the Generals Highway to the foothills. Mineral King, reached by a long, winding road of its own, Mount Whitney on the park's far eastern edge, and longer backcountry hikes such as the Lakes Trail are left out, as they are better suited to a longer stay.

Day 1

The first day centers on the Giant Forest, a grove of more than 8,000 giant sequoias at over 6,000 feet. A vehicle pass costs $35, is valid for 7 days, and covers both Sequoia and Kings Canyon National Parks. Parking at the General Sherman Tree and Moro Rock fills quickly in summer, so start early and check whether the park's free shuttles are running during your visit. Pets are not allowed on trails, and Crescent Meadow Road and the Moro Rock area are closed to vehicles in winter.

1

Begin your morning at the Giant Forest Museum, housed in a historic 1928 market building designed by Gilbert Stanley Underwood. Exhibits explain the biology, history, and conservation of giant sequoias, including the role of fire in helping them grow. From the museum, walk the Big Trees Trail, a 0.7-mile paved, accessible loop around Round Meadow lined with towering sequoias and interpretive signs about the grove and the meadow ecosystem. In spring and early summer, wildflowers brighten the meadow, and mule deer are often seen grazing at its edges.

2

Next, drive or ride the shuttle a short distance to Moro Rock, a granite dome topped by a stairway of about 350 steps built in 1931. The steep, exposed climb of about 0.4 mile round trip leads to one of the most spectacular views in the Sierra Nevada, with the jagged peaks of the Great Western Divide to the east and the foothills and San Joaquin Valley to the west. Railings line the narrow path, but avoid the climb during thunderstorms or icy conditions.

3

Continue along Crescent Meadow Road, a short scenic road through the heart of the Giant Forest. Drive through Tunnel Log, a fallen sequoia with a passage cut through its trunk in 1938, or take the bypass if your vehicle is too tall. Farther along, stop at the Parker Group, one of the most photogenic stands in the park, where eight giant sequoias grow closely together like enormous pillars. The trees honor the family of Captain James Parker, a U.S. Army cavalry officer who served as acting superintendent of the park in the 1890s. The road ends at Crescent Meadow, a lush meadow ringed by sequoias and a good spot for a picnic lunch.

4

In the afternoon, visit the General Sherman Tree, the largest tree on Earth by volume, standing 275 feet tall and about 36 feet across at its base, and estimated to be about 2,200 years old. A paved half-mile trail with stairs descends from the main parking area off Wolverton Road to the tree, with an uphill walk on the return. From the Sherman Tree, continue on the Congress Trail, a 2.7-mile paved loop that winds past some of the grove's most famous giants, including the President Tree and the closely clustered Senate and House groups. The late-afternoon light filtering through the canopy makes this a peaceful end to the day.

Day 2

The second day starts north of the Giant Forest along the Generals Highway and ends by descending its winding switchbacks to the foothills near the Ash Mountain Entrance, a drop of several thousand feet. Crystal Cave is open only on guided tours from late spring to early fall, and tickets must be purchased online in advance, as they are not sold at the cave. Vehicles over 22 feet are not recommended on parts of the Generals Highway, and the foothills can be very hot in summer.

1

Start the morning with a hike to Little Baldy, a 3.4-mile round-trip trail along the Generals Highway north of Lodgepole that climbs steadily through mixed conifer forest on gentle switchbacks. The trail emerges onto a wide granite dome at over 8,000 feet, with sweeping views of the Great Western Divide, Silliman Crest, and the surrounding Sierra Nevada wilderness. It sees far fewer visitors than Moro Rock, and the morning light on the distant peaks is beautiful. There are no facilities at the trailhead, so bring water and snacks.

2

Join a guided tour of Crystal Cave, a stunning marble cavern hidden beneath the forest floor. After a half-mile walk down through a shaded forest to the cave entrance, the 50-minute tour, led by naturalists from the Sequoia Parks Conservancy, winds through chambers filled with glittering formations, stalactites, and polished marble. The cave stays about 50°F year-round, so bring a light jacket. Crystal Cave reopened in 2025 after several years of closure following wildfire and storm damage.

3

After your tour, drive south on the Generals Highway as it descends from the Giant Forest toward the foothills. Built in the 1920s and 1930s, this winding road drops through a series of tight switchbacks, revealing views of Moro Rock towering above the canyon of the Middle Fork of the Kaweah River. As you descend, watch the landscape change from sequoia and pine forest to oak woodland and chaparral, reflecting one of the greatest elevation ranges of any national park. Take your time and use the pullouts to enjoy the views.

4

End the day at Hospital Rock, a cultural site once home to the Potwisha people, where ancient pictographs are painted on a large boulder and more than 50 bedrock mortars were used for grinding acorns. Interpretive signs share the traditions of the Native Americans who lived here long before the park was established. A short trail leads to the Middle Fork of the Kaweah River, and the shaded picnic tables make it a relaxing place to rest before stopping at the Foothills Visitor Center and leaving the park toward Three Rivers.
